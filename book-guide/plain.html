Chapter I

Parts of a book

Learn the names first. Every description, grade and points guide uses them, and buyers expect them.

Boards

Look for: The stiff front and back covers.

Early boards were wood. From the 1500s, pasteboard (layers of paper) took over, then millboard and strawboard in the 1800s, and modern binder's board today. Wooden boards almost always mean a very early book.

Wooden boards are a strong early sign.

Spine

Look for: The back edge you see on the shelf.

Carries the title. On older books the spine may be divided into panels by raised bands. Spines take the most light and wear, so check them first for fading and splits.

Joints and hinges

Look for: Joints are the outside folds where boards meet spine. Hinges are the matching inside folds.

The most common place for a book to fail. Describe them precisely: starting, cracked, split, or detached.

Text block

Look for: All the pages together, without the cover.

Made of folded sheets called gatherings or signatures, sewn or glued together.

Endpapers

Look for: The sheets joining the text block to the covers. The one glued down is the pastedown; the loose one is the free endpaper.

Endpaper style is a strong dating tool: marbled in older books, plain or coated in Victorian cloth, printed maps or pictures in many 20th-century books.

Headband and tailband

Look for: The small band of colored thread or cloth at the top and bottom of the spine, inside.

Hand-sewn headbands are part of the structure on early books. From the 1800s most are decorative strips glued on. Many book club editions from the 1940s to 2000s have no headband at all while the trade edition has one, so a missing headband on a jacketed modern book is a club tell.

Edges

Look for: Top (head), bottom (tail) and outer (fore-edge) edges of the pages.

Can be gilt, colored, sprinkled, marbled, rough or uncut. Each tells you something about date and quality.

Title page and verso

Look for: The title page names the book, author, publisher, place and often the date. Its back (verso) is the copyright page in modern books.

Your first stop for dating and edition. Early books may have no title page and give details in a colophon at the end.

Chapter II

Paper

Paper is the hardest thing to fake and the easiest to read. Hold a page up to the light and feel it between your fingers.

Handmade laid paper

Europe 1200s to about 1800; still used by fine presses

Look for: Against the light: fine close lines (laid lines) crossed by stronger lines about an inch apart (chain lines). Slightly uneven thickness.

Made one sheet at a time by dipping a wire mold into a vat of rag pulp. The wires leave the pattern. Nearly every book printed before about 1800 is on laid paper.

Strong sign of an early book. Check the type and date to rule out modern fine press paper.

Wove paper

Invented about 1757; usual after 1800

Look for: Even texture against the light, with no lines.

Made on a mold of finely woven wire, first by hand, then by machine. Baskerville used it in the 1750s. By the early 1800s most book paper was wove.

Tells you the book is probably after 1800.

Rag paper

Until the mid-1800s for most books

Look for: Soft, strong, flexible, stays pale for centuries.

Made from linen and cotton rags. Rags ran short as printing grew, which led to the search for other fibers.

Survives well. A 1700s book can have brighter paper than a 1950s paperback.

Machine-made paper

First English machine 1803; common by the 1830s

Look for: Very even sheets, uniform thickness, wove. Long continuous rolls cut to size.

The Fourdrinier machine made paper in a continuous web. It made paper cheap and helped create mass publishing.

Wood-pulp paper

1850s to about 1985

Look for: Browns from the edges inward, turns brittle, and cracks when folded. Smells sweet and dusty.

Ground wood (mechanical pulp) and acidic sizing made paper cheap but self-destructive. Newspapers, pulps, wartime books and cheap reprints are worst.

Dates the book and lowers condition. Never fold a corner to test. Handle brittle books with care.

Acid-free paper

Standard from 1984 (ANSI Z39.48), revised 1992

Look for: Infinity-in-a-circle symbol or a statement on the copyright page. Stays white.

Buffered paper that lasts for centuries. Most quality books since the late 1980s use it.

Coated paper

1880s on

Look for: Smooth, shiny or satin surface, heavy for its size, cold to the touch.

A mineral coating lets fine halftone dots print sharply. Used for photo plates and art books. Damp makes coated pages stick together into a solid block, which destroys them.

Check for stuck pages in any book that may have been damp.

India and Bible paper

1870s on

Look for: Very thin, opaque, strong paper. Thick books that are surprisingly slim.

Used for Bibles, dictionaries, and compact editions of collected works. Oxford introduced it in the 1870s.

Watermarks

From the 1280s

Look for: A design visible only against the light: a name, crest, bunch of grapes, pot, date.

Papermakers marked their molds. Watermarks help date and place early paper, and dated watermarks (from the late 1700s in Britain) give a date the book can't be earlier than.

A watermark date after the title-page date can expose a later printing or a made-up copy.

Deckle edge

Natural on handmade paper; imitated by machine from the 1900s

Look for: Rough, feathery outer edges of a sheet.

On handmade paper the deckle is the natural edge from the mold frame. On modern trade books it is a machine imitation for a literary look.

Good on early books, neutral on modern ones. Never a defect.

Foxing and toning

Any paper

Look for: Foxing is scattered rusty brown spots. Toning is even yellowing.

Foxing comes from iron traces and mold in paper, worse in damp storage. Victorian steel engravings and early 1800s paper fox badly. Toning comes from acid and light.

Note the degree: light, moderate, heavy. Heavy foxing on plates cuts value.

Chapter III

Leather and skins

Leather was the normal covering for books for centuries. The kind of skin and how it was finished tells you age, cost and often where the book was bound.

Calf

Main English binding leather 1500s to 1800s

Look for: Smooth, fine surface, no visible grain pattern. Brown, often rubbed and cracked at the joints.

The everyday leather of British and American books. Variants: sprinkled calf (fine spatter of dark spots), mottled calf, and tree calf (acid used to make a tree-like pattern, popular about 1770 to 1850).

Common on 18th-century books. Dry, powdery 'red rot' and broken joints are frequent.

Morocco (goatskin)

Luxury leather from the 1500s to today

Look for: Visible pebbled or wrinkled grain. Takes bright colors: red, green, blue.

Goatskin is strong and fine. Levant morocco has a large, polished grain. Straight-grain morocco has parallel lines, popular about 1790 to 1830 (approximate). Used for fine and presentation bindings.

A sign of a quality binding. Signed bindings by known binders (Rivière, Zaehnsdorf, Sangorski & Sutcliffe, Bayntun) add value.

Sheep and roan

Cheap leather, 1600s to 1900s

Look for: Soft, thin, scuffs and peels easily. Roan is sheepskin dyed to look like morocco.

Used for cheaper books, school books, ledgers and many 19th-century half bindings.

Lower quality. Often badly worn.

Russia leather

1700s to 1800s

Look for: Diamond-pattern grain, reddish brown, smells of birch oil.

Treated cowhide originally from Russia. The smell is distinctive and lasts for centuries.

Pigskin

1400s to 1600s, revived later

Look for: Visible hair follicles in groups of three. Usually pale and very tough.

Common on German early books, often blind-tooled over wooden boards.

Alum-tawed skin

Medieval to 1600s

Look for: White or pale, slightly suede-like, very strong.

Skin treated with alum rather than tanned. Used on early books, especially in German lands.

Vellum and parchment

Pages before printing; covers 1500s to 1700s; revived by fine presses

Look for: Creamy, smooth, hard, slightly translucent. Warps with damp. Limp vellum has no boards.

Animal skin prepared without tanning. Limp vellum bindings were cheap and common in the 1500s and 1600s, especially in Italy and the Netherlands. Arts and Crafts and private presses brought vellum back around 1900.

On an early book, a strong sign. Check for warped boards.

Bonded and imitation leather

1900s on

Look for: Too-uniform grain, plastic feel, peels at the corners in thin layers.

Ground leather scraps or plastic made to look like leather. Common on decorator sets and cheap gift Bibles.

Not real leather binding. Price as decor.

Red rot

Leather from about 1830 to 1900 especially

Look for: Leather turns powdery and leaves red-brown dust on your hands.

Caused by tanning methods and pollution. It can't be reversed, only stabilized.

Note it. Wrap the book so the powder doesn't spread to other stock.

Chapter IV

Binding structures

How the pages are held together and how much of the cover is leather. Both change with time and cost.

Sewn on raised cords

Until about 1800 as standard

Look for: Real raised bands across the spine, and the spine leather glued directly to the back of the pages (tight back).

The sections were sewn by hand around cords or thongs, which show as bands. Hold the book open: on a tight back the spine flexes with the pages.

Strong sign of hand binding. False bands are glued-on strips; the spine won't flex with the pages.

Hollow back

From about 1800

Look for: Open the book: a tube or gap forms between the spine and the back of the pages.

Made the book open more easily and allowed gilt spines that didn't crack. Most 19th-century leather bindings are hollow.

Case binding

From the 1820s; the modern hardcover

Look for: Cover made separately and glued on as one piece. Endpapers do most of the holding.

Allowed publishers to bind thousands of copies the same way. All publishers' cloth books are case bound.

Hinges fail first. Check them.

Smyth sewing

Machine sewing from the 1880s

Look for: Look into the center of a section: you'll see thread running through the fold.

Machine sewing through the fold of each section. Better quality than glue alone.

Perfect binding

1930s on

Look for: No thread. The pages are cut at the spine and glued.

Standard for paperbacks and many cheap hardcovers. Old glue dries and pages fall out.

Stab-stitching

1600s to 1900s for pamphlets and parts

Look for: Thread or holes through the side of the whole book near the spine.

Quick sewing for pamphlets and parts. Old stab holes in a bound book show it was once issued as parts or pamphlets.

Quarter, half, three-quarter and full

All periods

Look for: Quarter: leather spine only. Half: leather spine and corners. Three-quarter: wider leather spine and larger corners. Full: entirely leather.

The more leather, the more expensive the binding was. Sides are usually marbled paper or cloth.

Use these words in descriptions.

Original boards

About 1780 to 1840

Look for: Plain blue-gray, buff or drab paper over boards, often with a printed paper spine label, and untrimmed edges.

A temporary binding. Owners were expected to have the book properly bound. Most were, so original boards are scarce.

Collectors pay a premium for books in original boards. Don't rebind or 'tidy' them.

Publishers' cloth

1820s on

Look for: Cloth-covered case binding, the same on every copy of an edition.

Introduced in the early 1820s. First plain with paper labels, then gilt-stamped spines from the 1830s, then fully decorated covers.

Original cloth in bright condition beats a later rebinding for 19th-century firsts.

Rebacked and recased

Any

Look for: Spine leather or cloth newer than the boards, sometimes with the old spine laid on top. New endpapers.

Repairs. Rebacking replaces the spine; recasing reattaches the text block to its cover.

Acceptable on early books if done well. Always disclose it.

Chapter V

Book cloth and grains

Victorian publishers embossed their cloth with patterns called grains. Bibliographers use grain to tell issues apart, and it helps date a book.

Plain dyed calico with paper label

1820s to early 1830s

Look for: Smooth plain cloth, no grain pattern, no gilt, a printed paper label on the spine.

The first generation of publishers' cloth. Gilt spine lettering replaced paper labels in the early 1830s.

A sign of an early cloth binding. Keep the label, however worn.

Ribbed cloth

From the 1830s

Look for: Fine parallel ribs. Vertical, horizontal or diagonal.

One of the most common grains. Bibliographers distinguish fine, coarse and diagonal ribbing.

Bead grain

c. 1840s to 1900s

Look for: Rows of tiny raised dots like beads on a string.

Common on mid-Victorian novels.

Pebble grain

c. 1850s to 1900s

Look for: Irregular rounded bumps like small pebbles.

Seen on many trade books of the 1860s to 80s.

Sand grain

Early 1830s on

Look for: Fine, even, slightly rough texture.

A common cheap finish that lasted into the 20th century.

Morocco grain

About 1830 on, most used in the mid-1850s

Look for: Cloth embossed to look like pebbled goatskin.

Imitation leather in cloth. Popular for Bibles, prayer books and gift books.

Ripple and wave grains

c. 1840s to 1880s

Look for: Wavy lines like water.

A decorative grain popular in the middle of the century.

Diaper and patterned grains

Diaper from the early 1830s

Look for: Repeating diamond, dot, or floral pattern embossed into the cloth.

Very decorative. Often on gift books and annuals. All-over ribbon-embossed patterns (moire, floral, crackle, star) were fashionable only from the 1830s to the mid-1840s, so they date a binding closely.

Buckram and linen

c. 1880s on

Look for: Coarse visible weave, stiff and durable.

Buckram is heavily sized cloth used for library bindings and quality trade books. Linen is finer.

Plain buckram with call numbers usually means a library rebind.

Gilt and blind stamping

c. 1830s on

Look for: Gold lettering and designs pressed into the cloth (gilt), or pressed in without color (blind).

Brass dies stamped designs onto the case in one go. From the 1880s to 1910s, designers such as Margaret Armstrong and Talwin Morris signed elaborate covers.

Bright gilt and a signed design add value. Dull, rubbed gilt is common.

Short-lived grains

1830s to 1860s

Look for: Trefoil (three-lobed) grain, hexagon or honeycomb grain.

Trefoil grain appears in the mid-1850s, hexagon and honeycomb grains in the 1860s. Because they were used briefly, they date a binding closely.

Chapter VI

Cover decoration

Tooling and decoration styles changed with fashion, so they help date leather bindings.

Blind tooling

Medieval to 1500s; revived later

Look for: Patterns pressed into leather without gold, darker than the surrounding leather.

The main decoration on early bindings, often with roll-stamped borders and panel stamps.

Gold tooling

From the late 1400s in Europe

Look for: Gold leaf pressed into leather with heated tools, in lines, ornaments and lettering.

Arrived in Europe through Italy from the Islamic world. Styles changed by period: fanfare patterns, cottage-roof designs, neoclassical borders, Victorian revival styles.

Fine, crisp gilt on a period binding adds a lot.

Armorial bindings

1500s to 1800s

Look for: A coat of arms stamped in gilt on the covers.

Shows the original owner, often a noble family or institution.

Provenance adds value. Look up the arms.

Spine labels

1600s on

Look for: Leather or paper labels on the spine carrying the title, often a contrasting color.

Red and green morocco labels on calf are typical of the 1700s.

Fore-edge painting

Mainly 1780s to 1900s, many later fakes

Look for: Fan the closed edge slightly: a painted scene appears. Gilt hides it when the book is closed.

A specialty of English binders. Many were added in the 20th century to older books.

Collectible, but always flag. Dating the painting needs an expert.

Gauffered edges

1500s to 1600s, revived 1800s

Look for: Gilt edges with patterns tooled into them.

A luxury finish on early and some Victorian bindings.

Chapter VII

Endpapers, marbling and edges

Marbled paper has distinct named patterns, and each was fashionable in different periods and places.

Turkish or stone marble

1600s on

Look for: Random round or irregular spots of color, like pebbles.

The oldest European pattern, made by dropping colors on a size bath. Very common across centuries.

Dutch curl

1600s to 1700s

Look for: Rows of tight curls, often red, blue, yellow and green.

Common on German and Dutch books and English books of the 1700s.

French curl and snail

1600s to 1800s

Look for: Large spirals or snail-shell curls.

Made by turning a comb or stylus through the colors.

Nonpareil

1800s mainly

Look for: Very fine, regular, comb-like stripes, like feathers.

Common on 19th-century half bindings and endpapers.

Shell (French shell)

1800s

Look for: Each spot of color has a lighter ring, like a shell.

Made by adding oil to the colors.

Spanish marble

1800s

Look for: Diagonal bands of light and dark, like folded fabric.

Made by moving the paper while laying it on the bath.

Coated and glazed endpapers

1840s to 1900s

Look for: Plain colored endpapers with a shiny surface: chocolate brown, dark green, yellow, cream.

Typical of Victorian publishers' cloth. Yellow endpapers are common on 1850s to 1890s books. Victorian publishers also printed advertisements on endpapers, especially on yellowbacks and cheap reprints. The titles advertised can date the copy.

A good dating clue for 19th-century cloth.

Printed and pictorial endpapers

1890s on

Look for: Maps, patterns, illustrations or publisher's ads printed on the endpapers.

Common in children's books, travel and series books.

On some books the endpaper design is an issue point.

Gilt edges

1500s on

Look for: Gold leaf on page edges. 'All edges gilt' (aeg) or 'top edge gilt' (teg).

Top edge gilt protects against dust and was common on quality books from the 1880s to 1930s.

Sprinkled and stained edges

1600s to 1800s for sprinkled; any for stained

Look for: Fine spatter of color (often red or blue), or an even wash of color.

Sprinkled edges are typical of 18th-century calf bindings. Modern special editions use sprayed and printed edges. A stained top edge was standard on many 20th-century trade hardcovers, while book club editions often left it plain. It is a useful trade versus club check.

Uncut and unopened

Mainly 1700s to 1930s

Look for: Uncut: rough, untrimmed edges. Unopened: some folds still joined.

Uncut means the binder didn't trim. Unopened means no reader cut the folds. Collectors like both.

Never cut or trim them.

Chapter VIII

Printing

There are three basic ways to put ink on paper, and every printing process is one of them.

Relief printing (letterpress)

1450 to the 1970s as the main method

Look for: Type or blocks pressed into the paper. A slight dent you can feel, and ink squeezed to the edges of letters.

The raised surface is inked and pressed onto paper. Covers metal type, woodcuts, wood engravings and line blocks.

On a modern book, letterpress points to a fine press.

Intaglio (engraving, etching)

1400s on

Look for: A rectangular plate mark pressed into the paper around the image. Ink stands up slightly from the surface.

Ink sits in lines cut into a metal plate. Wiped, then pressed with great force. Needs a separate press, so plates are usually on separate pages.

Plates printed this way are often the most valuable part of a book.

Planographic (lithography, offset)

1796 on; offset dominant from the 1960s and 70s

Look for: Flat print with no dent and no plate mark.

The image is drawn or transferred onto a flat surface that accepts ink only where it should. Offset transfers the ink via a rubber blanket. Most modern books are offset.

The hand press

1450 to about 1800

Look for: Uneven inking, deep impression, variation page to page.

Wooden screw press, two workers, about 250 sheets an hour on one side. Iron presses like the Stanhope (about 1800) made it easier.

Steam and cylinder presses

1814 on

Look for: Even impression, faster production, more copies.

The Times of London printed by steam in 1814. Cheap printing fed the huge 19th-century readership.

Hand-set metal type

1450 to the 1880s for books

Look for: Slight unevenness, broken letters repeating through a book.

Compositors set each letter by hand into a composing stick.

Linotype and Monotype

Linotype 1886; Monotype patented 1887, machines from 1898

Look for: Even, regular typesetting. Hard to tell apart from hand-set without a loupe.

Machines cast type from keyboards. Linotype cast whole lines; Monotype cast single letters.

Phototypesetting and digital

1960s to 1980s, then digital

Look for: Flat offset print, very regular letters, sometimes slightly soft.

Type set photographically, then by computer. Combined with offset, it made printing cheap and fast.

Chapter IX

Type and page features

Letterforms and page conventions changed slowly. A few of them date a book almost on sight.

Blackletter (gothic)

1450s on; English use faded through the 1600s; German Fraktur phased out from 1941

Look for: Heavy, angular letters.

The first printed type, copying the handwriting of the time. A circular of 3 January 1941 began a gradual switch of German printing from Fraktur to roman type, so German trade books in Fraktur are usually before 1941.

Roman and italic

Roman from 1465 in Italy; italic from 1501

Look for: The upright and slanted letters we still use.

Developed by Italian printers such as Jenson and Aldus Manutius.

Old style, transitional and modern faces

Old style to about 1750; transitional about 1750 to 1800; modern (Didone) about 1800 to 1900

Look for: Old style has gentle contrast and slanted shading. Modern faces have strong thick and thin strokes and hairline serifs.

Caslon (old style) is typical of 18th-century English books. Bodoni and Didot (modern) are typical of the early 1800s. The Chiswick Press revived Caslon from the 1840s, and old style faces came back widely later in the century. Old style type on smooth wove paper with no long s is a Victorian or later revival, not an 18th-century book.

Long s

To about 1790 to 1810 in Britain, about 1820 in America

Look for: An s shaped like an f without the right side of the crossbar, used at the start and middle of words.

Dropped quickly once printers stopped using it. Facsimiles reproduce it, so check the paper too.

Catchwords

About 1470 to 1820

Look for: The first word of the next page printed alone at the bottom right.

Helped keep pages in order.

Signature marks

1470s on, numbers into the 1900s

Look for: Letters or numbers (A, A2, B, B2) at the foot of the first pages of each gathering.

Told the binder the order of the sections. Use them to check a book is complete.

Rubrication

1450s to early 1500s

Look for: Red and blue initials and marks added by hand after printing.

Early printers left spaces for hand decoration, copying manuscripts.

A sign of incunabula and very early books.

Colophon

1450s on

Look for: Printer's note at the end giving printer, place and date, sometimes with a printer's device.

In early books it took the place of the title page. In modern fine press books it names the type, paper and edition size.

Chapter X

Illustration processes

A loupe and a raking light will tell you how most pictures were made. The process dates the picture and often decides its value.

Woodcut

1400s on

Look for: Bold black lines, little fine shading. Printed on the same page as the text.

Cut along the grain of a plank. The earliest printed book illustrations. Revived by artists around 1900.

Wood engraving

About 1790 to 1900; revived by private presses

Look for: Very fine white lines on black, detailed shading, printed with the text.

Cut into the end grain of hard boxwood, popularized by Thomas Bewick. The main way of illustrating Victorian magazines and books.

Copper engraving and etching

1500s to 1820s

Look for: Fine lines and a plate mark, usually on a separate page.

Engraving cuts lines with a tool; etching uses acid. Copper wears quickly, so early impressions are sharpest.

Steel engraving

About 1820 to 1880

Look for: Extremely fine, grey, silvery lines. Often with a tissue guard in front.

Steel plates lasted for thousands of impressions, so Victorian books used them heavily.

Usually heavily foxed. Common in gift books.

Aquatint and mezzotint

Aquatint 1770s to 1830s; mezzotint 1660s to 1850s

Look for: Aquatint: soft tones like a watercolor wash, with a fine grain. Mezzotint: velvety dark tones.

Tonal intaglio processes. Aquatints were often hand colored for travel and topographical books.

Colored aquatint plate books are high value.

Hand coloring

Mainly 1700 to 1880

Look for: Watercolor that varies copy to copy, sometimes slightly over the lines.

Colorists worked by hand on printed plates. Natural history, botany, costume and travel books.

Top of the market. Check every plate is present.

Lithography

1796 on; common in books from the 1820s

Look for: Soft, crayon-like or brushed look, no plate mark, flat surface.

Drawn on stone with greasy crayon. Used for maps, portraits, views and music.

Chromolithography

1840s to 1930s

Look for: Bright, layered flat colors, often with a fine grain. No dots.

A separate stone for each color, sometimes more than ten. Owen Jones's Grammar of Ornament (1856) is a famous example.

Collected, especially ornament, botanical and children's books.

Collotype and photogravure

1870s to 1950s

Look for: Photographic look with no dots. Photogravure has a fine grain and rich blacks; collotype a fine wormy grain under a loupe.

High-quality photographic reproduction for art books and fine plates.

Halftone

1880s on

Look for: Under a loupe, the image is made of regular dots of varying size.

Broke a photograph into dots so it could print with type. Color halftone from the 1890s.

Shows a reproduction. A 'print' made of halftone dots is not an original engraving.

Pochoir

About 1900 to 1930s, mainly France

Look for: Very bright, flat, slightly raised gouache color, crisp edges.

Hand stenciling. Used in fashion plates and Art Deco books.

Collected.

Tipped-in plates

1900s to 1940s

Look for: A separately printed picture glued along one edge to a page, often on a tinted mount with a tissue guard.

The format of the golden age of gift books (Rackham, Dulac, Nielsen).

Check none are missing.

Mounted photographs

1843 to about 1910

Look for: Real photographic prints pasted onto pages.

Before halftone, the only way to put a photo in a book. Anna Atkins's cyanotype algae book (1843) was the first.

Often valuable. Flag it.

Chapter XI

Dust jackets

On 20th-century firsts, the jacket can carry most of the value. Learn to read it like a copyright page.

Early wrappers

1820s to about 1900

Look for: Plain or lightly printed paper wrappers, often just the title.

The earliest known printed publisher's jacket dates from 1829, and a plain one from 1819 may be earlier. Printed jackets were common from the late 1870s, and printing on the flaps from the late 1890s. Almost all were thrown away.

Very rare. Flag any jacket on a book before about 1920.

Pictorial jackets

1900s on, standard by the 1920s

Look for: Printed art, blurbs on the flaps, price on the front flap.

Jackets became marketing. Collectors focus on firsts in jackets from this point.

Price and issue points

1920s on

Look for: Printed price, reviews on the back, ads for other titles, the designer's name.

For many famous firsts, the jacket has its own points: the price, a review quoted, a typo.

Write down the exact price and anything printed on the back.

Book club jackets

1926 on

Look for: No price, or 'Book Club Edition' on the front flap corner. Doubleday clubs later used a five-digit code in a box on the rear panel instead.

The quickest book club tell. Book club copies may also be slightly smaller, on thinner paper. Not every unpriced jacket is a club copy: some 1970s BOMC jackets carry a price, Family Bookshelf jackets are usually priced, and some unpriced British jackets are export copies.

Price-clipped jackets

Any

Look for: Corner of the front flap cut off.

Usually done when giving the book as a gift.

Lowers value on collectible firsts. Always disclose.

Facsimile jackets

1974 on, common from the 1980s and 1990s

Look for: Too crisp, too bright, glossy modern paper, 'facsimile' in tiny print on a flap.

Reproductions made to dress jacketless firsts.

Must be disclosed. Flag if unsure.

Protective covers

Any

Look for: Clear Mylar or Brodart covers.

Protect the jacket. Library Brodart covers are often taped or glued on, which means ex-library.

Chapter XII

Handling and care

How you handle a book on intake can change its value. These rules protect both.

Don't tape, glue or erase

Look for: Any repair attempt.

Tape stains in a few years and pulls paper when removed. Erasing damages paper. Ask before any repair.

Support the spine

Look for: Old and tight bindings.

Don't open old books flat. Support the boards and open only as far as the binding allows.

Isolate mold

Look for: Musty smell, fuzzy spots, white or colored bloom.

Bag it and keep it away from the stock. Mold spreads in damp conditions.

Leave inserts in place

Look for: Letters, ads, slips, errata leaves, tissue guards.

They may be issue points or valuable in their own right. Note them in the description.

Store upright and dry

Look for: Shelving.

Stable temperature, moderate humidity, away from direct sun. Sunlight fades spines quickly.

Chapter XIII

How books changed

Every few decades the trade changed what it printed on a book: where the copyright notice sits, what numbers appear, how the price is shown, what the cover is made of. Each change gives you an earliest or latest possible date. Pick a theme to follow one thread through time.

18th century

  1. 1710 Copyright and the copyright page
    The Statute of Anne, Britain's first copyright law, is passed.
    Early English books do not carry a copyright notice. Imprints read 'Printed for' a bookseller, with the date at the foot of the title page.
  2. 1790 Copyright and the copyright page
    The first US copyright act is passed.
    American books from the 1790s may name the district clerk who recorded the title. Few say more than that.
  3. 1799 Paper, type and printing
    Nicolas-Louis Robert patents a continuous paper machine in France.
    Machine paper is wove and very even. Laid paper with chain lines usually means handmade.

19th century

  1. 1802 Copyright and the copyright page
    US law first requires a printed copyright notice on every copy.
    From here an American book claiming copyright should carry a notice. Before 1870 it names a District Court clerk's office.
  2. 1803 Paper, type and printing
    The Fourdriniers build working paper machines in England, with Bryan Donkin.
    By the 1830s most English book paper is machine-made.
  3. 1819 Jackets and bindings (approximate date)
    A German annual of 1819 may have the earliest known publisher's jacket, a plain paper wrapper.
    Jackets are older than most people think, but almost none survive from before 1870.
  4. 1820 Jackets and bindings
    Publisher's cloth appears. Pickering's Diamond Classics use cloth over ready-made cases.
    Before the 1820s, books were sold in boards or paper to be bound later. Original publisher's cloth on a book means about 1820 or later.
  5. 1829 Prices, barcodes and marks
    The earliest known printed publisher's wrapper already prints the price (12 shillings).
    Prices printed on wrappers are as old as jackets. On a jacket, the printed price is evidence the jacket belongs to a trade copy.
  6. 1829 Jackets and bindings
    The Friendship's Offering wrapper of 1829 is the earliest known printed publisher's jacket.
    It wrapped the book fully and was sealed. Any jacket from before the 1870s is a great rarity.
  7. c.1830s Copyright and the copyright page (approximate date)
    US notices settle on the back of the title page.
    A notice reading 'Entered according to Act of Congress' on the title verso is typical of American books from about the 1830s to 1870. Check the clerk's district against the place of publication.
  8. 1830 Jackets and bindings
    Adhesive (unsewn) binding is first tried in England, as Hancock's rubber 'caoutchouc' binding.
    Early adhesive bindings failed. Unsewn glued spines are a 20th-century norm, so on a Victorian book they are rare.
  9. 1832 Jackets and bindings (approximate date)
    Gold stamping directly onto cloth comes in.
    Gilt lettering on the cloth spine points to after about 1832. Earlier cloth used paper labels.
  10. 1841 Paperbacks
    Tauchnitz begins its Collection of British Authors in Leipzig. Volume 1 is dated 1842.
    Paper-covered English novels printed in Leipzig for the Continent. They say they are not for sale in Britain.
  11. 1850s Paper, type and printing
    Wood-pulp paper starts to replace rag paper.
    Browning, brittle pages usually mean a book from the 1850s to the 1980s.
  12. 1850s Jackets and bindings
    Jackets with folded flaps appear. The earliest flapped jackets in English are often dated 1857.
    A flapped jacket is the modern form. Early ones are plain and printed in one color.
  13. 1850s-1890s Paperbacks (approximate date)
    Yellowbacks, cheap novels in glazed pictorial boards, are sold at railway bookstalls.
    Bright yellow glazed boards with a printed picture and ads on the back mean a Victorian cheap edition.
  14. 1860 Paperbacks
    Beadle's Dime Novels begin in June 1860 with Malaeska, in orange wrappers.
    Thin paper-covered American novels at ten cents. Early numbers are scarce.
  15. 1870 Copyright and the copyright page
    US registration moves from the district courts to the Library of Congress (8 July 1870).
    A notice naming a District Court clerk's office means before about 1870. One naming the Librarian of Congress, at Washington, means 1870 or later.
  16. 1874 Copyright and the copyright page (approximate date)
    US law allows the short notice 'Copyright, 18.., by A.B.'
    The short 'Copyright 1885 by' form points to 1874 or later. Both long and short forms are seen until 1909.
  17. late 1870s Jackets and bindings
    Printed jackets become common, usually plain light blue, grey or tan paper.
    Publishers issued jackets from here on, but most were thrown away. A surviving 1880s jacket is rare.
  18. 1880s Paper, type and printing
    The halftone process is invented (Ives 1881, Meisenbach 1882).
    A photo made of tiny dots under a loupe means 1880s or later. Real photographs glued in are earlier or special.
  19. 1886 Paper, type and printing
    The Linotype machine is first tested at the New-York Tribune.
    Mechanical typesetting cut costs. You cannot easily see it in a book.
  20. 1887 Paper, type and printing
    Lanston's Monotype is patented. Commercial machines follow in 1897 to 1898.
    Most fine 20th-century book text was set on Monotype.
  21. 1890 Copyright and the copyright page (approximate date)
    The McKinley Tariff of 1890 starts the US rule that imported goods name their country of origin.
    'Printed in Great Britain' or 'Printed in Germany' on an imported book points to after about 1891. The line became routine on export copies.
  22. 1891 Copyright and the copyright page
    The Chace Act gives foreign authors US copyright, if their books are typeset in the US (in force 1 July 1891).
    Before July 1891, cheap American editions of British novels were legal and often unauthorized. After it, an authorized US edition of a British author often says 'Copyright' with a US date.

20th century

  1. 1901 Numbers and printing statements (approximate date)
    The Library of Congress begins selling printed catalog cards.
    A 'Library of Congress Catalog Card Number' on the copyright page is a 20th-century feature. It becomes common from about the 1940s on US books.
  2. c.1902 Numbers and printing statements
    Some publishers begin marking first printings. Appleton adds a '(1)' after the last line of text from 1902.
    Printing codes in old books are publisher-specific. Use a points guide for each publisher.
  3. 1900s Jackets and bindings
    Blurbs and flap copy become usual. Gelett Burgess coins 'blurb' in 1907.
    Flap text, reviews and author notes point to after about 1900.
  4. 1909 Copyright and the copyright page
    New US Copyright Act: a correct notice is required, or the work falls into the public domain.
    Twentieth-century American books carry 'Copyright 19xx by' on the title verso. A missing notice on a US book of 1909 to 1989 is unusual.
  5. 1909 Copyright and the copyright page
    The © symbol is allowed, but at first only for maps, art, photographs and prints.
    Books still used the word 'Copyright' or 'Copr.' Most US books before the mid-1950s spell the word out.
  6. 1910 Copyright and the copyright page (approximate date)
    The Buenos Aires Convention asks for a statement reserving rights. 'All rights reserved' spreads.
    'All rights reserved' became common on copyright pages in the 20th century. It does not date a book closely on its own.
  7. 1920s Numbers and printing statements
    US publishers begin printing 'First Edition' or 'First Printing' statements (Holt from 1924, Lippincott from 1925).
    A first-edition statement in a 1920s or 1930s American book is a good sign, but practice varied by publisher and by year. Book club copies sometimes keep the statement.
  8. 1920s Jackets and bindings
    Jackets become designed, illustrated selling tools. Bindings underneath get plainer.
    For a book of the 1920s to 1960s, the jacket is often most of the value. Grade it as carefully as the book.
  9. 1926 Book clubs and the trade
    The Book-of-the-Month Club sends its first selection in April 1926 (Lolly Willowes).
    Club copies exist from here on. Early ones were bought from the publisher and look like trade copies.
  10. 1926 Book clubs and the trade
    BOMC marks its July 1926 selection with a gilt spine stamp, dropped in 1927.
    A gilt 'Book-of-the-Month Club Selection' panel on a 1926 or 1927 spine marks an early club copy of trade sheets.
  11. 1927 Book clubs and the trade
    The Literary Guild is founded.
    The Guild is the second big US club. Later Guild copies are smaller and cheaper than trade copies.
  12. 1930 Book clubs and the trade
    BOMC begins printing its own editions in July 1930.
    From here, BOMC copies are separate printings. Look for the blind dot or shape on the lower rear board and an unpriced jacket.
  13. 1932 Paperbacks
    Albatross Modern Continental Library starts in Hamburg. No. 1 is Joyce's Dubliners.
    Its look and color coding were a model for Penguin.
  14. 1930s on Book clubs and the trade (approximate date)
    Club copies settle into their marks: no price on the flap, 'Book Club Edition' on the flap, and a blind stamp on the rear board.
    No price plus a blind stamp is the classic club copy. Some trade books have no price, so check the board too.
  15. 1935 Paperbacks
    Penguin Books launches on 30 July 1935 at sixpence, color-coded by subject.
    Orange is fiction, green crime, blue biography. Early numbered Penguins in jackets are collected.
  16. 1939 Paperbacks
    Pocket Books launches the US mass-market paperback at 25 cents.
    Small 25-cent paperbacks with the kangaroo logo date from 1939 on.
  17. 1940 Wartime books
    Britain rations paper for publishers from March 1940, at 60 percent of 1938-39 use.
    British books from 1940 on use thinner paper and smaller type. Grade them against other wartime copies.
  18. 1942 Wartime books
    The Book Production War Economy Agreement takes effect on 1 January 1942.
    A small emblem or note about the 'Book Production War Economy Standard' dates a British book to 1942 or the years just after.
  19. 1943 Paperbacks
    Armed Services Editions are printed for US troops, 1943 to 1947. Over 1,300 titles.
    Wide, short paperbacks bound on the short side with two columns of text.
  20. 1943 Wartime books (approximate date)
    The US War Production Board limits paper for book publishers (Order L-245, 'Books').
    Some books carry notices such as 'War Edition' or 'complete text, reduced size' to meet paper rules.
  21. 1945 Paperbacks
    Bantam Books is founded. No. 1 is Twain's Life on the Mississippi.
    Early Bantams with low numbers are collected.
  22. 1940s Numbers and printing statements (approximate date)
    Number lines (printer's keys) appear in some books by the late 1940s. Crowell's 1947 practice is one early case.
    A number line on a book from before about 1960 is unusual. Its lowest number usually gives the printing.
  23. 1950 Book clubs and the trade
    Reader's Digest Condensed Books begin. Renamed Select Editions in 1997.
    Abridged, several novels per volume, in matching bindings. Not first editions.
  24. 1950 Paperbacks
    Fawcett's Gold Medal line publishes paperback originals.
    A Gold Medal paperback from the 1950s may be the true first edition of a book.
  25. 1953 Book clubs and the trade
    The Science Fiction Book Club starts (first ads November 1952). It stopped taking orders in January 2025.
    SFBC copies have no jacket price and no first-edition statement. A few are true firsts, so check.
  26. 1953 Paperbacks
    Doubleday's Anchor Books launches the 'quality' trade paperback in April 1953.
    Larger paperbacks of serious books, at 65 cents to $1.25. Vintage followed in 1954.
  27. 1953 Paper, type and printing
    The first book typeset entirely by phototypesetting appears.
    Film-set type (1950s to 1980s) looks crisp and flat with no bite into the paper.
  28. 1954 Copyright and the copyright page
    The © symbol becomes acceptable on all US works. The US joins the Universal Copyright Convention, in force 16 September 1955.
    A © symbol in a US book points to the mid-1950s or later, though some publishers kept writing 'Copyright' as well.
  29. 1958 Book clubs and the trade
    Doubleday starts printing gutter codes (mid-1958 to mid-1987).
    A small letter-and-number code in the gutter of the last pages gives the year and week of printing. The last known code is from September 1987.
  30. 1950s-60s Prices, barcodes and marks
    Some US jacket flaps are printed with several prices, and the unused ones are cut off at the bindery.
    A clipped flap corner with a price still showing is a 'machine clip', not a price-clipped jacket. It does not lower value the way an owner's clip does.
  31. 1960s-80s Book clubs and the trade
    Doubleday sprays purple or speckled dye on page edges of remaindered books.
    A colored speckle on the edge of a Doubleday book usually means a remainder, not a club copy.
  32. 1966 Numbers and printing statements
    The 9-digit Standard Book Number (SBN) is devised in Britain. It is in use by 1967.
    A 9-digit SBN dates a British book to about 1967 to 1974.
  33. 1970 Numbers and printing statements
    The 10-digit ISBN becomes international standard ISO 2108.
    An ISBN on a book that claims to be from before 1970 means a later printing or reissue.
  34. 1960s-70s Paper, type and printing (approximate date)
    Offset lithography replaces letterpress for most books. Film setting replaces hot metal.
    No bite or dent in the paper usually means offset. Letterpress on a recent book suggests a fine press.
  35. 1971 Numbers and printing statements
    The Library of Congress Cataloging in Publication (CIP) program begins.
    A CIP data block on the copyright page means the book is from 1971 or later.
  36. 1971 Prices, barcodes and marks
    Britain switches to decimal currency on 15 February 1971.
    A British jacket priced in shillings and pence is from before 1971. A dual price such as '30s / £1.50' points to about 1968 to 1971.
  37. 1970s Numbers and printing statements
    Number lines become the normal way to mark printings.
    Most US trade books from the 1970s on have a number line. Watch the exceptions, such as Random House firsts with a line starting at 2 until about 2003.
  38. c.1977 Numbers and printing statements (approximate date)
    The British Library begins its own CIP programme.
    'British Library Cataloguing in Publication Data' on a British copyright page means the late 1970s or later.
  39. 1978 Copyright and the copyright page
    The 1976 Copyright Act takes effect on 1 January 1978.
    Notices change little in look, but terms change. This date matters for value research, not for dating by eye.
  40. 1979 Prices, barcodes and marks
    The Thor Power Tool tax ruling makes US publishers remainder or destroy unsold stock more often.
    Remainder marks (a marker stroke or stamp on the edge of the pages) become common on US books from the late 1970s on.
  41. 1980 Prices, barcodes and marks
    The EAN authority and the ISBN agency agree to create the 'Bookland' barcode prefix (978, later 979).
    This sets up the book barcode, but barcodes on books are rare until the mid-1980s.
  42. 1970s-80s Jackets and bindings (approximate date)
    Paper-covered boards with cloth spines (half cloth) replace full cloth on many trade hardcovers to cut costs.
    Paper boards under the jacket are typical of the 1970s on. Full cloth on a recent trade book is now a sign of quality.
  43. late 1970s-2007 Prices, barcodes and marks (approximate date)
    Mass-market paperbacks carry a UPC grocery-style barcode on the back cover, with the Bookland EAN inside the front cover.
    A UPC on the back of a paperback, with the ISBN barcode inside the cover, is typical of mass-market books before about 2007.
  44. 1984 Paper, type and printing
    ANSI Z39.48, the US standard for permanent paper, is published. Revised in 1992.
    The infinity sign in a circle, or a statement on acid-free paper, means 1984 or later.
  45. 1985 Prices, barcodes and marks
    BISG publishes machine-readable coding guidelines for US books.
    A barcode on the jacket of a book that claims to be from the 1970s means a later jacket or later printing. Barcodes spread across US jackets in the late 1980s.
  46. 1985 Paper, type and printing
    PageMaker and laser printers start desktop publishing.
    Small-press and self-published books from the late 1980s on are often laid out on a computer.
  47. 1986 Copyright and the copyright page
    The manufacturing clause expires on 1 July 1986.
    Before this, books by American authors had to be printed in the US or Canada to keep full protection. After it, 'Printed in Hong Kong' or 'Printed in China' becomes common on American books, especially illustrated ones.
  48. 1989 Copyright and the copyright page
    The US joins the Berne Convention on 1 March 1989. A copyright notice becomes optional.
    Notices continue in nearly all trade books, so their presence does not date a book. A US book with no notice is more likely after 1989.
  49. 1990s Book clubs and the trade
    Barnes & Noble and Borders superstores spread across the US.
    Heavy discounts hurt clubs. Remainder marks and retailer stickers on jackets from this era are common.
  50. 1995 Book clubs and the trade
    Amazon sells its first book in July 1995.
    Not visible on a book, but it marks the start of online used-book pricing.
  51. 1996 Book clubs and the trade
    The AbeBooks website goes live.
    Online listings made common books cheaper and rare books easier to price.
  52. 1997 Paper, type and printing
    Ingram forms Lightning Print (later Lightning Source) for print on demand.
    A last page with a printing code and a place such as La Vergne TN means a print-on-demand copy. No collector value.

21st century

  1. 2005 Numbers and printing statements
    ISO 2108:2005 sets up 13-digit ISBNs. Many books print both 10 and 13 digits during 2005 and 2006.
    A book with both a 10-digit and a 13-digit ISBN usually dates from about 2005 to 2007.
  2. 2007 Numbers and printing statements
    ISBNs become 13 digits on 1 January 2007.
    A 13-digit ISBN alone usually means 2007 or later.
  3. 2007 Paper, type and printing
    The Espresso Book Machine is shown at the New York Public Library in June 2007.
    Single copies printed in a shop. These are modern reprints.
  4. 2007 Paper, type and printing
    Amazon releases the Kindle on 19 November 2007.
    Ebooks spread. Print runs of many trade books fall afterward.
  5. 2020 Numbers and printing statements
    The US begins issuing ISBNs that start 979-8.
    An ISBN starting 979-8 means a US book from 2020 or later, very often self-published.
  6. 2020s Jackets and bindings
    Sprayed or printed page edges spread from subscription-box editions to trade first printings.
    Sprayed edges on a trade book usually mean the 2020s. Fourth Wing (2023) is an often cited first-printing example.
