**Premise.** At 28, Kabi Nagata has never dated, kissed anyone or had sex. Ten years earlier, after high school, she dropped out of an art college after half a year; depression, anxiety, an eating disorder and self-cutting followed, and she drifted through part-time jobs while living with her parents and trying to win their approval (Otaku USA; Manga Bookshelf; The OASG; Autostraddle; imidas; Japanese Wikipedia). She had told herself she had "no right" to eat and must not think about sex, because she believed her parents loved her more as a child (imidas, quoting the book). Seeking a change, she searches for a lesbian escort service, starts bathing and dressing every day, books a session, and meets the worker in a love hotel (imidas; Anime Feminist). The book opens on that scene, then goes back to show how she got there (Autostraddle; The Slings & Arrows). The encounter is awkward and does not fix her loneliness; she realizes that what she wanted was to be held and cared for and that she had never loved herself. She posts the story as a manga, and its warm reception online is part of the ending (Anime Feminist; Otaku USA; Manga Bookshelf; imidas). The publisher calls it "the candid tell-all of a young woman's struggles with depression and sexuality" (Seven Seas page).

## Life message

**A good life starts with treating yourself as worth caring for: stop living for others' approval, tell the truth about what you feel, and take small, concrete steps toward the work and connection you actually want.**

## Creator and publisher context

- **Kabi Nagata, on record:** born May 28, 1987; writes under a pen name; dropped out of an oil-painting course at art college after six months; won a magazine manga prize at 25 and published short fiction in Harta before turning to her own life (Japanese Wikipedia). She had "no interest at all" in essay manga before this book, which was her first (Real Sound, 2025). She posted the story to Pixiv in 2015 thinking that "doing something people rarely do might break through my situation" and resolving to hold nothing back (Japanese Wikipedia). She drafts by listing events and feelings and arranging them, and aimed to neither lower nor glorify herself (Wikipedia, citing interviews not opened here).
- **On the paid encounter:** "I haven't gone to a lesbian fūzoku since I drew the manga. Even if I'm held or receive sexual things in exchange for money, I thought I couldn't reach the kind of relationship I want. Of course there are people who need it, so I'm not rejecting lesbian fūzoku. It's just that for me it was different" (Bunshun Online, 2021; this entry's translation).
- **On sexuality:** lacks "a really clear grasp or understanding of my own sexuality"; does not set out to make yuri (ANN, 2021); had no dating experience by 34 and did not know whether she was drawn to men or women (Bunshun, 2021).
- **On family:** felt that turning real events into manga "made my parents cry" and set essay manga aside from September 2018, returning in 2019 (Japanese Wikipedia); her mother asked in 2021 whether a later book could be stopped, and her mother then checked her drafts for a time; today the family is "fairly harmonious" (Real Sound, 2025). She regrets how she portrayed her family early on (ANN, 2021). She tries to "make sure that what I'm creating isn't hurting anyone" while staying honest (ANN, 2021).
- **Later life on record:** hospitalization for alcohol-related acute pancreatitis (2018), and a 2022 collapse from overwork followed by years of flashbacks, covered in later books; she calls drawing manga both "a lethal dose of poison" and as necessary as "air and water" (Real Sound, 2025; Japanese Wikipedia). No statements on religion or politics found. Nothing beyond what she has published or said on record is given here.
- **Publisher:** East Press (Japan, after the Pixiv original); Seven Seas Entertainment (English), which has published her later essay books in English and reissued this one as a 2023 hardcover with a new bonus chapter (Wikipedia; Penguin Random House). ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a lonely person actually need? Kabi believes the answer is sex, or at least touch: if she can get through a first sexual experience, perhaps she will become "normal" and able to talk to people (Anime Corner). The book tests that belief against the record of her own life.

**The answer the story gives.** Not sex, and not approval. The encounter in the love hotel is awkward; she freezes, and the purchase does not include "intimacy, gratification, or comfort" (Anime Corner; Anime Feminist). What she discovers is older and harder: "Because I couldn't love myself, I'd always treat myself and my accomplishments like they were crap" (Otaku USA, quoting the book; The Lesbrary paraphrases the same line). The way forward is to treat herself as someone worth caring for: bathe, change clothes, dress well, keep working, tell the truth in her manga (imidas; Anime Feminist). Sex is "not the end, only the beginning of more discoveries" (Otaku USA). Five years later the author said the same in her own voice: paying to be held could not give her the relationship she wanted (Bunshun).

**Characters as arguments.** Kabi is the whole argument: the depressed young woman who wants her parents' approval, and the older narrator who looks back with irony and kindness. Her parents stand for a conventional life she cannot live, though reviewers say they are left thin (The OASG), and the author later said she regretted how she drew them (ANN interview). The escort workers stand for the limits of a transaction: kind and professional, but not friends.

**Symbols and motifs.** The love hotel in daylight (The Slings & Arrows); the bald patch and the scars on her arm as visible marks of inner pain (imidas; Autostraddle); the daily bath as the first act of self-care (imidas); pink screentone on black and white (Wikipedia); the book itself as the turn, since publishing the story and being received kindly is part of its ending (Manga Bookshelf; imidas).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese I-novel and essay manga, with their candid self-exposure; the recovery memoir; the coming-of-age story moved to the late twenties (Autostraddle). Amamiya Karin compares it with her own autobiographical debut, a book written as if "I'll die if I don't write this" (imidas). Religious readers may see a picture of a soul seeking in the wrong place; Buddhist readers may see craving for approval loosened. These are readings, not the book's claims.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and secular humanism are affirmed: the self is where healing starts, without religion. Hedonism is tested and found wanting. Traditional family expectation is shown mainly as pressure. Progressive themes are present but quiet: reviewers stress it is a mental-health story first (Otaku USA; Anime Feminist).

**Shifts across the series.** The Japanese table of contents gives the shape: "The Beginning" (ch. 1, which opens at the hotel), "Prequel" (ch. 2: dropout, depression, self-harm, the eating disorder), "Up to the Booking" (ch. 3: the decision, cleaning up), "The Day" (ch. 4: the encounter), "Aftermath" (ch. 5: what changed, a later visit, publishing), and an "Extra" (National Diet Library; scene placement from reviews, so "likely"). The 2023 English hardcover adds a new bonus chapter whose content was not found.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kabi thought one experience would make her "normal." Why do people hope a single event will fix them?
2. What is the difference between being touched and being cared for?
3. How can wanting a parent's approval help a person, and how can it hurt?
4. The author later said her book made her parents cry, and she regretted how she portrayed them. What do we owe the people in our own stories?
5. Your family's tradition has its own teaching on sexuality, the body and despair. Where does this book agree with it, and where does it not?
