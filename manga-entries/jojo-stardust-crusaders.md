**Premise.** In 1988, the Japanese high-school delinquent Jotaro Kujo locks himself in a jail cell, convinced he is possessed. His grandfather Joseph Joestar and the Egyptian fortune-teller Mohammed Avdol explain that it is a "Stand," a fighting spirit, awakened because Dio Brando has returned: a century after Part 1, Dio survived by taking the body of Jotaro's great-great-grandfather Jonathan (Wikipedia). When Jotaro's mother Holly falls ill because her own Stand is killing her, Jotaro, Joseph, Avdol and the student Noriaki Kakyoin, later joined by the Frenchman Jean Pierre Polnareff (who seeks his sister's killer) and the dog Iggy, set out for Egypt to kill Dio within fifty days (Wikipedia). Araki made it a "road movie" inspired by Around the World in Eighty Days rather than a tournament (Wikipedia). The route runs through Hong Kong, Singapore, India, Pakistan, the Arabian desert, the Red Sea and Egypt, and every stop brings an assassin whose Stand is named after a tarot card or an Egyptian god (jojowiki). In Dio's mansion in Cairo, Avdol and Iggy die shielding Polnareff from Vanilla Ice, Kakyoin dies discovering that Dio's Stand stops time, and Joseph is killed and drained. Jotaro learns to stop time himself, splits Dio's Stand in two, revives Joseph with Dio's blood, and burns Dio's body in the sunrise. Holly recovers and the survivors go home (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life protects its family and friends, faces evil without flinching, and is ready to give everything for others; the bloodline's "heritage for the future" is courage passed on, not power.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Kin selection drives the plot: Jotaro and Joseph cross half the world to save Holly, mother and daughter (Wikipedia). Dio is a parasite on the Joestar line, literally living in Jonathan's body. The group survives as a cooperating band of non-kin who die for one another (Avdol, Iggy, Kakyoin). |
| Pragmatic | Each fight is a puzzle: a narrow power, a trick, a counter. Reviewers praise the fights as "creative" (Silverman, via Wikipedia) and "powerful, wily, dangerous" (Comic Book Bin). Knowledge wins the last fight: Kakyoin's dying message reveals the time stop (Wikipedia). |
| Religious | No church or scripture guides the heroes. The supernatural is occult and mythic: Stands named after tarot cards and Egyptian gods, a fortune-teller hero, a vampire villain, a witch-like mother (Enya), ghosts (Iggy's ghost, ch. 132 [245], jojowiki), gambling for souls (D'Arby). The original printings showed fights on buildings that "look like mosques," later redrawn (ANN 2008; Wikipedia). |
| Socio-cultural | A late-1980s Japanese view of Asia and the Middle East as an adventure route. Araki drew Avdol's "ethnic" design to tie him to Egypt and the era's interest in the "birthplaces of civilization" (Wikipedia). ANN's anime review calls the cast "an amalgam of superhero, rock star, and racial stereotype" with "an ignorant and infectious glee"; a MyAnimeList reviewer jokes that the author seems to have "a personal grudge against" India. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Dio is destroyed and Holly is saved, but Avdol, Iggy and Kakyoin die on the way and the survivors part as a smaller band.
Hope source: P — the way through is protecting others: the journey exists to save Jotaro's mother, and Avdol, Iggy and Kakyoin each give their lives to shield a friend or pass on what will stop Dio.

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, nearly every frame sees a clear moral: friends risk everything to save a mother and destroy a tyrant. The divisions are about the occult and about depiction. Traditions that forbid divination (Orthodox Jewish, Jehovah's Witness, Pentecostal, many Muslim and Sikh readers) weigh the tarot names, Egyptian gods and a fortune-teller hero; Wiccan and Pagan readers see the same names as respectful or neutral. Muslim readers have the most specific history with this Part: the mosque-like buildings in the original printings and the OVA's Qur'an pages, both answered by an apology and edits. Progressive frames weigh stereotyped portraits of Asia and the Middle East and the treatment of women. Traditions that guard the senses (Baptist, Adventist, Orthodox Jewish, Muslim) weigh the shower scene, the gore and the toilet humor.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987; Stardust Crusaders is Part 3 (Wikipedia).
- On structure (Wikipedia): he first planned JoJo as a trilogy, avoided the tournament format popular in Jump, and made Part 3 a "road movie" inspired by Around the World in Eighty Days. Joseph's return was "playing it by ear."
- On design (Wikipedia): Jotaro was modelled on Clint Eastwood; Stand designs draw on indigenous clothing, masks and dolls fused with biological or mechanical elements; Avdol's "ethnic" design was meant to tie him to Egypt, reflecting interest in the "birthplaces of civilization." He later regretted not giving Avdol a larger role.
- **JoJonium afterwords (2014-2015, translated on jojowiki).** On Jotaro: "My image of a hero is that of a loner... my idea of a hero is someone who is an unappreciated symbol of justice. There are times where taking the correct path leads to loneliness." On Avdol: his "ethnic" design "was a product of the times," and he took Avdol out for a while in India "to inject a little reality in there with having someone get sacrificed every once in a while." On Enya's Stand, Justice: "'Justice' is a subjective thing, after all." He calls horror films his teacher and says Enya was built from the horror-film "scary old woman." He planned from the start for Kakyoin and Polnareff to change sides, and kept Hol Horse an enemy partly because he loves Westerns.
- **On women and Anne (Billboard Japan, August 2021, via jojowiki).** Araki said he "wasn't able to include any women" in the group because "it didn't feel right to have pretty girls in life-or-death fistfights," and that 1980s Jump readers would have "outright rejected" female characters. Of Anne: "I included her, but then I retired her... With the long and difficult journey ahead of them, I had to change it back to being all men." He said the same in JOJO magazine 2026 Spring (p. 267, cited by Japanese Wikipedia). Anne was unnamed in the manga ("runaway girl"); Araki named her for the 2014 anime (jojowiki; Japanese Wikipedia). No statement by Araki on the shower scene was found.
- **On smoking.** Araki swapped Hol Horse's cigarette for a no-smoking pipe because he had come to dislike tobacco (Japanese vol. 23 jacket column, via Japanese Wikipedia).
- **2008 controversy.** In the 2001 OVA episode "The Mist of Vengeance," Dio vows to kill Jotaro while looking at pages copied from the Qur'an (Surat Ar-Ra'd); the manga's scene showed only "unintelligible scrawls" (ANN, May 22, 2008). The Japan Times reported "an outcry in the Muslim world." Sheikh Abdul Hamid Al-Atrash, chairman of the Fatwa Committee at Al-Azhar, said: "The scene depicts Muslims as terrorists" (ANN). ANN reports that both the anime and the manga showed "buildings that look like mosques" in fight scenes. Shueisha and A.P.P.P. said the staff could not read Arabic, that the manga's author did not know of the Qur'an pages, and that the setting was fantasy, not a depiction of Islam; they offered a "sincere apology to Muslims," halted shipments of the manga and OVA, and promised not to print new copies without corrections (ANN; The Japan Times). Shueisha had Araki redraw the mosque panels; they changed in Viz's paperback vol. 15 and later Japanese printings, and Viz paused the English run for about a year, resuming in April 2009 (Wikipedia). The revised Japanese edition carried a "Kaiteiban" band (ANN, January 27, 2009).
- **Which panels changed.** The Censorship Wiki says the reprints, which the TV anime follows, changed the Cairo scenery: in the Kakyoin vs. DIO fight a minaret became a steel tower, a mosque tower destroyed in the fight became a generic tower, and a falling minaret top became a billboard (Japanese vol. 27, ch. 134-143; exact chapter to verify). Japanese Wikipedia, citing A.P.P.P. and Shueisha notices, confirms the 2008 halt and the February 2009 corrected reissue. In the manga DIO reads "a generic book" (Censorship Wiki; ANN).
- Other later edits (Wikipedia): animal violence redrawn (a dog decapitation, a mutilated dog), feces scenes removed, real brand names and logos erased.
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened, and no statement by Araki himself on the 2008 controversy was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do we owe the people we love, and what would we give for them? Holly is dying because Dio lives; the only cure is to cross the world and kill him within fifty days (Wikipedia). The story asks how much a son, a father, and friends who met only weeks before will risk.

**The answer the story gives.** Everything, if needed. Araki's own image of the hero is "an unappreciated symbol of justice" for whom "taking the correct path leads to loneliness" (JoJonium afterword). Avdol and Iggy die shielding Polnareff; Kakyoin uses his last strength to tell Joseph the secret of Dio's power (Wikipedia). Their deaths are what make victory possible: Jotaro wins because others paid for the knowledge and time he needed. The serial title, "Heritage for the Future," names what is passed on: not a power, but the courage to use it for others.

**Characters as arguments.** Jotaro argues for quiet strength: few words, steady action, a delinquent's manner hiding care for his mother. Dio argues that power is freedom from fear, and he gathers followers who serve him out of fear, money or awe; the story destroys him and frees them. Polnareff argues for revenge, and the story grants it (J. Geil dies, ch. 32), then asks him to become loyal rather than only avenging. Avdol argues for wisdom and sacrifice. Iggy, the selfish dog, argues that even the least loyal can learn to die for a friend. Enya, a grieving mother turned killer, mirrors Holly's family from the other side: love for a son that serves evil.

**Symbols and motifs.** Tarot cards: each assassin a card, fate as a deck that the heroes outplay. Egyptian gods: the journey toward an ancient land where Dio waits. The sun: Dio is a creature of the night and ends in the sunrise. Time: the fifty-day limit, and the time stop that decides the last fight. The star birthmark: the Joestar line marked across generations.

**Traditions it echoes.** The road movie and Around the World in Eighty Days (Araki, via Wikipedia); the Western, through Clint Eastwood's influence on Jotaro (Wikipedia); the vampire tale; the 1980s adventure film set in exotic lands (my own framing; to verify). Reviewers place it among adventure, horror and "occult-history" (Comic Book Bin).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue and loyalty are affirmed. Domination by fear is critiqued. Occult imagery is a source of names and spectacle rather than a belief the story asks readers to hold. Family lineage is honored. Cultures along the route are mostly scenery; reviewers describe stereotype played with "ignorant and infectious glee" (ANN anime review).

**Shifts across the series.** The early chapters (1-20s) are one-off ambushes on the road out of Japan, with Anne's stowaway subplot (ch. 14-41 [127-154], on and off) and the shower scene that carries this entry's content warning (ch. 18 [131]). India and Pakistan (about ch. 26-50) bring J. Geil's death, Enya and the most disgusting episodes (Silverman). Egypt (from about ch. 70) brings Iggy and the god-named assassins. The Cairo chapters (about 109-152) turn to loss: Avdol, Iggy and Kakyoin die, and the long final fight with Dio closes the Part.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Avdol, Iggy and Kakyoin all die for others. Why does the story make victory depend on their sacrifice?
2. Polnareff gets his revenge. Does the story treat revenge as justice? How would your family's tradition answer?
3. Dio says fear is what people want to escape, and offers them power. Why do people follow him?
4. The heroes travel through India, Pakistan, the Arabian desert and Egypt. How are the people of those places shown, and how might readers from those places feel?
5. Shueisha redrew scenes after Muslim readers objected to a related anime. Is changing a book after publication respectful, or a loss? Why?
