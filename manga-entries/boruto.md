**Premise.** Years after *Naruto*, the Hidden Leaf Village is at peace and modernizing, with trains, screens and mass-produced "scientific ninja tools." Naruto is now the Seventh Hokage, and his son Boruto resents a father who is always at work. Boruto trains under Sasuke, forms a new Team 7 with Sarada (Sasuke and Sakura's daughter) and Mitsuki (a synthetic child of Orochimaru), and is branded with "Karma," a mark that lets the alien Otsutsuki clan, who harvest worlds' chakra, reincarnate through a host. The Uzumaki family adopts Kawaki, an escapee from the Kara organization who was sold by his father and experimented on. Kawaki's devotion to Naruto grows into an obsession. In the final arc of Part I, Kawaki seals Naruto and Hinata away, and a cyborg's power rewrites everyone's memory so the village believes Boruto is the outsider who killed his parents. *Two Blue Vortex* follows a 16-year-old Boruto, a fugitive, fighting human-like "Divine Trees" that devour people. The opening page of chapter 1 shows a grown Boruto and Kawaki facing off over a ruined village.

## Life message

**A good life is one where you protect the people you love, carry your parents' legacy without being trapped by it, and keep your own sense of who you are even when everyone else sees you wrongly.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Family is central: a son competes for a busy father's attention, and an adopted son competes with the biological son for the same father, then removes the parents to "protect" them. Inherited eyes (Sharingan, Byakugan, Boruto's Jogan) pass power through bloodlines. The Otsutsuki are parasites who use other bodies to reproduce themselves. |
| Pragmatic | Peace has made ninja into a profession and an industry. Technology (scientific ninja tools, cyborg bodies) is a shortcut the story treats as useful but risky. Fights are won by planning and teamwork, and by knowing an enemy's limits. Power is taken away from the old heroes (Kurama dies, Sasuke loses his Rinnegan) so the young must solve problems themselves. |
| Religious | Japanese and Buddhist terms are used as fantasy mechanics. "Karma" names a curse mark, not a moral law. The Otsutsuki are god-like beings, but the story shows them as alien predators, not gods to worship. The Divine Tree (Shinju) and its human-like offshoots echo sacred trees in Japanese tradition. No real religion is taught. |
| Socio-cultural | It reflects a Japan of long work hours and modernization. Anime Feminist (2018, anime) reads Naruto's overwork and Hinata's role at home as mirrors of Japanese work culture and gender roles. It also asks what heroes do when the war is over and their children did not live through it. Wikipedia notes parallels to the post-Cold War era and a fragile peace. |

**Where traditions disagree.** On the occult-styled mechanics (possession, Karma, devouring trees): Pentecostal, Orthodox Christian, Jehovah's Witness and Adventist families weigh them most; Anglican, Lutheran, Shinto and many evangelical families least. On the cover art: Muslim, Orthodox Jewish, Latter-day Saint and many Christian families treat sexualized images of children as a strong depiction concern, and secular progressive readers raise it as sexualization of minors; Satanist and secular humanist guides focus on harm rather than modesty. On family: conservative and Latter-day Saint families may welcome the family-centered story and traditional roles, while Anime Feminist and secular progressive readers fault Hinata's and Temari's roles. On violence: Anabaptist and Jehovah's Witness families weigh it most.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The story sides with people who protect others without controlling them: Boruto, who keeps his identity and protects his family, is vindicated by the reader's knowledge, while those who seize bodies, worlds or people (the Otsutsuki, Kara, and in a different way Kawaki) lose or are shown as dangerous.

## Creator and publisher context

- **Kishimoto.** Wikipedia (Boruto Uzumaki) reports he created Boruto because he wanted Naruto to be a father by the end of *Naruto*, and wanted Boruto to resemble his father with clear differences. At a 2024 convention in France he said, "I wouldn't have done 'Boruto' if Ikemoto-san hadn't agreed to take over" (via SlashFilm, citing CBR). In August 2024 he said "Ikemoto-sensei is really the one who is fully in charge of *Boruto*… I just enjoy reading it!" and that he wrote only broad outlines at first (via ComicBook.com, quoting a social-media translation; original interview not opened). TV Tropes says the opening flash-forward was Kishimoto's idea; Wikipedia (Kawaki) says Kishimoto suggested introducing Kawaki through it.
- **Kodachi.** Wrote vols. 1-13 and supervised anime episodes 1-216. Announcing his exit on Twitter (ComicBook.com, November 16, 2020), he wrote that "the baton of *Boruto* has finally been returned to Kishimoto-sensei and Ikemoto-sensei!" No reason beyond that was given in the article.
- **Ikemoto.** Calls Boruto his "most relatable character" because both follow a very strong father (Wikipedia). Says the plot now goes beyond Kishimoto's original outline and that he plans with his editor monthly (via ComicBook.com, 2024).
- No statements on religion or politics by any of the three were found. Their private lives are not covered here.
- Publisher: Shueisha (Hitotsubashi Group). VIZ Media publishes in English; VIZ is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). The manga moved from weekly to monthly serialization in 2019.
- Controversies: no widely reported controversies about the creators personally were found. The repeated fan objections to Ikemoto's covers of Sarada and Sumire are covered under How readers took it.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a child owe a famous parent, and who are you when everyone else decides who you are? *Boruto* opens with the son of a living legend who is angry that his father belongs to the whole village. It ends its first part with that son erased from his own identity: through a cyborg's power, the whole world remembers Kawaki as Naruto's son and Boruto as the outsider who killed his parents.

**The answer the story gives.** You owe your parents respect and you owe yourself your own path. Boruto learns to admire Naruto after seeing what his father does to protect the village (vols. 2-3). He then refuses to become a copy of him. He trains under Sasuke, keeps protecting people who now hunt him, and does not try to win back his name by force. The story suggests that identity rests on what you do and on the few people who know you, not on what the crowd believes.

**Characters as arguments.** Kawaki is the sharpest argument. He is a child his father sold and Kara experimented on. Naruto's family takes him in, and he comes to love Naruto so much that he will seal Naruto away or kill Boruto to keep him safe. The story treats his love as real and his methods as dangerous. The Otsutsuki argue that higher beings may harvest lesser ones; the story condemns them. Amado argues that science may do anything for a good end, and is kept as a useful but untrusted ally. Code argues for loyalty to a dead master. Eida, whom everyone loves because of her power, asks whether love produced by force is love at all.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Karma mark stands for an inheritance a child did not choose: Momoshiki's will lives in Boruto's body. Eyes again carry power: Boruto's right eye, the Jogan, is slashed by Kawaki (ch. 80). Ninja tools stand for shortcuts; Boruto's early cheating with one is punished. The Divine Tree, which turns people into copies of themselves, is the final image of identity taken over. The opening flash-forward, a grown Boruto and Kawaki over a ruined village, shapes how every later chapter reads.

**Traditions it echoes.** The father-son rivalry and filial respect echo Confucian values. Words like "karma" and "chakra" come from Indian religion, and a sacred tree recalls Shinto practice, but both are used as game mechanics. The fear of a peace that depends on a few heroes echoes post-war and post-Cold War worries, as Wikipedia notes. The worries about technology versus craft are common to Japanese stories about modernization.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Family loyalty and virtue are presented as right. Transhumanist ambition, rule by power, and the control of others' bodies and minds are presented as wrong. Utilitarian choices are tragic, as in *Naruto*, but here they are made by a young protagonist's foil rather than by a state. Religion is borrowed imagery. Commentators have criticized the adult characters' gender roles (Anime Feminist) and the cover art of girls (ComicBook.com, Sportskeeda), while CSM notes women in leadership and combat roles.

**Shifts across the series.** The early volumes retell the film and are lighter, with school life and a father-son quarrel. From Kawaki's arrival (vol. 7) the story darkens. After Kishimoto took over the writing (vol. 14), the old heroes lose their power: Kurama dies (ch. 55) and Sasuke loses his Rinnegan. *Two Blue Vortex* is colder: The Outerhaven describes a "colder and older" Boruto, and the enemies devour people. Fan reception improved with the shift; MAL scores rose by about one point.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Boruto is angry that his father works all the time. Was Naruto wrong, or was Boruto?
2. Kawaki seals Naruto and Hinata away to protect them. Can love become controlling? How would you tell?
3. If everyone believed something false about you, what would you do? Who would you trust?
4. Boruto cheats with a ninja tool and is disqualified. When is a new tool a shortcut, and when is it progress?
5. Is it fair that the old heroes lose their powers so the young ones must step up?
