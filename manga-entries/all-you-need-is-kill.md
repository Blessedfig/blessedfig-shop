**Executive summary.** A short, tightly plotted military science-fiction manga: a raw recruit killed on his first day of battle against alien "Mimics" wakes up on the morning before, again and again, and turns hundreds of deaths into training until he can fight beside the world's best soldier, Rita Vrataski, the only other person who has lived through the loop. The overall content level is 7 out of 10, set by graphic battlefield violence (a severed arm and impalement in the opening pages, per Angry Anime Bitches) and by the hero's repeated deaths, including a gunshot to the head he uses to test the loop, played partly as black comedy (TV Tropes). Sexual content is light: a steam-covered shower scene with Rita (an adult, 19), a flirtatious cafeteria worker, and a night together that the manga makes clear is spent talking (TV Tropes). The ending is the most discussed point: to break the loop, one of the two loopers must die, and Keiji kills Rita in a duel she proposes; she dies in his arms. Readers split on whether the ending is moving or forced (Kotaku; Angry Anime Bitches). VIZ rates the English omnibus T+ (Older Teen); one reviewer calls it M, which does not match the press release. No documented controversy was found.

**Premise.** Humanity is losing a war against the Mimics (gitai), alien creatures born from nanomachines meant to terraform Earth (Mangapedia). Keiji Kiriya, a new recruit in the United Defense Force's Japanese unit, wears a powered "Jacket" into his first battle on Kotoiushi Island and is killed almost at once, after killing an unusual Mimic. He wakes in his barracks bed the morning before the battle, with only his memories carried over (Wikipedia; Mangapedia). Loop after loop he trains, learns the battlefield and hardens, while his mind frays. On roughly his 158th loop he meets Rita Vrataski, the American "Full Metal Bitch" who fights with a 200-kilogram battle axe and who once lived through a loop of her own (VIZ press release; Mangapedia). Together they learn that a "server" Mimic resets time. They destroy it, but the loop continues: the two loopers have themselves become like the Mimics' antennae, and one must die. Rita proposes a fight to the death; Keiji wins, she dies in his arms, and he destroys the server and ends the loop (Wikipedia; Honcierge; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life is earned through effort: you fail, learn and try again until you are strong enough to protect others. Love and comradeship give the struggle meaning, but victory may cost the person you love most, and the survivor must carry that cost forward.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Most traditions that accept a just, defensive war read the message as a fair fit with things to discuss: perseverance, sacrifice and love are honored, and war is shown as costly. The dividing lines are the place of violence itself (Anabaptist, Jehovah's Witness, Adventist, Bahá'í, Buddhist and Unitarian Universalist frames rate the fit lower because the only answer the story offers is combat), the suicide test played for laughs (a concern for Christian, Jewish, Muslim and secular-progressive frames alike), and the ending in which a man kills the woman he loves at her request (read as sacrifice by Weber and as duty in Gita terms by Hindu readers, but troubling to frames that hold all killing grave). On depiction, gore drives most concern; fan service matters mainly to Orthodox Jewish, Muslim and Latter-day Saint families.

## Creator and publisher context

- **Hiroshi Sakurazaka (novel):** He took the idea from a video game player's online account of restarting after a character's death and improving by trial and error, and consulted time-loop fiction such as Groundhog Day (Wikipedia). In a 2014 Weekly ASCII interview he said failure and success repeated in games had become part of his life, that seen as a single line into the past this becomes a story, and that the lesson of a story where the hero revives is "when you die, it's over"; repeating success and failure is itself life (paraphrased from Japanese). He also said he deliberately kept a single heroine and one story line partly in reaction to branching visual novels (Weekly ASCII). Japanese Wikipedia reports he names "Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer" as an inspiration and that an editor first doubted the idea would sell.
- **Takeshi Obata (art) and Ryōsuke Takeuchi (storyboards):** No on-record statements about this manga's themes were found. Obata based the designs on Yoshitoshi ABe's novel illustrations (ANN news, November 2013). Statements on religion or politics: none found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- **Publisher:** Shueisha (Weekly Young Jump). VIZ Media published it in English under Shonen Jump Advanced and in digital Weekly Shonen Jump, timed to the Edge of Tomorrow film; editor Mike Montesa called it "tense and engrossing" (press release). VIZ is owned by Shueisha, Shogakukan and Shogakukan-Shueisha Productions (from general knowledge; to verify).
- **Other adaptations:** VIZ also published a separate American graphic novel by Nick Mamatas and Lee Ferguson (ANN news). No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could live the worst day of your life over and over, what would it make of you? Keiji is given endless retries and must decide whether they are a curse or a chance. Sakurazaka says the idea came from games, where players fail and try again until they win, and that the lesson he meant was the opposite of the premise: "when you die, it's over" (Weekly ASCII, paraphrased).

**The answer the story gives.** Effort answers despair. Keiji stops running and starts training, and each death becomes a lesson. But effort alone does not end the loop. The rules demand a final, unrepeatable loss: one of the two loopers must die. The story's answer is that strength is earned, love is real, and the cost is still paid. TV Tropes calls it a "Bittersweet Ending": Keiji wins, Rita dies, and he is left feared and alone.

**Characters as arguments.** Keiji is the argument that an ordinary, frightened person can become extraordinary through practice. Rita is the argument that duty can carry a person who has lost everything; she enlisted under a stolen name to avenge her parents and turned vengeance into protecting others (TV Tropes; Mangapedia). Ferrell is the argument for discipline as care. Rita's old commander, who told comforting stories to dying soldiers, argues for mercy in the middle of war (Mangapedia). The Mimics argue nothing; they are the indifferent force the humans must answer.

**Symbols and motifs.** The morning in the barracks bed, repeated. The battle axe, a weapon that does not run out of ammunition, passed from Rita to Keiji. Coffee and green tea: Rita's coffee recalls her father, and the right answer about green tea tells her a new looper has found her (TV Tropes, "Trust Password"). Rita's red armor, and Keiji's armor painted blue in her memory (Wikipedia, novel). Book ends: Rita comforts a dying Keiji in the first pages, and he holds her as she dies at the end (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** The time-loop story (Groundhog Day, which Sakurazaka consulted, Wikipedia). Video-game "save and retry" logic. The war story of a green recruit who becomes a veteran. The tragic duel between lovers or comrades found in many epics; Hindu readers may hear Arjuna's dilemma. Weber, writing from a Christian view, hears John 15:13 and a longing for a savior.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and pragmatism fit the long middle of training and trial. Utilitarian reasoning governs the ending: one life for many. Existentialist and absurdist notes run through the early loops, where death is pointless and sometimes darkly funny. Christian and other religious readings come only from readers; the text offers no God, afterlife or prayer.

**Shifts across the series.** The first chapters are panic and black comedy as Keiji dies and tests the loop. The middle is a training montage and a partnership, with Rita's backstory chapters (TV Tropes; Angry Anime Bitches). The last chapters turn to tragedy. Reviewers say the first half is slow and the second fast (MyAnimeList review), and some find the ending rushed (Angry Anime Bitches). Compared with the novel, the manga softens Keiji's madness, keeps more of the squad alive and makes the night with Rita clearly chaste (TV Tropes).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Keiji gets hundreds of chances to get one day right. Would endless retries make you braver or more careless? Why?
2. Keiji shoots himself to test the loop, and the story plays it as a joke. How should stories handle suicide? What is different about real life?
3. Rita proposes that they fight and one of them die. Was there another way? Was her choice brave, wrong, or both?
4. Sakurazaka says the lesson is "when you die, it's over." How does that idea change the way you read the story?
5. Rita tells comforting stories to dying soldiers. Is it kind or dishonest? What would your family do?
