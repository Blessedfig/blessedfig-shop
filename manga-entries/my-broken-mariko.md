**Premise.** Tomoyo Shiino, a hard-smoking, short-tempered office worker in her twenties, learns from a TV news report in a diner that her childhood friend Mariko Ikagawa, 26, has died by suicide. Since school, Shiino had watched Mariko suffer beatings and sexual abuse from her father, then a string of violent boyfriends; Mariko depended on her completely. Convinced that Mariko's father has no right to her remains, Shiino skips work, breaks into his home with a kitchen knife and runs off with the urn of ashes, meaning to take Mariko to Marigaoka Cape, a seaside place Mariko once said she wanted to see. On the way she is robbed of everything but the urn, is helped by a quiet stranger, Makio, nearly throws herself from a cliff, and then rescues a girl being chased by a man, swinging the urn at him so that the ashes scatter. She goes home, where a last letter from Mariko is waiting (Wikipedia; Kleefeld; Business Standard; But Why Tho; AniList reviews).

## Life message

**Grief and guilt after a suicide cannot be solved, only lived through: the one thing the living can do for the dead is go on living, and the love of one true friend matters even when it could not save her.**

| Pragmatic | Shiino's plan is reckless (knife, theft, no money), and the trip goes wrong at each step; what helps is a stranger's cash and plain advice. Bleeding Cool notes no one holds the father to account and no system steps in. |

The core question for faith readers is the sacred handling of the dead (Shiino takes the remains from the father and scatters them by accident) and the treatment of suicide, met here with grief and the charge to keep living. Beneath the Tangles reviews it without a faith reading: "This is not a story of redemption, but rather about death and coping." |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of social indifference to abuse: diners ignore the news, Shiino's boss demands she work, teachers ignore a girl's bruises (AniList review). Outlook: Bittersweet — Mariko's death and her father's abuse are never undone or punished, but Shiino chooses to live, saves a stranger, lets her friend go, and ends with a letter whose tone the author meant to leave room for hope.

## Creator and publisher context

- Waka Hirako is a Japanese manga artist. She drew fan art on pixiv before an editor contacted her, won a "Step Up" prize at the MF Comic Award, and spent about four years getting "YISKA" published. She names as influences Natsuki Takaya's Fruits Basket, Moto Hagio, Takehiko Inoue's Vagabond, Hayao Miyazaki, European bande dessinée and small-theater films, and the absurdity of French cinema; Xavier Dolan's It's Only the End of the World showed her "it's allowed to end like this" (Real Sound, 2020, part 1).
- On-record intent: the story grew from her frustration over real child-abuse cases in the news and from a survivor who told her about their past; she felt "a frustration I could do nothing about" (自分にはどうにもできなかった歯がゆさ). It began as an image of "a woman traveling with a set of remains," drafted in one flow as four episodes (Real Sound, part 1). In a 2021 dialogue with novelist Akira Otani she said it "wasn't a story I set out to draw" but a release valve after a stalled project (Real Sound, 2021).
- On the story: it is "Shiino's story," about her inner conflict, "not a mystery"; she cut investigative scenes from the first draft of chapter 4. She did not want Mariko to be a "caricatured" character and dislikes the label "mentally unstable." She chose not to show the letter's text, and left Shiino's reply ("...yeah") open to a hopeful reading. Readers with experiences like Mariko's wrote to her, which made her feel "truly rewarded"; to them she says, "Thank you for staying alive" (生きていてくださってありがとう) (Real Sound, part 2).
- On romance: Okazu reports she said readers should decide; no direct statement was found in sources opened.
- No statements about religion or politics found. Personal life is private, and nothing beyond the above is recorded here.
- Publisher: Kadokawa (Comic Bridge; Japanese volume); Yen Press in English (Yen Press is a joint venture of Kadokawa and Hachette; from general knowledge; to verify). No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do the living owe the dead, and what do they owe themselves, after a suicide? Shiino learns of Mariko's death from a TV in a diner. Everything that follows is her attempt to do something, anything, for a friend she could not save.

**The answer the story gives.** Not justice, which never comes, and not understanding, which Hirako deliberately withholds: she cut the scenes that explained Mariko's last days because "this is Shiino's story" (Real Sound). The answer is to keep living. A stranger, Makio, tells Shiino at her lowest point that the only way to see the person who is gone is to go on living (AniList review). The book closes with a letter from Mariko whose words are not shown, and with Shiino's quiet "...yeah," which Hirako leaves open to a hopeful reading.

**Characters as arguments.** Shiino is the argument for loyalty without limits; she takes the ashes at knifepoint because no one else will honor Mariko. Kleefeld argues she is as "broken" as Mariko. Mariko is not a saint: she clings, threatens and seeks pity (AniList review), and Hirako refused to make her a caricature. The father argues nothing; he is the force that broke her, and the story gives him no voice or redemption. Makio, who once tried to die himself, is the argument that survival is possible. Kyoko, the father's partner, hints that abuse spreads beyond one victim.

**Symbols and motifs.** The urn, carried like a child and finally broken open in an act of rescue, so that Mariko's ashes scatter as Shiino saves another girl. The sea and Marigaoka Cape (the name echoes Mariko's), the place they promised to visit. Letters: Mariko's old letters, stolen in the robbery, and the last one waiting at home. Cigarettes as Shiino's constant crutch. Windows and frames that box the girls together and apart (AniList review).

**Traditions it echoes.** The road story of carrying the dead home; the josei realism of women's lives under strain; European art cinema's open endings, which Hirako names as an influence. Reviewers compare it to Fleabag and I May Destroy You (Bleeding Cool).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialist and secular humanist lenses fit best: meaning is chosen and found in people. Feminist readers find a strong account of women harmed by men and helping one another; Bleeding Cool wishes it said more about the systems that failed. Religious readers find abuse treated as plainly evil and life chosen over despair, with no faith, forgiveness or justice offered.

**Shifts across the series.** Chapter 1 (likely) is shock, guilt and the theft of the ashes. Chapters 2-3 (likely) are the road: robbery, Makio, drink and memory. Chapter 4 is the cape: despair, rescue, release and return.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shiino could not see that Mariko was about to die. What signs can friends watch for, and whom should they tell?
2. Was Shiino right to take the ashes? What else could she have done?
3. Why does the story never punish Mariko's father? How does that make you feel?
4. What does Makio mean when he says the only way to see the dead is to keep living?
5. Hirako tells readers, "Thank you for staying alive." Who are the people in your life you could say that to?
