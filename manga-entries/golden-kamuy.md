**Premise.** Hokkaido, 1907, just after the Russo-Japanese War. Saichi Sugimoto, a veteran nicknamed "Immortal Sugimoto," hears of a hoard of gold stolen from the Ainu, Japan's Indigenous people of the north. The map to it is tattooed in pieces on the skins of escaped convicts from Abashiri Prison. Sugimoto wants the gold for the widow of a fallen comrade. He teams up with Asirpa, a young Ainu hunter whose father was killed in the robbery. They race against Lt. Tsurumi's rogue 7th Division, who want the gold for a military coup, and Toshizō Hijikata, the old Shinsengumi commander, who wants a second Republic of Ezo. The story mixes survival, hunting and Ainu food and custom with gore, crime-thriller plotting and wild comedy.

## Life message

**A good life is lived by your own hands and promises: survive with skill, respect the animals and land that feed you, keep faith with the dead and the living, and refuse to let greed or other men's wars decide your future.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Survival is the engine: hunting, food, cold, predators. Bears are shown as man-eaters (TV Tropes links this to the 1915 Sankebetsu attack). Kin and clan drive many choices: Asirpa's search for her father, Sugimoto's promise to a dead friend's widow, Ogata's wound as an unwanted son. Romance is minor; Asirpa is a child for most of the series and is not cast as a love interest (Anime Feminist). |
| Pragmatic | Know-how wins: Asirpa's hunting and trapping, Sugimoto's combat skill, Shiraishi's escapes. Factions make and break alliances as needed. Greed and grand political schemes get people killed. The ending keeps the land, not the gold (Japanese Wikipedia). |
| Religious | Ainu animism is central and shown with respect: animals are kamuy visiting the human world, and hunters honor them by using every part (Wikipedia notes "respectful use of natural resources"). There is a seer, Inkarmat, whose prophecies often come true (ch. 122, v13). No Christian or Buddhist framework is foregrounded. |
| Socio-cultural | A study of men discarded by the state: veterans abandoned after two wars (Tsurumi's grievance), samurai displaced by the Meiji Restoration (Hijikata), an Indigenous people losing land and language (Asirpa). Strong Ainu women (Noda in The Japan Times). Critics differ on whether the story faces or softens Japan's colonial history (see How readers took it). |

## Creator and publisher context

- Satoru Noda was born in Hokkaido (Japan Media Arts Festival exhibition bio). His previous series, *Spinamarada!*, was cancelled. He first planned a hunting manga, then added bears, Hijikata, buried-gold legends and the Ainu so as not to run out of material (Pixiv Encyclopedia, opened).
- On the Ainu: Noda chose a bright, entertaining portrayal rather than one centered on oppression, and said Ainu collaborators asked for "strong" Ainu characters rather than pitiable ones (Pixiv Encyclopedia). He told readers that Asirpa's strength is "surprisingly typical" and that the image of Ainu women as dependent is "a prejudice" (The Japan Times, Michael Hoffman). Linguist Hiroshi Nakagawa supervised the Ainu language (Wikipedia).
- On the ending: Noda said Asirpa giving up the gold was fixed from the start, and that the story should end in a state that matches reality (Japanese Wikipedia, opened). Japanese Wikipedia also reports praise for the ending from Ainu Museum staff and the Hokkaido Ainu Association, and a remark by the Chief Cabinet Secretary (details not checked).
- Noda has said he does not find smoking cool, so few characters smoke (Pixiv Encyclopedia).
- No public statements on religion or party politics were found. No reputable reports of personal controversy involving the creator were found. A conversation with Rumiko Takahashi was found but not opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha (Hitotsubashi Group). VIZ Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge). VIZ publishes it under the VIZ Signature line, rated Mature.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who should own the future of a land and a people? The gold was taken from the Ainu. Every faction wants it to build something: a state for soldiers, a samurai republic, an armed uprising. The story asks what wealth does to the people who chase it, and what an Indigenous girl should do with a fortune everyone else wants to spend on war.

**The answer the story gives.** The gold does not save anyone. Its seekers die, vanish or give up. Asirpa decides it should not become a war chest. She and her companions rebury it and keep the land deeds, which she uses to protect forest land that later becomes national parks (Japanese Wikipedia; ch. 311-314). Noda said this choice was fixed from the start. The answer is quiet: keep the land, keep the language, keep living.

**Characters as arguments.** Sugimoto argues that a killer can still keep faith. He kills in war and in self-defense, but he "won't kill in cold blood" (ANN) and carries guilt. Asirpa argues that a tradition survives by changing hands, not by freezing. Tsurumi argues that a betrayed soldier may build his own state by any means; his charm is real and his methods destroy his own men. Hijikata argues for a lost cause with dignity. Ogata argues that guilt is an illusion, and his end refutes him. The convicts (Henmi, Ienaga, Edogai, Anehata) are less arguments than portraits of desire gone wrong, which Hotta (Nippon.com) sees as the core of the work.

**Symbols and motifs.** The tattooed skins: human beings turned into a map of greed. Food: every hunt ends in a meal, and gratitude to the animal closes the loop the gold breaks. Bears: nature as both provider and killer. The train: a runaway machine of the new Japan carrying everyone to the end (ch. 302-313).

**Traditions it echoes.** Ainu oral tales and animism. Westerns and survival films (Beneath the Tangles compares it to *The Revenant*). Samurai drama in Hijikata. War literature on trauma and survivor's guilt. Japanese comedy-horror manga (Anime Feminist compares the tone to *Dorohedoro*).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism and animist respect for nature are shown as wisdom. Virtue ethics lives in Sugimoto's promises. Nationalism and militarism are given sympathetic faces and then shown to fail. Nihilism collapses. Hedonism and perversion are the villains' fuel and the comedy's raw material. Critical readers differ on colonialism: Ito says the anime erases Japan's colonial past; others credit the series with making Ainu culture widely known (Anime Feminist; Number 1 Shimbun).

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-14 are a hunt across Hokkaido for convicts, with arc-length villains. The Abashiri raid (v13-14) turns the story. The Karafuto journey (v15-21) becomes a road story about Asirpa's identity. The Sapporo and Hakodate arcs (v25-30) shift into war. The final volume resolves the gold and the land.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Asirpa refuse to let the gold pay for anyone's war? Do you agree?
2. Sugimoto has killed many people. What makes him a hero anyway, if he is one?
3. Tsurumi's anger at the government has real causes. Where does a fair grievance turn into something wrong?
4. Asirpa thanks the animals she hunts. How does your family think about where food comes from?
5. Some terrible characters are played for laughs. When is it fine to laugh at evil in a story, and when is it not?
