**Premise.** Under ordinary London lies Reverse London, a hidden magical city that the final sign of the one-shot names as the "West Branch" of Soul Society, the afterlife realm of Bleach. Dragons live unseen among Londoners and cause deaths and disasters. Noel Niihashi and Ninny Spangcole are young witches in Wing Bind, the agency that pastures, protects and, when needed, fights dragons. Ninny is also a singer in the idol group Cecile Die Twice. Noel and Ninny work mainly for pay and promotion points. They are assigned to look after Balgo Parks, a lazy, lecherous classmate who can see dragons and who becomes a "Dragonclad" after his dog-like dragon Osushi bites him. In season 1, Ninny's former bandmate Macy Baljure turns out to secretly own a dragon, Elly, which is really "Cinderella," one of the seven fairy-tale-named "Märchen" Dark Dragons. Wing Bind's enforcement arm marks Balgo for death as a "Punitive Dragon." The arc ends with Cinderella killed, Balgo's death sentence lifted and Macy placed in the witches' care.

## Life message

**A good life is honest work done with a partner you trust. Real power is earned rather than wished for, and wanting to be "special" through borrowed magic leads to disaster.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Status competition drives most characters: Ninny wants rank and promotion, Macy wants the fame Ninny has, and Balgo's lechery is played as crude mating display that Noel answers with force. Cooperation between two unrelated partners, not kin, is what keeps them alive. There is no family or romance plot beyond Noel's embrace of Balgo at the end (ch. 4). |
| Pragmatic | Noel and Ninny are paid professionals who track pay and "points," refuse unpaid overtime, and finish the job anyway. The punch line of the arc is that saving London earns them five pounds and five points each (ch. 4). Rules exist to manage a real danger: unsanctioned contact with dragons is "punishable by death" (ANN). |
| Religious | Witches and wizards are a civil service, not a religion; magic works through wand-like guns ("Witch Kits"). The link to Soul Society places the story inside Bleach's afterlife cosmology, but no gods, worship or prayer appear in the sources. Fairy tales are recast as dangerous dragons, and Ninny calls them nonsense. Traditions that teach against witchcraft will notice that the heroines are called witches, even though nothing occult is practiced in a real-world sense. |
| Socio-cultural | A workplace comedy about two young women in a bureaucracy, with a satirical edge about idol fame, tabloid newspapers ("The Realist" prints false stories about Ninny) and an opaque enforcement agency (the Inks). The male lead is a running panty-peeking gag, which reviewers count against the series (Manga Test Drive; ANN). Death is mostly offstage; the one personal loss is Balgo's childhood friend Shelby, killed in a train accident and impersonated by a Dark Dragon (one-shot). |

Outlook: Hopeful — the death sentence is lifted, the rival who caused the disaster is taken in rather than punished, and the two partners end the arc stronger together, though the system they serve stays cold and stingy.
Hope source: F — the way through is the Noel and Ninny partnership and their willingness to take in and shield others (Balgo, then Macy) when their own agency treats them as threats.

## Creator and publisher context

- Tite Kubo is the creator of Bleach (2001-2016). Burn the Witch is set in the Bleach universe; the one-shot ends on a sign reading "Soul Society: West Branch" (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).
- On-record statement: Kubo took the title from Radiohead's 2016 song "Burn the Witch" because he liked its sound, and said in a 2018 interview that "the content of the song and the content of the story are completely unrelated" (Wikipedia, opened). No statement on religion, politics or intended message was found in the pages opened.
- The series has been on hiatus since 2020, with a second season announced but undated (Wikipedia; MyAnimeList). Kubo later worked on the Bleach: Thousand-Year Blood War anime (MyAnimeList reviewer complaint; from general knowledge; to verify).
- No reputable reports of controversy involving the creator were found in the pages opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media, the English publisher, is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). Anime: Studio Colorido (film), distributed by Shochiku; Crunchyroll streamed it.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you make yourself special by wishing, or only by work? Reverse London is a city where fairy tales are real and dangerous. Its residents can stumble into power they did not earn, and that power can turn on them.

**The answer the story gives.** Earned beats borrowed. Macy, a former idol who feels eclipsed by Ninny, bonds with a dragon that makes her feel special (ch. 3). The dragon is Cinderella, a Märchen, and its awakening destroys part of the city (ch. 4). When the magic fades, Ninny tells Macy it was never hers. Noel offers the quieter answer: "We don't need to be powerful." The heroines are not chosen ones. They are civil servants who argue about overtime and get paid five pounds for saving the city.

**Characters as arguments.** Noel is the argument for humble competence: deadpan, paid, reliable. Ninny is ambition that has been earned, and her temper and hunger for rank are shown with a wink. Macy is the argument against envy and wish-fulfillment, drawn with sympathy: critics at ANN called her motivation "the most human" in Kubo's recent work. Balgo is a mix of crude comedy and sudden courage; he shields Ninny from the Shelby dragon (one-shot) and his energy sword stops Cinderella (ch. 4). Bruno and the Inks stand for an institution that sentences first and explains later.

**Symbols and motifs.** The title, from a Radiohead song, evokes witch hunts, but here the witches are the authorities and the hunted are teenagers bitten by dragons. Fairy tales are the monsters. Witch Kits are guns. The newspaper "The Realist" prints lies. The giant idol billboards show fame as something sold.

**Traditions it echoes.** European fairy tales (Cinderella), British urban fantasy, the "magic ministry" of school-wizard stories, and Bleach's own Soul Society. Workplace comedy and idol-industry satire are Japanese pop-culture forms.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism and virtue ethics are affirmed in the partners. Expressive individualism, the wish to be special, is critiqued through Macy. Authority is explored: the agency's rules are harsh, unexplained and reversible. Feminist readers find two capable leads and a harassment gag played for laughs. Religious readers divide on whether "witch" heroines in a fantasy bureaucracy raise concerns.

**Shifts across the series.** The one-shot is a self-contained job story with a personal tragedy (Shelby). Season 1 widens to institutions (the Inks, the press) and to a villain driven by envy. The arc ends unresolved for the larger world; the second season has not appeared.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Macy wanted to feel special. What made her dragon dangerous?
2. Noel says, "We don't need to be powerful." Do you agree?
3. Balgo's attempts to see under Noel's skirt are played as a joke. How would it feel if it happened to someone you know?
4. Wing Bind sentenced Balgo to death without a hearing, then pardoned him. Is that justice?
5. The heroes are called witches. Does a word mean the same thing in a fantasy world as in your family's tradition?
