**Executive summary.** A 24-chapter, single-volume (in English) horror adaptation of Osamu Dazai's 1948 novel about Yozo Oba, a man who hides his fear of people behind clowning and slides through drink, women, morphine, failed suicides and an asylum. The overall content level is 10 out of 10, set by Mental health and self-harm (several suicides and suicide attempts drawn in detail, including the author Dazai's own drowning, which frames the book) and by the depicted childhood sexual abuse. Viz rates it M (Mature). Ito adds deaths the novel does not have (a classmate's throat-cutting suicide, a stabbing), ghosts and body horror, and a meeting between Yozo and Dazai; the ending is darker than the novel's. Critics praise it highly (Library Journal: "might be Ito's magnum opus"); The Comics Journal calls it more misogynistic than the novel, and one MyAnimeList reviewer calls it "a monument on how not to depict depression." Most traditions read it as a portrait of sin, shame or despair that shows the consequences but offers no way out.

**Premise.** The book opens on a rainy night as a man and a woman tie themselves together and jump into the Tamagawa Aqueduct (Solrad; Booklive reader review), a retelling of Dazai's real death in June 1948. The main story is Yozo Oba's notebooks. The youngest son of a rich, cold landowner in northern Japan, he cannot understand what other people feel and is terrified of them, so he plays the clown. As a child he is sexually abused by servants. At school a plain classmate, Takeichi, sees through his act. In Tokyo the painter Horiki leads him into drinking, prostitutes and underground Communist meetings. He survives a double suicide in which the café waitress Tsuneko dies, lives off a series of women, marries the trusting young Yoshiko, and falls apart after she is sexually assaulted. Morphine addiction and a stay in a mental hospital follow, where Ito has him meet Dazai, who decides to write his story. Ito then carries Yozo about twelve years past the novel's ending into an abusive marriage, catatonic and looking ninety at thirty-nine (TV Tropes; CBR).

## Life message

**The book promotes no way of life. It shows a man who cannot be honest with others or himself, and whose fear, pleasures and lies destroy him and the people who love him; its last words are "Now I have neither happiness nor unhappiness. Everything passes" (USC Scribe, quoting the Viz text, p. 610).**

| Pragmatic | Every escape (clowning, drink, women, activism, drugs) works briefly and then costs more than it gave. | Religious | Dazai read the Bible seriously from about 1935, and a "strong Christian worldview" runs through his late works, though he was not a Christian (Nagahama, Handbook of Japanese Christian Writers). The novel's shame, sin and fear of judgment can be read through that lens; whether Ito keeps the novel's Bible references is not confirmed (to verify). Ito adds ghosts: Takeichi haunts Yozo after his suicide (The Comics Journal), and a son is implied to be the reincarnation of a boy who haunts him (TV Tropes). | Socio-cultural | The fear of seken, "the world" or "society" and its judging gaze, drives Yozo; a rich landowning family, prewar student Marxism, cafés and red-light districts place it in early Showa Japan. Outlook: Bleak — the story ends with its narrator catatonic, abused and empty, and with the author who heard his story drowning himself, offering only that "everything passes."
Hope source: N — no person, faith or effort rescues Yozo; the brief respite of his talk with Dazai (Rue Morgue: "a moment of much-needed respite") leads to Dazai's own suicide, and the book closes on neither happiness nor unhappiness.

## Creator and publisher context

- Junji Ito (born 1963) is a horror manga artist best known for Tomie and Uzumaki (from general knowledge). He was inducted into the Eisner Hall of Fame in July 2025, and in a 2025 Bungei Shunju essay said "My character was formed by Kazuo Umezu's manga" (Bunshun, opened).
- On this work (ANN interview, September 2019, opened): "The editor listed out the titles for me, and No Longer Human from Osamu Dazai popped out, which I hadn't read it before." "I thought a classical literature story from Japan would fit better." "After reading it, I could feel sympathy with the main characters, much like in my stories."
- Mangapedia summarizes a 2018 Kokosho Kojitsu (好書好日, Asahi) interview: his editor proposed a literary adaptation; Ningen Shikkaku fit best; he identified with Yozo hiding his true feelings, saying adolescence "was especially bad, and I often told jokes to make myself look cheerful" (思春期の時期が特にひどくて、自分を明るく見せようとよく冗談を言ったりしていました); he "expanded the original's human drama and added horror elements"; he drew on the ghost-story rakugo of San'yutei Encho (Mangapedia, opened; interview not opened).
- Viz released a video interview in which Ito discusses how he came to adapt it, his history with Dazai, his changes and what he hopes readers take away (ComicBook.com, opened; video not watched).
- Translator Jocelyne Allen says the adaptation starts "very faithful to Dazai's novel before gradually diverging" (Viz blog, opened).
- Ito has not spoken publicly about religion or politics as far as found. No controversy involving Ito personally was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to be "qualified" as a human being? The Japanese title, Ningen Shikkaku, literally "disqualified as a human," frames Yozo's life as a failed test. He cannot understand what others feel, cannot say what he feels, and survives by acting. The book asks whether a person who has never been honest with anyone has lived a human life at all.

**The answer the story gives.** The story gives no answer that rescues Yozo. Each escape fails: clowning, the Communist meetings, the double suicide, marriage, morphine. The novel ended with a bar madam remembering him kindly; Ito's version goes about twelve years further, into an abusive marriage and catatonia, and closes on the line "Now I have neither happiness nor unhappiness. Everything passes" (USC Scribe; TV Tropes). Ito adds that Dazai, after hearing the story, loses hope and drowns himself, which turns the opening scene into the ending (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Yozo is the mask. Takeichi is honesty: he alone sees the act, and in Ito's version Yozo's lie drives him to suicide and he returns as a ghost (TCJ; USC). Yoshiko is trust: her assault teaches Yozo that trust cannot survive the world (EBSCO). Horiki is the hypocrisy of "the world," untouched by consequences. The women who shelter Yozo carry love and forgiveness, and critics disagree whether Ito honors them or reduces them to tropes (Comics Beat; TCJ). Dazai, as a character, is the artist who turns this despair into a book, and Ito makes him its last victim.

**Symbols and motifs.** The clown's mask. Water: the Tamagawa Aqueduct at the start and end, and the double-suicide drowning. Insects for the activists (Solrad; Rue Morgue). Faces that melt or swell under fear (Rue Morgue). The pharmacy's wall of poisonous plants (USC; TCJ; Viz). Yozo's self-portraits as monsters, and the son who may be a reborn ghost (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese I-novel and Dazai's own life (Wikipedia; Bleeding Cool). Western existentialism, Dostoevsky and Kafka (Library Journal). Christian themes of sin, shame and judgment in Dazai's source, from his reading of the Bible (Nagahama). Buddhist impermanence in the final line (my own framing). Japanese ghost tradition, through Ito's use of San'yutei Encho's rakugo ghost stories (Mangapedia summary of Ito's interview).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and nihilism are explored through the narrator's voice without being argued for or against. Hedonism is shown failing at every step. Marxism is treated as a pose. Faith is absent as a solution. Feminist critics argue the book's view of women is distorted (TCJ), while defenders say the women's suffering is the point of Yozo's indictment (Solrad: "I believe that is the point").

**Shifts across the series.** Translator Jocelyne Allen notes that the manga starts "very faithful to Dazai's novel before gradually diverging" (Viz blog). The first chapters follow the novel closely, adding detail to the servants' abuse and to Takeichi's fate; the middle adds a love-triangle murder; the last chapters leave the novel entirely, carrying Yozo past its ending and bringing in Dazai.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yozo says he plays the clown because he fears people. Who in the story might have helped him if he had been honest, and why did he not tell them?
2. The abuse Yozo suffered as a child is never spoken of to anyone (Solrad). How does silence about abuse shape the rest of his life?
3. Takeichi, Tsuneko and Yoshiko are harmed by Yozo's choices. Is he a victim, a villain, or both?
4. Ito has the author Dazai meet Yozo and then end his own life. Does that make the story more honest or more hopeless?
5. The last line is "Everything passes." Is that comfort, resignation or despair, and what would your family's tradition say instead?
