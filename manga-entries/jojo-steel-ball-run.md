**Premise.** In 1890, about 3,600 racers set out from San Diego in a horse race across the United States to New York, for a $50 million prize. Johnny Joestar is a former jockey star. He was shot in the spine and is paralyzed from the waist down. Gyro Zeppeli comes from a family of royal executioners in Naples. He races to win a pardon for Marco, a boy he has been ordered to execute. Gyro's spinning steel balls make Johnny's legs twitch, so Johnny joins the race to learn the Spin. The race turns out to be a front. US President Funny Valentine sponsors it to gather the scattered parts of a 1,900-year-old "Saint's Corpse." The story strongly implies it is the body of Jesus, but never names him. Each part gives supernatural power ("Stands"). Valentine wants the whole Corpse so that its blessing makes the United States first among nations.

## Life message

**A good life is the honest pursuit of one's own fulfillment and growth through hardship and friendship: getting from "minus to zero" by one's own effort, not by power taken from others. No nation, and no sacred object, justifies sacrificing the powerless.**

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, most frames see a ruler's abuse of the sacred condemned and repentance treated with sympathy, so mid grades are common. The dividing lines are how the sacred itself is handled. Traditions that venerate relics (Catholic, Orthodox) find both kinship and profanation in the Corpse. Traditions that reject relics or images of Christ (Reformed, Baptist, Jehovah's Witness, Muslim, Sikh) weigh the premise more heavily. The story's Christ dies again (ch. 67), which conflicts with the Ascension (most Christian churches) and with Qur'an 4:157-158 (Muslim) in different ways. Secular humanist and Satanist frames read the anti-nationalist, self-made-growth message as a close fit. Anabaptists note that the Saint's only teaching is "do not shoot," and the hero shoots anyway.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Individual friendship and honest striving win. The president who seizes the sacred for his nation loses, but the story gives him dignity and leaves his patriotism partly sympathetic.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** Gyro Zeppeli is widely treated as the heart of the story; Geek.com's K. Thor Jensen called the Johnny-Gyro friendship one of the best platonic friendships in comics (via Wikipedia). In a 2016 Buzz Plus poll of 500 JoJo fans on the series' final villains, the alternate Dio placed 5th and Funny Valentine 8th (Otaku USA). Valentine is a frequent subject of "best villain" lists (Fandomwire, found, not opened). Fans and TV Tropes often read him as a "Noble Demon" with "a point" (TV Tropes, White-and-Grey Morality), so his patriotism draws more sympathy than the plot's verdict on him. The phrase "dojyan!" and his napkin speech are well-known fan references (jojowiki). An ANN column called the series "a cartoon about a horse race with Jesus."

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987. Statements on this Part (via Wikipedia and jojowiki): the theme is "the pursuit of satisfaction." A recurring JoJo message is humanity's ability to grow "through strength and spirit, without relying on machines or gods." Good and evil are not always easy to tell apart. Valentine's patriotism and goals could be just; his exploitation of powerless people is what makes him evil. The fighting president came from Araki's enjoyment of the film Independence Day. Johnny's paralysis was not planned at the start. Araki made research trips to the West Coast deserts, the Great Plains, Chicago and New York.
- On the Corpse: in his vol. 24 author's note, Araki says he left its identity unclear and drew it as "a symbol of purity," linked to an instinctive sense of good and evil, virtue and what deserves respect (jojowiki, Jesus Christ page, summarizing the note; exact wording to verify in the volume).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What may a person, or a nation, do to win a blessing? Everyone in the race wants something: money, a pardon, a cure, forgiveness, power. The Saint's Corpse promises all of these. The story asks who has a right to the sacred, and what it costs others when someone takes it.

**The answer the story gives.** The Corpse belongs to no one. Valentine wants its blessing so that misfortune falls on other nations instead of America (ch. 78). The story calls this exploitation of the powerless, and Araki names that as what makes him evil (Wikipedia). The heroes want smaller things: Johnny wants to walk and to get "back to zero" (ch. 72); Gyro wants to save one boy. Their growth comes from friendship and effort more than from the relics. At the end, Steven Steel concludes that the Corpse belongs to no one, and it is sealed in a church vault (ch. 90-95). Araki's vol. 24 note calls it "a symbol of purity" whose identity he left unclear (jojowiki).

**Characters as arguments.** Johnny is the argument that a damaged, selfish person can grow, though his "dark determination" is never fully cleansed. Gyro is the argument for honor grounded in consent and justice rather than law (ch. 21). Valentine is patriotism argued at full strength. He is brave, keeps his oath not to kill Steven, and dies saying his heart and actions are "utterly unclouded" (ch. 89). But he uses others as shields. Hot Pants is repentance: she seeks forgiveness for sacrificing her brother (ch. 56-57). Lucy Steel is innocence that becomes courage. Diego is ambition born of poverty.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Corpse: scattered holiness that attracts the greedy and the guilty. The napkin: who sets the rules (ch. 61). The flag: Valentine's scars and his father's handkerchief tie his patriotism to loss (ch. 87). The Spin and the Golden Rectangle: a natural order learned through practice. The race map, drawn by the Saint himself in the story (ch. 67), turns the American continent into holy ground.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Christian relic tradition and the Grail quest. Joseph of Arimathea appears by name (ch. 25). The story's Christ rises, then travels and later dies far away (ch. 67). This differs from the creeds' Ascension and from Qur'an 4:157-158. It has some resemblance to legends of Jesus traveling east (from general knowledge; to verify). The American Western and the frontier myth. The political novel of a president who misuses power. Buddhist relic division and the problem of craving (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nationalism and authoritarian rule are critiqued through Valentine, but given dignity. Individualism and friendship are affirmed. Christianity is borrowed with respect for the figure and freedom with the doctrine. The Saint's one recorded teaching, "do not shoot when your heart wavers" (ch. 58-59), is a call to restraint that the hero does not finally keep. Critical readings of race and land appear (Sandman, Pocoloco). Common Sense Media calls them surface-level, and some anime viewers said they were trimmed (OtakuKart). Secular readers can take Araki's own line: growth "without relying on machines or gods."

**Shifts across the series.** The Weekly Shonen Jump chapters (ch. 1-23, v1-4) are a race adventure. The move to Ultra Jump brings the Corpse plot and the President to the front (from v5, "All the President's Men"). Gettysburg (ch. 56-59, v15) makes guilt and repentance explicit. The Philadelphia and train arcs (ch. 60-88, v16-22) turn into a war over the Corpse. They bring the deaths of Hot Pants (ch. 77) and Gyro (ch. 84). The ending (ch. 89-95, v23-24) closes on loss, restraint and the sealing of the Corpse.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Valentine says the first to take the napkin sets the rules (ch. 61). Is he right about how power works? Is he right that it should work that way?
2. Araki says Valentine's patriotism could be just. What makes love of country good, and where does Valentine cross the line?
3. The Saint tells Johnny not to shoot when his heart wavers (ch. 58-59). Does Johnny follow that advice at the end (ch. 89)?
4. How does the story's picture of Jesus differ from what your family believes? Why might an author borrow a holy figure without naming him?
5. Johnny wants to get from "minus to zero." Can a person do that alone, or does it take friendship, grace or forgiveness?
