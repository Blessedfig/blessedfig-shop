## Historically Black Protestant
**beliefs:** God created all things and acts in history to deliver the oppressed, as in the Exodus; humanity is fallen and is saved by grace through faith in Jesus Christ, whose death and resurrection bring forgiveness and new life. Believers hope for eternal life with God and expect God's justice both in the world to come and in this one. Authority rests on the Bible as God's word, preached and heard in community, with the Holy Spirit's guidance stressed especially in Holiness and Pentecostal churches.
**values:** Personal faith and daily prayer; the church as extended family and center of community life; preaching, gospel music and expressive, participatory worship; hope and endurance through suffering; the God-given dignity and equality of every person; education, mutual aid, service and civic duty, including voting.
**forbids:** Sexual immorality, drunkenness and drug misuse, profanity, gambling and occult practice are widely taught against; Holiness churches such as COGIC also ask for modest dress and limits on worldly entertainment. Racism is treated as a sin against God, not only a social wrong: most Black adults say opposing racism is essential to their faith (Pew 2021).
**family:** The large bodies officially teach that marriage is between a man and a woman (AME Discipline, reaffirmed 2024; COGIC; NBC USA 2012 statement); the Progressive National Baptist Convention leaves same-sex blessings to each congregation, and members' views vary (56% favored same-sex marriage in Pew's 2023-24 study). Women are ordained in the AME, AME Zion, CME and PNBC; COGIC does not ordain women as pastors but has a large Women's Department and honored church mothers; National Baptist congregations decide for themselves. Elders and the church family share in raising children. Identity is marked by baptism, Sunday worship, choir and youth ministries, revivals and church anniversaries.
**stories:** Stories are judged by whether they honor God, tell the truth about suffering and hope, and treat people with dignity. Bible stories of deliverance (Moses, Joseph, Daniel) are the model: the faithful endure, God makes a way, and the lowly are lifted up. Fantasy is accepted by many families; Holiness and Pentecostal families are more wary of spells, spirits and the occult, which they may see as spiritually real. How Black characters are drawn and portrayed is often noticed.
**range:** Seven large denominations studied by Lincoln and Mamiya (National Baptist Convention USA, National Baptist Convention of America, Progressive National Baptist Convention, AME, AME Zion, CME, Church of God in Christ), plus Black Missionary and independent Baptist, Holiness, Pentecostal and nondenominational churches. Baptist congregations are self-governing; COGIC and Holiness churches keep stricter standards; the PNBC is the most socially activist and on some questions the most open.

**media:** No denomination-wide media code. Philippians 4:8 is a common test, and Holiness churches teach separation from worldly entertainment. Many families also ask how Black people are shown, valuing dignity and fair representation alongside clean content.
**grading_note:** Grade as a Bible-centered tradition whose personal morality is close to the Baptist and Pentecostal frames but whose reading of the Bible puts deliverance and justice at the center. Tends to welcome: deliverance from bondage or oppression, faith and prayer that carry people through suffering, hope that does not give up, loyalty to family and community, elders' wisdom, standing up to injustice (especially without hatred), protecting the weak, redemption and second chances, education and self-improvement. Tends to worry about: occult or demonic power shown as good, sexual content and fan service, profanity and mockery of God or the church, revenge celebrated, despair with no hope, and racial caricature (a depiction concern; it lowers the grade only when the story's message endorses contempt for a people). Same-sex romance is a concern for many families but not all; note it rather than treating it as decisive. Stories that join justice themes with personal holiness grade highest.

## Atheist
**beliefs:** Atheists lack belief in any god (American Atheists' definition); most see the natural world as all there is and expect no afterlife, though there is no shared creed, scripture or leader. Pew (2023) found 78% of atheists say science can in principle explain everything, while about one in five still believe in some higher power or spiritual force, not the God of the Bible. Authority rests on evidence and reason, with each person reaching their own conclusions.
**values:** Honesty about what can and cannot be known; reason and evidence; not hurting people and logic as the main moral guides (86% and 87% call them very important, Pew 2023); freedom of conscience; separation of church and state; science education; meaning found in relationships, work and this life.
**forbids:** No formal prohibitions. Common concerns are religious pressure on children, claims presented as true without evidence, and harm or cruelty.
**family:** No set family pattern; equal roles and same-sex marriage are widely accepted (88% of the religiously unaffiliated favor same-sex marriage, Pew 2023-24). Parents usually explain the reasons behind rules, want children to think for themselves and learn about many religions, and leave final beliefs to the child (Dale McGowan; Christel Manning). Identity is marked lightly: some join local atheist or freethought groups, Secular Student Alliance chapters, or advocacy groups such as American Atheists and the Freedom From Religion Foundation (Madison, Wisconsin).
**stories:** Myths, gods, demons and magic are welcomed as fiction and cultural heritage, and religious stories are often enjoyed as literature. Concern arises when a story teaches that faith is needed to be good, that doubters are wicked, or that unbelief is punished.
**range:** From quiet, private nonbelievers to active members of atheist and church-state groups; from those who see religion as mostly harmful (73% of atheists say it does more harm than good, Pew 2023) to those who value it as culture. Mostly but not entirely left-leaning (78% Democratic or lean). Distinct from the Secular humanist frame (an organized positive philosophy) and the Secular progressive frame (a political-values frame): the only shared trait is not believing in gods.

**media:** No media rules. Families tend to favor curiosity, science and free expression and to oppose censorship; they notice stories that present belief as the only road to goodness or treat doubters as villains.
**grading_note:** Grade the story's conclusions about belief, not its fantasy furniture: gods, spirits and magic inside a fictional world do not lower the grade. Tends to welcome: characters who question, investigate and test claims; goodness that comes from empathy rather than command; meaning built in this life; heroes who solve problems by their own effort and reason; exposure of manipulative religious or cult authority; respect for free thought. Tends to worry about: stories whose resolution is that faith or prayer is the real answer, conversion arcs that treat unbelief as a flaw to be cured, skeptics punished or mocked as fools, and real-world supernatural claims presented as fact. A story set in a world with real gods can still grade well if its characters act from human compassion and choice.

## Agnostic
**beliefs:** Agnostics hold that whether God exists is unknown or cannot be known; T. H. Huxley coined the word in 1869 and called it a method rather than a creed. Many stay open: Pew (2023) found 67% of agnostics believe in God or some higher power and 61% believe there is something spiritual beyond the natural world, though they name no religion. Authority rests on reason and evidence, with honest admission of the limits of knowledge.
**values:** Intellectual humility and open-mindedness; reason and evidence; tolerance of differing beliefs; not hurting people and logic as moral guides (88% each call them very important, Pew 2023); freedom to keep exploring.
**forbids:** No formal prohibitions. Common concerns are dogmatism and certainty claimed without evidence, from religious or anti-religious voices alike; pressure on children to adopt a belief; and harm or cruelty.
**family:** No set pattern; equal roles and same-sex marriage are widely accepted. Parents often expose children to several traditions and leave the choice to them; some attend a congregation for community or for a spouse's faith (Christel Manning). Identity is mostly private, and holidays are often kept as family and cultural occasions.
**stories:** Gods, spirits and mysteries are welcomed as ways of wondering about big questions, and endings that leave a mystery open are often appreciated. Concern arises when a story insists on certainty either way: that one faith is the only truth, or that all belief is foolish.
**range:** From near-atheists who simply will not claim certainty to seekers who believe in a higher power but no religion (41% describe themselves as spiritual, Pew 2023). About half (51%) say religion does more harm than good, while 38% say it does equal good and harm. Nearly half are college graduates (48%), and most lean left (71% Democratic or lean). Distinct from Atheist (lack of belief) and from the Secular humanist and Secular progressive values frames: the defining trait is not knowing.
**sources:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, Agnosticism (Huxley, 1869) — https://www.britannica.com/topic/agnosticism; American Atheists, About Atheism (atheism vs. agnosticism distinction) — https://www.atheists.org/activism/resources/about-atheism/; Pew Research Center, Religious 'Nones' in America: Who They Are and What They Believe (Jan. 2024), report PDF pp. 21, 23, 47, 49, 64, 73, 76-87 — https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2024/01/PR_2024.01.24_religious-nones_REPORT.pdf; Pew Research Center, Are all 'nones' nonbelievers? (Jan. 2024) — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2024/01/24/are-all-nones-nonbelievers/; Christel Manning, Losing Our Religion (NYU Press, 2015), summary essay at Powell's — https://www.powells.com/post/original-essays/losing-their-religion-mdash-or-choosing-for-themselves; Lois Lee, Recognizing the Non-religious: Reimagining the Secular (Oxford University Press, 2015) — https://academic.oup.com/book/27632 (cited for the point that nonreligion is varied, not one stance; to verify against the text); Dale McGowan, interview in The Humanist — https://thehumanist.com/?p=8701
**media:** No rules. Families tend to value stories that raise big questions honestly, present more than one view fairly and leave room for the reader to decide.
**grading_note:** Grade for openness and honesty about the big questions. Tends to welcome: characters who wrestle with doubt without being punished for it, fair portrayal of several worldviews, humility, wonder at mystery, ethics grounded in empathy, open endings about what lies beyond. Tends to worry about: preachy certainty in either direction, conversion or deconversion as the only acceptable ending, contempt for believers or for doubters, and charismatic leaders who demand unquestioning faith shown as admirable. Gods and spirits as fiction do not lower the grade; a story that answers every spiritual question with a firm doctrine may grade lower than for Atheist or Nothing in particular, and a story that sneers at all belief may grade lower than for Atheist.

## Nothing in particular
**beliefs:** Not a belief system: people who do not identify with any religion, nor as atheist or agnostic. Most still believe in God or a higher power (83%, Pew 2023); many believe in a soul (78%), heaven (50%) and hell (41%), and 19% pray daily, though few attend services. Authority is personal: what makes sense to them, how they were raised, and a sense that what goes around comes around (54% call it very important in moral choices).
**values:** Not hurting people (80% very important) and logic and reason (79%); family, friends and loyalty; fairness; staying out of trouble; personal freedom. Many value spirituality without religion: 60% describe themselves as spiritual or say spirituality is very important to them (Pew 2023).
**forbids:** No formal prohibitions. Many dislike religious institutions or religious pressure; concerns center on harm, cruelty and content beyond a child's age.
**family:** Family forms vary; most accept same-sex marriage (the unaffiliated as a whole favor it 88%, Pew 2023-24). Most parents want children to choose for themselves. Some are believers without a church who pray or read the Bible at home; others are spiritual seekers who mix traditions, and others are simply indifferent (Christel Manning's four types). Identity is marked by family traditions, with religious holidays often kept as family occasions.
**stories:** Gods, spirits, fate and an afterlife are welcomed and often taken half-seriously; many readers like the sense that the universe is fair or that loved ones watch over us. Religious content is fine when it is not preachy; concern arises with stories that push a religion, or that attack belief.
**range:** The largest unaffiliated group (19% of U.S. adults; 63% of nones): from people who believe in God but skip church, through spiritual-but-not-religious seekers, to those who rarely think about religion. On average less formally educated (27% college graduates) and more politically mixed (54% Democratic or lean) than atheists and agnostics, and less involved in civic groups; scholars such as Ryan Burge note many are less connected to institutions of all kinds, not only religious ones. Not the same as the Secular humanist or Secular progressive frames: many hold spiritual beliefs and few belong to secular organizations.
**sources:** Pew Research Center, Religious 'Nones' in America: Who They Are and What They Believe (Jan. 2024), report PDF pp. 21, 23, 47, 49, 59, 64, 73, 76-87 — https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2024/01/PR_2024.01.24_religious-nones_REPORT.pdf; Pew Research Center, Are all 'nones' nonbelievers? (Jan. 2024) — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2024/01/24/are-all-nones-nonbelievers/; Pew Research Center, Spirituality Among Americans (Dec. 2023): 22% of adults spiritual but not religious, 54% of them unaffiliated — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/12/07/spirituality-among-americans/; Pew Research Center, Religious Landscape Study 2023-24 (2025): 19% of adults 'nothing in particular' — https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/02/26/religious-landscape-study-religious-identity/; Christel Manning, Losing Our Religion (NYU Press, 2015), summary essay at Powell's — https://www.powells.com/post/original-essays/losing-their-religion-mdash-or-choosing-for-themselves; Ryan Burge, quoted in Religion Unplugged, Under the 'Nones' umbrella (Apr. 2021) — https://religionunplugged.com/news/2021/4/12/wc31gbj62l8pic1wjyczadpz8coj2z ; The Nones (Fortress Press, 2021; 2nd ed. 2023); Lois Lee, Recognizing the Non-religious (Oxford University Press, 2015) — https://academic.oup.com/book/27632 (to verify against the text)
**media:** No rules; families decide case by case, mostly by age-appropriateness, kindness, and whether a story is enjoyable rather than preachy.
**grading_note:** Do not grade this frame as a copy of Secular humanist: most of these families believe in God or a higher power, many in an afterlife and in spirits. Tends to welcome: loyalty to family and friends, everyday decency, fairness and karma-like justice, a personal spiritual sense that does not demand membership, reunion with or protection by the dead, hope, and finding your own path. Tends to worry about: preachy religion or preachy anti-religion, despair and nihilism, cruelty celebrated, and institutions (religious or not) that control people. Spiritual-but-open stories (gods, souls, fate) usually grade higher here than for Atheist; stories that end in a call to one exclusive faith grade somewhat lower than for the Christian frames but are not rejected.

## New lenses
- **Hindu ideas** (Faith and the spiritual): Life is ordered by dharma (right order and one's duty) and by karma, the soul passes through many births (samsara), and liberation (moksha) comes through right action, knowledge, or loving devotion (bhakti) to God, who can enter the world in forms called avatars. Gods and demons drawn from Indian myth, reincarnated heroes and avatars, characters bound by caste or duty, or a hero told to do their duty without clinging to the result.
- **Buddhist ideas** (Faith and the spiritual): Life as usually lived is marked by suffering (dukkha) that comes from craving, everything is impermanent and there is no lasting self, and a path of ethics, meditation and wisdom (or, in Pure Land teaching, trust in Amida Buddha's vow) leads out of rebirth to nirvana, with compassion for all beings at its heart. Monks and temples, karma following a character across lives, peace found by letting go of a grudge or attachment, mercy toward enemies, and in Japanese works, funerals, family altars, Obon visits from the dead, Zen training, and Buddhist hells judged by King Enma.
- **Bushido / honor code** (How to live and choose): A warrior should live by a code of honor: loyalty to one's lord or cause above self, rectitude, courage, self-control and courtesy, and readiness to die rather than live in disgrace. A swordsman who keeps his word at any cost, loyalty to a master or clan, duels fought by the rules, shame worse than death, ritual suicide, and stories asking whether the code is noble or a trap.
- **Patriotism / nationalism** (Society and politics): Love of and loyalty to one's country and people, with a readiness to serve and sacrifice for it; in its fuller political form, nationalism, the belief that the nation is the main unit of political life, should govern itself, and has first claim on its members' loyalty. Heroes defending a homeland, soldiers serving their country, the founding or unification of a nation, rival peoples at war, and stories asking whether love of country is noble or has turned into hatred of others.
- **Pacifism / nonviolence** (How to live and choose): Violence, and above all war, is wrong and should be refused on principle, even at cost to oneself, and conflicts should be met by nonviolent means such as forgiveness, persuasion, protest, or bearing wrong rather than doing it. A hero who vows never to kill, a warrior who lays down the sword, characters who break a cycle of revenge, or a story showing the horror of war and calling for peace.
- **Duty ethics (deontology)** (How to live and choose): Some acts are right or wrong in themselves, whatever their results: we must keep promises, tell the truth, and treat every person as an end and never merely as a tool, even when breaking the rule would bring a better outcome. A character who will not lie or break a promise even to win, who refuses to sacrifice one innocent to save many, or who defends the law against a vigilante's results.
- **Liberalism** (Society and politics): Each person has equal rights and freedoms that government exists to protect, power is legitimate only with the consent of the governed and under the rule of law, and people of differing beliefs should tolerate one another. Constitutions and elections defended against a tyrant, fair trials and rights for the accused, free speech and religious tolerance, and heroes who fight for a free republic rather than a good king.
- **Anarchism** (Society and politics): No state or ruler has a rightful claim to govern others by force, and society should run instead on free agreement, mutual aid and local cooperation without coercive hierarchy. Rebels and outlaws who want no rulers at all, not just better ones, free towns or crews without a chain of command, and stories showing a stateless society as freedom or as chaos.
- **Taoist ideas** (Faith and the spiritual): The Dao, the Way behind all things, is followed by living naturally and without forcing (wu wei), keeping yin and yang in balance and cultivating qi; religious Daoism adds the search for long life and immortality through inner alchemy, ritual and immortal sages.
