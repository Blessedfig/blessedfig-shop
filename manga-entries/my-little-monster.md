**Premise.** Shizuku Mizutani, a first-year high-school student, cares only about grades: she wants a career earning 10 million yen a year, like her hard-working mother, and classmates call her "dry ice." Asked to bring printouts to Haru Yoshida, a suspended boy who never comes to school, she meets a brilliant, naive and violent loner who decides she is his first friend and then that he loves her. Over two school years the pair, both socially awkward, gather friends (Asako Natsume, Sasayan, class rep Chizuru Oshima), deal with a rival (Kenji "Yamaken" Yamaguchi) and confront Haru's family: a rich politician father who threw him out, a mother who abandoned her sons, an older brother, Yuzan, he resents, and a late aunt, Kyoko, who taught him that "the medicine" for loneliness is "to find other people" (fan wiki). They become a couple (ch. 32), separate when Haru leaves for a research internship and a year at sea, and in a time skip Haru proposes after Shizuku passes the bar exam (ch. 48) (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life means letting other people in: two lonely, guarded people learn, through each other and a circle of friends, to show feeling, to forgive family and to choose a future of their own.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

Older-male, younger-female pairing is refused: Mitchan turns Asako down because she is a high-school student (vol. 5, 7; fan wiki). The story ends in marriage (ch. 48, vol. 13). |
| Pragmatic | Shizuku begins with a cost-benefit view of people ("if it's their problem, they should solve it," fan wiki) and a salary target. She learns that relationships cost effort and pay back. Her mother's advice is to work twice as hard to have both love and grades (vol. 6-7). Shizuku ends a lawyer, so the pragmatic goal is kept, not dropped. |
| Religious | No religion beyond New Year temple visits (ch. 16; ch. 45-46). Faith readers mainly weigh the early coercive scenes, Haru's violence, and the family themes (an absent mother, a womanizing father, an older brother's resentment). |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of Japan's elite and political class (Haru's father, Yuzan's birthday party for socialites, ch. 37-40) and of school labels ("monster," "dry ice"). A family where the mother is the breadwinner and the father keeps house (ch. 13). Critics read the romance as a "bad romance" that excuses a violent boy (Otaku Journalist, ComicBook.com); ANN's review of the last volume calls the end "a healthy relationship in shoujo romance." |

Outlook: Hopeful — two isolated teenagers end with friends, reconciled families, chosen careers and a marriage, and even the resentful brother finds his own path.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a person human, and what heals loneliness? Haru carries his late aunt's answer: "Education, language and lastly love," and "Humans anguish alone. And the unique medicine to this, is to find other people" (fan wiki). The series tests that claim on two people who have closed themselves off: a boy who speaks with his fists and a girl who speaks only through grades.

**The answer the story gives.** Connection, slowly learned. Shizuku brings Haru to school and to friends; Haru pulls Shizuku out of her routine. Both have to learn what they lack. He has to learn restraint and patience; she has to learn to show feeling and to stop treating love as a distraction. Her mother tells her to work twice as hard to have both (vol. 6-7). In the end she keeps her ambition, becomes a lawyer, and accepts his proposal (ch. 48).

**Characters as arguments.** Haru is the case for honesty without social filters, and the story's most debated figure: his first acts toward Shizuku include a grab, a threat and a kiss she did not agree to (ch. 1-2), which reviewers call hard to watch (Manga Bookshelf; ANN) and which critics say the story never really addresses (ComicBook.com). Shizuku is the case for self-sufficiency, softened rather than abandoned. Yuzan is the case for conforming to family status, and the story shows its cost. Mitchan is the adult who keeps a boundary with a teenage girl. Yamaken argues that a rival can stay a friend.

**Symbols and motifs.** The title's "monster" and the nickname "dry ice": labels both leads outgrow. Nagoya the rooster, Haru's odd pet kept at school. Shizuku's goldfish project, abandoned when her mother could not come (fan wiki), and Haru's offer to catch her a fish (vol. 4). The brothers' moon rocks (vol. 12). Haru's letter at the end of the third year (ANN vol. 12).

**Traditions it echoes.** The shojo "bad boy reformed by love" pattern, which the Otaku Journalist names as the series' risk: it "teaches that the right girl, with love and tenderness, can reform a violent boy." The screwball comedy, which ANN's anime reviewer compares it to. The Japanese family drama of the political dynasty and the outcast son.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian and humanist lenses are affirmed: friends, study and science make the good life. Expressive individualism is affirmed in Haru's refusal of his family and Shizuku's late honesty. Feminist readers find an ambitious heroine and a breadwinner mother, and also a romance that begins with coercion. Religious frames find little religion and focus on the opening scenes, the violence and the teen dating.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 are comedy and first friendship, including the coercive opening and the festival punch. Volumes 5-8 bring confessions, the ski trip, Shizuku's own kiss and declaration (ch. 24) and the couple forming (ch. 32). Volumes 9-11 turn to Haru's family and a crisis between the leads. Volume 12 sends them apart and brings them back years later; volume 13 adds side stories.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Haru grabs and kisses Shizuku early on, and the story moves past it quickly. How should a story handle a hero's wrongs?
2. Kyoko says love is part of what makes us human. What did Haru and Shizuku each lack at the start?
3. Why does Mitchan turn Asako down, and what does that say about adults' responsibility?
4. Is Haru's violence ever right? Compare the fight for Chizuru (ch. 6) with the festival punch (ch. 12).
5. Shizuku keeps her goal of a career. Did she have to choose between love and ambition?
