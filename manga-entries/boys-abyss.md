**Premise.** Reiji Kurose is a high-school boy in a nameless rural seaside town. He cares for his grandmother, who has dementia, lives with a withdrawn, angry older brother, Kazumasa ("Kazu"), and is relied on by his mother Yuko, an exhausted nurse's aide. His childhood friend Gen Minegishi, son of a construction boss whose firm is reportedly a front for organized crime, controls him. His other childhood friend, Sakuko "Chako" Akiyama, dreams of a Tokyo university. Then Nagi Aoe, a 20-year-old member of the idol group Acrylic on a health break, starts working at the local convenience store. She and Reiji sleep together, and she asks him to make a suicide pact at the "Lover's Abyss" (Jōshigafuchi), a local spot where couples are said to die together. Their attempt fails; his 29-year-old homeroom teacher, Yuri Shibasawa, stops it, takes him in and begins a sexual relationship with him. Nagi turns out to be married to Esemori, a 46-year-old novelist who knew Reiji's mother as a teenager. Over 18 volumes the story uncovers the adults' pasts and ends with Reiji confronting his mother and choosing to live (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; ciatr).

## Life message

**The series presents a good life as one where a young person breaks free of the adults and the town that use him, faces the truth about his family and chooses to keep living on his own terms. VIZ's blurb puts the problem plainly: "Self-serving adults and broken dreams push a teenager toward the abyss."**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a young person owe to a family and a town that use him, and is death the only way out? Reiji is asked to be caregiver, emotional partner and errand boy. When a famous idol offers to die with him, the offer feels like freedom. The series asks whether it is.

**The answer the story gives.** Death is not the way out; truth is. The suicide attempt fails in the first volumes. Over the next sixteen volumes, the adults' secrets come out: Yuko's past, Esemori's role in it, the killing of Kazu's father. The ending is a confrontation in which Reiji and his mother speak honestly and then live apart (ciatr). Nagi, who once offered death, says she would still die with him but won't, because he won't ask (Outer Haven, vol. 12). Living on is presented as the harder and better choice.

**Characters as arguments.** Reiji is the argument that despair in the young is often a response to what adults do to them. Nagi is the argument for romantic death as escape, and she abandons it. Shibasawa is the argument that "protection" can become possession and abuse; fans love her, and the story arrests her. Yuko is the argument that a hurt child can become a parent who hurts. Gen is the argument that loyalty without limits becomes violence, and that responsibility is still possible (he turns himself in). Esemori is the argument that an artist can treat people as material; his last unfinished work is named after the boy.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Lover's Abyss (Jōshigafuchi), a ravine where couples are said to die, is the central symbol. The river carries both the failed pact and Yuko's body. The idol image of Nagi versus the real person. Esemori's novel Ao no Hitsugi (Spring Coffin in VIZ) recasts real lives as fiction. The final image places Reiji upstream of the ravine.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese shinjū (lovers' double suicide) tradition in drama and literature, which the legend calls up (my own framing); rural noir; the coming-of-age story of leaving home.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is voiced by Nagi and the town and is critiqued through its cost. Existentialism shapes the ending: meaning is made by choosing to live. Expressive individualism wins over family duty. Religion is absent.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols 1-3 (ch. 1-27): the idol, the suicide pact, the teacher's rescue and the sexual relationships. Vols 4-7 (ch. 28-68): the teacher's obsession, Chako and Esemori, the teacher's exposure. Vols 8-13 (ch. 69-129): the adults' past, Esemori's backstory, Kazu's father. Vols 14-16 (ch. 130-160): Gen kills Yuko and surrenders. Vols 17-18 (ch. 161-183): confrontation and epilogue. Readers note "arc fatigue" in the backstory volumes and a rushed ending (TV Tropes; MyAnimeList).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does dying with Nagi seem like freedom to Reiji at first? What changes his mind?
2. Shibasawa says she is protecting Reiji. When does protection become control or abuse?
3. What did Reiji owe his mother and grandmother, and what did they owe him?
4. Many fans like characters the story treats as harmful. Why might that happen?
5. If someone you know felt like Reiji, who could they talk to?
