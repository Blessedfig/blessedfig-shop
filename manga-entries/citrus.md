**Premise.** Yuzu Aihara (née Okogi), a 16-year-old self-styled gyaru who loves fashion, transfers to Aihara Academy, a strict all-girls school, after her widowed mother remarries. On day one she clashes with the stern student council president, Mei Aihara, then learns at home that Mei is her new stepsister and roommate. Mei kisses her that night (ch. 1). Mei is the granddaughter of the school's chairman and is engaged to a teacher who wants the school; Yuzu exposes him (ch. 2). The two girls draw close through Mei's family wounds (a father who left to travel, ch. 7-8), jealous rivals (Mei's childhood friend Himeko, v2; Yuzu's 14-year-old childhood friend Matsuri, v3; the twins Sara and Nina on the Kyoto trip, v4) and school politics (Mitsuko and the council election, v5). They confess on the Kyoto trip (ch. 16) and date in secret (v5-8). Yuzu proposes a "Fall in Love With Mei" plan (v6), sinks into depression when she learns not everyone will accept them (ch. 24), and then faces Mei's arranged marriage to a man her grandfather picks (v9, revealed in a letter, ch. 36). Mei quits school to hurry the marriage (ch. 40); Yuzu proposes and Mei breaks the engagement (ch. 41). An epilogue shows their wedding (Seven Seas synopses; fan wiki; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life follows the heart past rules, family pressure and taboo: an honest, persistent love can thaw a lonely, controlled person and should win out over duty and arranged marriage.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Pair-bonding without reproduction among teens in a closed group, with constant mate competition (Himeko, Matsuri, Sara, Udagawa). Sexual dominance (Mei's pinning and kissing, ch. 1, 3) is used to assert control. An arranged marriage for family status (Amamiya, ch. 1-2; Udagawa, ch. 33-41) is set against personal choice, and choice wins. |
| Pragmatic | Problems are made by silence and solved by direct talk: Mei never explains herself, and the decisive scenes are confessions (ch. 16, 41). Yuzu's planning notebook (v6) and the student council election (v5) are practical tools. Critics argue the story's early "conflicts" are produced by coercion rather than communication (Friedman, Okazu). |
| Religious | No religion beyond a shrine scene on the Kyoto trip (Yasaka Shrine, ch. 16) and the epilogue's wedding. Traditions with teaching on sexuality weigh the affirmed same-sex romance, the step-sibling setting, and the forced contact between minors. Traditions that affirm same-sex relationships still weigh consent. |
| Socio-cultural | A Japanese yuri melodrama in a girls'-school setting. It sets gyaru self-expression against a school that "prepares you for society" through rules (Mei, ch. 1), and personal love against family duty (the grandfather's arranged marriages). Fathers are absent or controlling; the mothers' side (Ume) is accepting ("It's not wrong to love people"; fan wiki). |

Outlook: Hopeful — the lonely, controlled Mei learns to smile and choose for herself, the families come around, and the story ends in a wedding.
Hope source: L — the way through for both girls is their love for each other, which breaks the arranged marriage and Mei's isolation (ch. 41: Yuzu proposes and Mei "finally smiles"; fan wiki).

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** No official popularity poll was found. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can love grow out of a relationship that begins in force, secrecy and taboo? Yuzu and Mei meet as rivals, become sisters by their parents' marriage, and are pushed together by Mei's sudden kisses. The series asks whether two people so unlike each other, and so tied by family, can turn that into something honest.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if one of them keeps telling the truth. Yuzu keeps saying what she feels. Mei, who has learned to live by rules after her father left, keeps silent and acts. The turning points are confessions: on the Kyoto trip (ch. 16), when Yuzu kisses Mei "as proof of her love," and at the end, when Mei is about to marry the man her grandfather chose and Yuzu proposes instead, saying she will "love you more than anyone in the entire world" (ch. 41, quoted on the fan wiki). Mei breaks the engagement and "finally smiles."

**Characters as arguments.** Yuzu is the case for openness and self-expression. Mei is the case for duty and control, and her arc argues that control hides loneliness. The grandfather argues for family and institution, and the story lets him bend and help. Amamiya argues for marriage as a ladder to power, and he is exposed (ch. 2). Matsuri, 14, argues that people can be used, through photos, blackmail and men who pay her; Mei answers that "the two of us aren't so different," and Matsuri gives up her scheme (likely ch. 12). Sara gives the story's plainest line on attraction: it "doesn't matter if they are a boy or girl" (anime ep. 10).

**Symbols and motifs.** Citrus: sweet and sour. Chapter titles all use "love." The stuffed bear keychain Yuzu gives Mei. The ring Yuzu gives Mei, which Mei keeps wearing under her shirt even as she prepares to marry another man (ch. 41). Yuzu's planning notebook, in which Mei finally writes after the wedding (epilogue). Hair dye: Yuzu dyes her hair because she was told she did not look like her late father, and dyes it black for a time to fit in (ch. 17).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Class S girls'-school romance and yuri's "sisters" framing; Okazu's guest reviewer calls it a "pseudo-incest" drama, and Anime Feminist notes the leads are not blood-related and meet as teens. Shojo melodrama: Manga Bookshelf's reviewer says it feels like "standard shoujo" and "a decent shoujo potboiler." The arranged-marriage plot recalls older romance where love defeats family duty.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressive views win: the couple chooses each other and marries. Tradition and authority are obstacles that soften rather than villains, except Amamiya. Feminist and secular critics argue the consent problem undercuts the story's own message: Friedman says the series asks the viewer "to find a sexual assault amusing"; Yurimother answers that the first kiss "is intended to be disconcerting" and that Mei's reasons unfold later. Religious traditions with teaching on sexuality read the romance itself as the main issue.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-16, the anime's span) are the most sexually charged and the most criticized: forced kisses, an undressing, rivals and blackmail, ending in mutual confession. Volumes 5-8 (ch. 17-32) are a secret relationship with school politics, dates and a summer trip; TV Tropes says the early assault-driven drama "was eventually dropped." Volumes 9-10 (ch. 33-41) bring the arranged marriage and the proposal. Citrus+ continues into a "more chaste" engagement period (TV Tropes).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mei kisses Yuzu the night they meet. How does Yuzu react, and how does the story treat it? How would you treat it in real life?
2. Why did the grandfather want Mei to marry? What changed his mind?
3. Matsuri uses a photo to force Mei into something. Why is blackmail so damaging, and what makes Matsuri stop?
4. Some reviewers love this series and some say it makes force look romantic. Which details would you point to for each view?
5. Your family may have its own beliefs about romance, sexuality and marriage. How do they compare with what this story presents as a happy ending?
