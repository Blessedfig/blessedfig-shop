**Premise.** Akira Tendo, 24, has spent three years at an exploitative "black company" in Tokyo: 72-hour shifts, a screaming boss, and a crush, Saori Ohtori, whom the CEO coerces into sex. One morning his landlord is eating another tenant. Tokyo is overrun by zombies, and Akira is overjoyed: he never has to go to work again. He writes a list of 100 things to do before he becomes a zombie and sets off with his college friend Kencho (an aspiring comedian), Shizuka Mikazuki (a cautious former accountant who escaped a controlling father) and Beatrix (a German woman who loves Japanese culture), later joined by the hippie Takemina, the vaccine researcher Tsurumi and Izuna, a teenage girl immune to the virus. The road trip runs from Akira's parents' village in Gunma (ch. 15-21) to Hokkaido, Osaka, Kyoto, Shikoku, Kyushu, the International Space Station (ch. 62-69) and Okinawa (ch. 74-82). Akira and Shizuka become a couple (ch. 57). Later arcs reveal that a pharmaceutical company, Umbriel, weaponized a virus from a comet, and a vaccine is developed from Izuna's blood (Wikipedia; fan wiki; VIZ synopses).

## Life message

**A good life means doing what you truly want, now, with friends, instead of postponing your life for a job that uses you up; the real "zombies" are people who have stopped living.**

Parents are sought out and protected (ch. 15-21). |
| Pragmatic | The list is a practical tool: concrete goals give purpose. Shizuka's caution and Akira's recklessness balance each other; skills (rugby, archery, makeup, gaming) win fights. The story rewards planning less than it rewards nerve. |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot. One list item is a New Year shrine visit at Ise (fan wiki). Faith readers weigh the hedonism (drinking, casual sex) against the rejection of work-as-idol and the stress on gratitude to parents. |
| Socio-cultural | A satire of Japan's overwork culture: Aso says he wanted to comment on Japanese society, where "there are a lot of 'zombies'" (Variety). Anime Feminist reads it as a critique of capitalism and labor exploitation but warns of "misogynist undertones." The villains (Akira's ex-boss Gonzou, the bitter NEET Higurashi and his gang) are people who blame others or crush others. |

Outlook: Hopeful — the end of the world frees people to live and to love, friends and family are saved more often than lost, and a vaccine is in production by the latest arcs, though many side characters die.
Hope source: S — the story's way through is being true to yourself: Akira escapes a soul-crushing life by doing what he actually wants, and Aso states the message as "Do what you want to do, as fully as you can" (Variety, 2023); friendship is the close second (F).

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the satire of exploitative work and the gratitude to parents. Progressive readers split: the labor critique is welcome, the early treatment of women is not.

## Creator and publisher context

- Haro Aso (story), born in Osaka, studied at Kansai University and dropped out; created Hyde & Closer and Alice in Borderland (2010-2016), then turned to writing stories for other artists: Zom 100 (2018-) and Noyu Girl (2021-) (Wikipedia, Haro Aso). Born 1980 per the fan wiki cited in this site's Alice in Borderland entry.
- On-record intent (Variety, 2023): the zombie genre felt oversaturated and he "wanted to make something that had never been done before"; he wrote it "to be a little offbeat, a little silly"; he wanted to comment on Japanese society, where people cannot do what they want and "there are a lot of 'zombies' here"; the message is "Do what you want to do, as fully as you can"; the pandemic resonance was not intended. Wikipedia adds that Aso, who never worked in a company, built Akira from a friend's weekend complaints about office life and thought, "If this is the case, I'd still rather be a zombie."
- Kotaro Takata (art) said he always wanted to draw zombies, citing Zombieland and Z Nation (Wikipedia), and drew on the zombie movement in Train to Busan; volume 1's cover has no zombies so that "not only men, but women" would pick it up; his message to fans: "do it before you turn into a zombie" (MyAnimeList, 2023).
- No statements on religion or politics beyond the social comment above were found. Neither creator has spoken publicly about the series' sexual content in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Monthly Sunday GX, a seinen magazine). VIZ Media (owned by Shogakukan and Shueisha; from general knowledge) publishes it under VIZ Signature, rated T+. VIZ co-produced the anime with Shogakukan (Wikipedia).
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you knew the world would end, what would you do, and why aren't you doing it now? Akira Tendo is alive in name only: three years at an exploitative company have drained the color from his life (the anime literally mutes the palette in flashback, Den of Geek via Wikipedia). The zombie apocalypse is the first good day he has had. The series asks whether the true death is the bite or the life he was already living.

**The answer the story gives.** Live now, on purpose, with people you love. Akira's list starts with small pleasures (clean my room, drink beer all day, ride a motorcycle) and grows into larger goods: telling off his boss (ch. 10), repaying his parents (ch. 15-21), becoming a "hero" (ch. 6-7), meeting the woman of his dreams (ch. 55-57). Aso states the message directly: "Do what you want to do, as fully as you can" (Variety). The answer is not pure pleasure: the most-praised items are acts of courage and gratitude.

**Characters as arguments.** Akira argues for joy as resistance. Shizuka argues for caution, then learns that survival without living is its own kind of death; she even confesses to a zombie by mistake (ch. 43) before confessing to Akira (ch. 57). Kencho argues for pleasure and laughter, and is also the most loyal friend. Gonzou is the case against domination: his followers desert him (ch. 11). Higurashi is the dark mirror of Akira, a man with a bucket list of revenge who blames society for his failures; his redemptive death (ch. 21) suggests even resentment can turn. Satoru, the robber whose kindness was abused until he became cruel, is turned back by Beatrix's mercy (ch. 40). Izuna, a jaded teenager, finds a family in the group and becomes the key to the vaccine.

**Symbols and motifs.** The list, crossed off item by item. Color returning to Akira's world. The "zombies" of the office, set against literal ones. The RV as a moving home. Space (ch. 62-69): Akira's father's childhood dream of seeing the Earth from orbit (the overview effect, Wikipedia) fulfilled by the son. The vaccine from a girl's blood: hope comes from the young.

**Traditions it echoes.** The zombie comedy (Zombieland; One Cut of the Dead, which Aso admired), the road movie, and the Japanese critique of karoshi culture. Its "remember you will die" frame echoes memento mori and Ecclesiastes for some Christian readers, and maranasati for Buddhists, though the series' answer leans to enjoyment rather than restraint. Its villains' "lists" echo the warning that freedom without care for others becomes cruelty.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and hedonism are celebrated. Critical theory's labor critique is central at the start (Anime Feminist), while its feminist critique is turned on the series itself for its early fan service and for Ohtori's lack of agency. Secular humanism wins in the end: science and friendship, not gods, push back the plague. Religious readers find a respectful pilgrimage and a real moral core (gratitude, mercy, courage) wrapped in casual sex, drinking and nudity.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 (ch. 1-21) are Tokyo, the road and the village war: the sharpest satire and the most sexual comedy. Volumes 7-12 (ch. 23-46) are travel episodes (Hokkaido, Osaka, Kyoto, Shikoku, the cruise). Volumes 13-16 (ch. 47-61) bring the vaccine plot, Izuna and the romance. Volumes 17 onward (ch. 62-92) go to space, Okinawa and the vaccine's production, turning the series toward saving the world.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Akira was glad the world ended because he hated his job. What would have helped him before the zombies came?
2. Higurashi also made a bucket list. What made his list different from Akira's?
3. What would be on your list, and how many items could you do now?
4. The series makes jokes about casual sex and drinking. Does "living fully" need those things? What do they cost?
5. Beatrix spares the man who planned to hurt her. Why does that change him?
