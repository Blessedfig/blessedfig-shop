**Premise.** Nariyuki Yuiga, a third-year high-school student from a poor family whose father has died, wants the school's "special VIP recommendation," a scholarship that pays all university fees. The principal grants it on one condition: he must tutor classmates who are geniuses in one field but hopeless in the subject they want to study. Fumino Furuhashi is a literature genius who wants to study astronomy; Rizu Ogata is a math genius who wants to study psychology; Uruka Takemoto is a star swimmer who needs English to win a sports scholarship abroad. Two older women join the cast: Mafuyu Kirisu, a strict teacher who is a slob at home, and Asumi Kominami, an alumna studying again for medical school who works at a maid café. As the girls work toward their goals, each falls for Nariyuki (Wikipedia; Viz; MangaUpdates). At the culture-festival fireworks a legend says whoever is touching at the first firework will become a couple; a hidden girl helps Nariyuki up (ch. 69). Each of the five endings follows a different girl as that girl (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**A good life comes from honest effort toward a goal you choose, even in your worst subject, and from helping others reach theirs; love and family grow out of that shared work.**

The story's own moral (effort and helping others) is not contested by fans; the debate is about who "wins."

## Creator and publisher context

- Taishi Tsutsui (story and art) was an assistant on Naoshi Komi's Nisekoi and drew the Magical Patissier Kosaki-chan spin-off (TV Tropes); his later Jump series is Syd Craft: Love Is a Mystery (Wikipedia). No date of birth or biography was found in sources opened.
- On-record statement: when ch. 150 ended Uruka's arc, Tsutsui announced in a note to readers that alternate "parallel" endings would follow and that readers could decide which ending they prefer; in his post he wrote that he expected "various opinions" but wanted to try it, believing some readers would be glad (ComicBook.com, March 9, 2020, embedding his post; translation ours). The fan wiki cites an official English Shonen Jump post in which Tsutsui says all routes are equally valid (not opened; to verify). AIPT's essay on the endings (January 2021) reports that he "had planned to do five endings since he started the series" and that readers can "take [the endings] as they choose" (secondhand; original interview not identified). He announced on Twitter that he had finished the storyboard for the final chapter (ANN, November 28, 2020). No statements about religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can effort beat talent? The title's joke is that the geniuses "can't study": each is brilliant in one field and hopeless in the one she loves. Nariyuki, who began with poor grades and became an all-rounder through hard work (fan wiki), is asked to help them cross that gap.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, with help. The scholarship plot rewards steady work, and the girls improve by learning methods from someone who had to struggle himself. The teacher Mafuyu argues the opposite, that it is a waste to leave one's gift for one's dream; she is given a reason (her own path from skating to teaching, inspired by Nariyuki's father) and changes her mind, apologizing to her students (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Nariyuki is the case for usefulness through effort, his father's motto. Fumino is the case for following a dream against the odds and for putting friends first, which the story later questions when Uruka says they all "have the right to be happy" (ch. 144). Rizu argues for logic and slowly learns to read feelings. Uruka argues for honest feeling and is the only heroine to confess before the endings (ch. 141). Asumi argues for persistence after failure. Mafuyu argues for talent, then for second chances.

**Symbols and motifs.** "[X]," the unknown in every chapter title, which ends as "An [X] Future" (ch. 187). The fireworks legend, a romantic myth that turns out to be a father's invention. Stars (Fumino), games (Rizu), water (Uruka), ice (Mafuyu), the maid café (Asumi), each the name of a route.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Jump harem romantic comedy (Tsutsui worked on Nisekoi), the visual-novel "route" structure, and the Japanese school story of exams and festivals.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and pragmatism are affirmed. Confucian family duty is affirmed through the widowed mother and repaired father-daughter bonds. Expressive individualism wins the argument with Mafuyu. Feminist readers find girls aiming at science and medicine but objectified in the fan service (AIPT). Hedonism is not argued for, only present in the comedy. Religious frames find no religious content and focus on the fan service.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-69 build the tutoring group, add Mafuyu and Asumi, and end at the culture-festival fireworks. Ch. 70-141 move through exams and confessions to Uruka's confession. Ch. 142-150 give Uruka's ending. Ch. 151-186 return to the fireworks and play out four other futures, several years on. Ch. 187 is a playful coda in which every girl touches Nariyuki at once.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mafuyu thinks students should study what they are best at. Was she wrong, or partly right?
2. Nariyuki's father said a useless man should strive to be useful. What does "useful" mean in this story?
3. Is it fair to readers to give five endings? Which one fits the story best, and why?
4. Which scenes treat a character's body as the joke? How would the scene change if it were your friend?
5. In ch. 144 the three girls decide not to hide their feelings for one another's sake. Was that wise?
