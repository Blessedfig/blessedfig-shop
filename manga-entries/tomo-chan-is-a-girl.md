**Premise.** Tomo Aizawa is a tall, strong, tomboyish first-year high school student who trained from childhood in her father Gorou's karate dojo and is the ace of the boys' karate club. She confesses to her childhood friend and neighbor Junichiro "Jun" Kubota (ch. 1), but Jun, who thought she was a boy until middle school, treats it as a joke and keeps treating her as "one of the guys." The strips follow Tomo's attempts to be seen as a girl, helped and hindered by her sharp-tongued best friend Misuzu Gundou and the cheerful, wealthy, part-British Carol Olston, along with karate captain Kousuke Misaki. Jun confesses at the culture festival (vol. 8), wins a match against Tomo's father for permission to date her, and the couple get together (vol. 8; Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life is being loved and accepted as you already are, by friends and by the one you love, rather than reshaping yourself to fit what a "girl" or a "guy" is supposed to be; loyal friendship is the ground that love grows from.**

| Pragmatic | Problems are solved by honest talk, eventually: Jun apologizes after a hurtful remark (anime ep. 2), Misuzu admits her meddling (late; Anime Feminist), Jun finally says what he feels (vol. 8). Years of silence and misreading cost both leads (vol. 6 flashback). |

| Socio-cultural | A comedy built on Japanese gender expectations: a girl who talks rough and fights well is not seen as a girl (TV Tropes "Values Dissonance"). Critics differ on whether it questions those norms (Kotaku; ANN's Silverman: "you are enough, no matter how you present yourself") or leans on them (ANN's Hopper; Anime Feminist premiere review). Outlook: Hopeful — the misunderstandings end in honesty, the couple gets together with family approval, and every friend is still there at the end.
Hope source: S — The story's resolution is that Tomo never needed to change: Jun loves her as the tomboy she is, and Misuzu admits "No one ever wanted you to change."

**Where traditions disagree.** The main dividing line is gender expression: progressive, humanist, UU, Reform Jewish and Pagan frames welcome a heroine who is loved without conforming, while some conservative Christian, Muslim and Orthodox Jewish families question a girl dressing and fighting like a boy (one Christian blog objects outright). A second line is modesty: the manga's mild fan service and the anime's bath scene matter most to Orthodox Jewish, Muslim, Adventist, Anabaptist and Jehovah's Witness frames. Almost every frame agrees with the story that the bus assault is wrong; the disagreement is over how fully the story answers the victim-blaming that follows.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Everyone wins in a gentle way: Tomo is loved as she is, Jun finds his voice, Misuzu keeps her friend, and Carol and Misaki pair off; what loses is the idea that a girl must change to be seen as one, and the silence that kept the leads apart.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** Misuzu is the breakout character: TV Tropes calls her "practically a Base-Breaking Character," nicknamed "Demon Queen," and lists Carol and Misuzu as the "Fan-Preferred Couple," more popular than either girl's official pairing. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone a girl in the eyes of the person she loves? Tomo Aizawa can outfight every boy in her karate club, talks rough, and has been Jun's best friend since childhood. When she confesses in the first chapter, he laughs it off. The series asks whether Tomo has to become someone else to be seen, and whether Jun's blindness is about her at all.

**The answer the story gives.** Tomo does not have to change. Most of the comedy comes from her attempts to be "girly": skirts, makeovers, a date, a role as Cinderella that her class gives to someone else while she plays the prince (vol. 7). These attempts mostly fail or misfire. The late reveal is that Jun saw her as a girl all along and kept quiet out of fear of losing the friendship, and that Misuzu pushed Tomo toward femininity partly to keep her close. When Jun finally confesses at the culture festival (vol. 8), critics report that he values the rivalry and roughness that make her Tomo (Anime Feminist). ANN's Rebecca Silverman sums up the message as "you are enough, no matter how you present yourself."

**Characters as arguments.** Tomo argues that love should not require a costume. Jun argues for silence as a way to protect what you have, and the story shows its cost: a middle-school rift and years of misreading (vol. 6). Misuzu argues that girls must be shaped to be seen, and then retracts it with "No one ever wanted you to change." Gorou argues that a suitor must prove himself, and the story lets the old custom stand in a light-hearted form. Carol argues, against appearances, that a cheerful face can hide deep feeling.

**Symbols and motifs.** The skirt is the central symbol: it marks Tomo as a girl, draws the attention of a predator, and becomes the subject of Jun's hurtful remark and apology (anime ep. 2). The dojo stands for family, discipline and the father's blessing. The *Cinderella* play reverses the fairy tale: the girl who wants to be the princess plays the prince. The four-panel strip itself shapes the story: short gags, slow progress.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese tomboy romantic comedy and the childhood-friend romance; the yonkoma gag strip; the old motif of a suitor tested by the bride's father. Its view of identity echoes expressive individualism, while its courtship echoes older family customs.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Feminist and progressive readers are split: Kotaku praises its look at "how antiquated gender norms get in the way of love," while ANN's Faye Hopper calls volume 1 "the worst, most toxic thoughts about romance," and Anime Feminist's premiere review objects to jokes that Tomo is "not like other girls because she's a dude." Anime Feminist's later essay finds it rewarding on gender nonconformity but incomplete on victim-blaming. Traditional readers can see a chaste courtship with a father's consent; a conservative Christian blog (Holy Anime Wars) instead objects to "masculine women," violence and profanity. Religion is absent.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-17) set up the premise, Misuzu, Carol and the first date. Volumes 3-4 (ch. 18-31) cover a sleepover and the sports tournament. Volumes 5-6 (ch. 32-41) widen to Misaki joining the dojo, the fireworks outing, the Carol and Misaki romance and the middle-school flashback. Volumes 7-8 (ch. 42-51) bring the *Cinderella* play, Jun's confession, the duel with Gorou and the couple's beginning.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tomo tries to become "more of a girl" for Jun. What did she think a girl had to be, and was she right?
2. Jun stayed silent for years to protect the friendship. When is silence kind, and when does it hurt?
3. After Tomo is groped on the bus, Jun tells her to stop wearing skirts. What is wrong with that advice, and who is responsible for an assault?
4. Misuzu pushed Tomo to change partly so she would not lose her. Have you ever wanted a friend to stay the same, or to change, for your own sake?
5. Jun has to face Tomo's father before they can date. What does your family think a boyfriend or girlfriend should earn from parents?
