**Premise.** Rito Yuuki, a clumsy, shy high-school boy, lives with his younger sister Mikan and a growing household of alien girls. Momo, younger sister of the alien princess Lala who wants to marry Rito, launches a "harem plan": if Rito marries Lala and becomes king of the Deviluke empire, he may take many wives, Momo included (Seven Seas; Penguin Random House). Alongside the romantic comedy runs an action plot about Golden Darkness ("Yami"), an alien living weapon once hired to kill Rito who now lives peacefully on Earth. Mea, a second living weapon, and her master Nemesis try to awaken "Darkness", a destructive power inside Yami. Rito's accidental falls into the girls' bodies, which a school nurse calls "Cataplectic Indecency Syndrome" (Wikipedia character list), drive most chapters.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can something built for destruction choose a different life? Yami and Mea are living weapons; Nemesis is a being of dark matter who wants the galaxy at war. The action plot asks whether their makers' purpose is their fate.

**The answer the story gives.** No: belonging changes what a being is. Yami's "Darkness" awakens at the moment she fully accepts a peaceful life, as if her programming fights back (TV Tropes, ch. 39), and it ends when Rito reaches her (vols. 11-12). Mea breaks from her master with help from a friend, and the defeated Nemesis is given room in Rito's own body (Wikipedia). The answer is mercy and inclusion rather than justice.

**Characters as arguments.** Rito argues for gentleness: he never forces anything, and his power is that people feel safe with him. Momo argues for planning love: she wants everyone in, ordered by a royal law. Yui Kotegawa argues for rules and modesty, and the story treats her as comic. Nemesis argues that chaos is freedom; the story answers by giving her a home.

**Symbols and motifs.** "Darkness" first names Yami's hidden form and later points to Nemesis (TV Tropes). Transformation (weapons that reshape their bodies, Run/Ren's sex changes, Rito turned into a girl) runs through the book: bodies are changeable, and identity is chosen.

**Traditions it echoes.** The weapon who learns to be a person echoes Frankenstein and Pinocchio. The household of alien girls echoes Urusei Yatsura, which reviewers name as the original's model (Manga Bookshelf). The forgiveness of the enemy echoes common shōnen patterns (the defeated rival who joins the hero).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism is assumed, never examined. Communitarianism and nonviolence carry the plot. Traditional morality appears only as Yui's comic outrage. Feminist concerns about objectification are absent from the text; they appear only in critics' reactions to the original series ("sexual morals are particularly ugly", ANN Fall 2017 guide, on To Love Ru).

**Shifts across the series.** The first volumes are a romantic comedy around Momo's plan. Volumes 9-12 turn to a darker action arc around Darkness. Volumes 15-16 move to space and Nemesis. Volume 18 closes with confessions but no choice (Penguin Random House; TV Tropes). Critics note the fan service intensifies well beyond the original (ANN's Martin and Loveridge).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yami was made to kill. What lets her choose otherwise, and is it something she does or something done for her?
2. Rito forgives Nemesis, who wanted a galactic war. Is that mercy, or naivety?
3. The story treats Yui's concern about public morals as a joke. Is there anything in her view worth taking seriously?
4. How does the story treat touching without consent when it comes from a boy, and when it comes from a girl? Is that consistent?
5. Several governments restricted this book because of how it draws characters who are or look under 18. Who should decide what is acceptable in fiction, and why?
