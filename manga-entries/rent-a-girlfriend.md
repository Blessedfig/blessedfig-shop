**Premise.** Kazuya Kinoshita is a 20-year-old college student in Tokyo. His girlfriend Mami Nanami dumps him after a month. Lonely, he uses an app to rent a girlfriend for a date: Chizuru Mizuhara, a polished, popular rental girlfriend from the "Diamond" agency. Then his grandmother Nagomi is hospitalized, and he introduces Chizuru to her as his real girlfriend. It turns out Chizuru is his next-door neighbor and goes to his college under her real name, Chizuru Ichinose. Her own grandmother, Sayuri, becomes friends with Nagomi. Both families now believe the couple is real, so the two keep up a paid fake relationship (ch. 1-3). Other rental girlfriends join in: Ruka Sarashina, who demands to be his "trial" girlfriend, and the shy beginner Sumi Sakurasawa. Mami keeps trying to expose the lie. Kazuya falls for Chizuru. To make her acting dream come true before her ailing grandmother dies, he crowdfunds and produces a film starring her (ch. 102-167). The story has run for more than 400 chapters without settling the romance.

## Life message

**A good life means loving someone sincerely and showing up for them, even when the relationship started as a paid act. Lies told for others can hide a truth, but they have to be faced in the end.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | A low-status young male competes for a high-value mate. The plot keeps showing mating signals: the grandmothers push for marriage and great-grandchildren, Kazuya's spending advertises commitment, and Ruka and Mami are rival suitors. The rental market sells the *signal* of a mate (a girlfriend to show family and friends) without the bond. The story asks whether the signal can become real. |

**Where traditions disagree.** The split here falls on two things: the lie and the lust. Most religious frames object to the long deception of grandparents and parents and to sexuality played for comedy, so they grade it C or D. Stricter frames treat the fan service and ch. 218 as significant. Frames that judge by framing (Anglican, Buddhist, mainline, humanist, UU) give more weight to Kazuya's growth and the self-giving film arc. Secular progressive and feminist readers worry less about the lie and the romance. Their concern is male entitlement and the male gaze, and many defend Chizuru's right to refuse (ch. 380).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

## Creator and publisher context

- **Origin of the idea.** In a Kodansha USA interview (June 3, 2020, opened), Miyajima said he got the idea "from an online news article out of China" about renting a partner to reassure parents. He found it "dumb" but "cute." He said he did "actually 'rent' a girlfriend and even interviewed them," and that meeting one showed him "there's a 'real person' there." He said "all the models are me": Chizuru is the side of him that respects rules, and he wants her "to be cherished." His one request for the anime was that she not be too "dere." He likes dramas "where characters tell lots of lies."
- **On themes.** In an Anime Corner interview at DOKOMI, Düsseldorf (Marcel Kober, June 2026, opened), Miyajima said social media makes connections shallower, and that in Tokyo "physical closeness doesn't mean your hearts are connected." He called rental dating "a very modern way of thinking", because "if you pay, you can have a date right away." His message: "let's love anyway, in spite of everything." He called Kazuya "almost like a double" of himself and Chizuru "the ideal woman." He said he does not deliberately write in response to fans, though fan reactions may shape the story unconsciously. His immediate goal is to "finish the series."
- No statements on religion or politics were found. No public comment on the ch. 218 or ch. 380 controversies was found in the sources opened. Volume afterwords were not checked.
- **Publisher.** Kodansha publishes both the Japanese magazine and the English edition (Kodansha USA), so the 16+ rating is the publisher's own. Kodansha's K Manga app called it its best seller in May 2024 (Wikipedia). The franchise includes TMS anime, a live-action drama, games and fan crowdfunding projects (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can something real grow out of something bought? Kazuya pays for a girlfriend to cure a breakup's loneliness. Within days he is using her to deceive his grandmother. The series asks whether a performance of love, kept up for money and then for kindness, can turn into the thing itself, and what honesty is owed along the way.

**The answer the story gives.** So far, a slow and conditional yes. The paid frame never quite disappears: Chizuru keeps working as a rental girlfriend, and when Kazuya confesses she says she loves him but cannot start a relationship "right now" (ch. 380). He answers by promising to wait, and she kisses him as he leaves (ch. 385). Miyajima's own summary is "let's love anyway, in spite of everything" (Anime Corner). The story suggests that love is shown less by what one pays than by what one builds for another person. The clearest proof is the film Kazuya produces so a dying grandmother can see her granddaughter act (ch. 102-167).

**Characters as arguments.** Kazuya argues for sincere, clumsy devotion. His flaws are drawn openly: lust, self-pity, cowardice about the truth. Critics split on whether the story critiques them or indulges them (ANN's Silverman and Martin; Kotaku's Colbert; Anime Feminist's more hopeful reading). Chizuru argues for self-reliance: a professional mask over a life of loss (ch. 61, 136, 152), a vocation she will not give up, and a slow learning to accept help (ANN's Dupree). Sayuri argues that some lies protect a truth and that one must press forward (ch. 150-151). Mami argues that lies should be exposed, though her motives are possessive. Her later backstory (ch. 215) makes her a wounded person rather than only a villain. Ruka argues that love can be claimed by insistence, even by blackmail. Sumi stands for shy honesty and is the fans' favorite.

**Symbols and motifs.** The star rating stands for love as a reviewable product. Two names, Mizuhara and Ichinose, mark the mask and the person. The thin wall between neighboring apartments stands for closeness without connection. The film within the story is a fiction made to tell a truth. Kazuya's pet lungfish (ch. 41) is a creature that survives drought by waiting, and fans made it a meme ("Kazuya's fish spittin' facts").

**Traditions it echoes.** The fake-relationship romantic comedy; the shōnen harem rom-com of *Weekly Shōnen Magazine*; the backstage drama of actors and film-making; and the Japanese social novel of urban loneliness. Miyajima says the idea came from a Chinese news story about rented partners for family visits (Kodansha interview).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Consumerist logic is the premise. The story explores it without condemning it outright: rental workers are "real people" (Miyajima), and the business funds dreams. Expressive individualism is affirmed in Chizuru's choice of career over romance. Pragmatic ethics appear in Sayuri's view of protective lies. Feminist critics read Kazuya's entitlement as the series' central problem. Christian blogger Twwk reads Chizuru's undeserved care as a picture of grace. Traditional readers can point to the grandmothers' hopes for marriage and to Kazuya's single-minded fidelity.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-6 (ch. 1-49): the rental premise, grandmothers, Mami, Ruka's arrival, the beach trip, heavy sex comedy. Vol. 7-13 (ch. 50-112, likely): Chizuru's acting dream, the father's blow (ch. 55), Chizuru's family history (ch. 61), the crowdfunding plan (ch. 107). Vol. 14-20 (ch. 113-175, likely): the shoot, Sayuri's death (ch. 146-152), Chizuru's tears (ch. 164-165), the premiere (ch. 167) and Kazuya's confession (ch. 174), widely seen as the peak. Vol. 21-28 (ch. 176-247, likely): the Hawaii "Paradise" arc, with Mami exposing the lie, Kazuya's breakdown (ch. 218) and Chizuru's kiss (about ch. 230). Vol. 29-48 (ch. 248 onward, likely): Chizuru's childhood home, cohabitation (ch. 256-261), later dates, rejection (ch. 380) and a second kiss (ch. 385).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kazuya rented a girlfriend because he was lonely. What does the story suggest money can and cannot buy?
2. Was lying to the grandmothers kind or wrong? Does Sayuri's idea of "lies that protect a truth" (ch. 150) convince you?
3. Kazuya gave Chizuru years of help. Did that mean she owed him a relationship? Why did ch. 380 make so many readers angry?
4. How does the story treat Kazuya's lust and fantasies? Is the reader meant to laugh, judge or sympathize?
5. Is rental companionship honest work, a harmless fiction or a problem? What would each character say?
