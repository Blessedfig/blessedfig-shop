This page deals only in what can be measured: which part of a living thing you take, how fast it grows back, which animals depend on it, and what controlled harvest studies found. Where no study exists for our area, it says so.

1. What a harvest removes

The single best predictor of harm is which part of the organism you take. A plant or fungus that loses only its fruit lives on; one that loses its storage organ may not.

Fruit and nutsThe plant keeps its roots, stems and leaves. You compete with seed-eating and seed-spreading animals, not with the plant's survival.
MushroomsA mushroom is the spore-making part of a fungus that lives in soil or wood. The fungus itself stays underground or in the log.
Leaves and shootsThe plant survives, but loses the sugar those leaves would have stored for next year. Repeated picking weakens it.
Bulbs and rootsTaking the storage organ kills that plant or shoot. Slow-spreading perennials recover over years or decades.

2. How strong the evidence is

Each claim below carries a label:

strong a controlled field experiment or long-term monitoring of that species · moderate a study of a related species, a different region, or a model · limited general biology without a harvest study.

3. Mushrooms

Picking itself strong

The longest experiment on this ran in a Swiss forest reserve from 1975. Systematic harvesting over decades did not reduce later mushroom yields or the number of fungus species, and it made no difference whether mushrooms were pulled or cut. What did lower the number of mushrooms was trampling of the forest floor (Egli et al. 2006). A ten-year study of matsutake in the Oregon Cascades likewise found careful picking did not harm production (Luoma et al. 2006).

What this means here
Pull or cut, whichever you prefer. The measurable damage comes from feet, raking and vehicles. Walk lightly, keep to the same routes, never rake leaf litter or moss to find buttons.
Not yet studied
No harvest experiment has been run in Wisconsin forests or on most of the species on our list. The Swiss and Oregon results are the best available evidence, not a local measurement.

Mycorrhizal versus wood-rotting fungi limited

Chanterelles, king boletes, hedgehogs, black trumpets and matsutake trade nutrients with living tree roots; their fruiting depends on the trees staying alive and the soil staying undisturbed. Oysters, chicken of the woods, hen of the woods, lion's mane and turkey tail break down dead or dying wood; they depend on logs and old trees being left in the woods. Leaving downed wood where it lies keeps their food supply.

4. Ramps, fiddleheads and other greens

Wild leeks (ramps) strong

How it grows
A long-lived perennial. In northern populations new plants come mainly from the bulbs dividing, not from seed; seedling germination barely affected population growth in the Quebec study (Nault & Gagnon 1993).
Measured effect
Modeled bulb harvests of only 5 to 15 percent were enough to push northern populations into decline (Nault & Gagnon 1993). In the Great Smoky Mountains, plots needed years to recover from one-time harvests, and the authors judged about 10 percent once every 10 years sustainable for bulbs (Rock et al. 2004).
What this means here
Take one leaf per plant and leave the bulb. That keeps the storage organ and the plant. Langlade County caps wild leeks at 1 lb per person on county forest, and picking them is not allowed at all on DNR land.

Ostrich fern fiddleheads strong

How it grows
A perennial crown that spreads by underground stems along rivers and floodplains (University of Maine Extension).
Measured effect
In a four-year Maine experiment, crowns stripped of every fiddlehead fell from about 5 fiddleheads per crown to 1.4, and half died after the third year of harvest. Crowns picked once at 50 percent produced somewhat fewer but stayed close to unpicked crowns (Fuller 2020).
What this means here
Take fewer than half the fiddleheads from a crown, pick each crown only once a season, and skip crowns someone already picked. Not allowed on DNR land; allowed on county and national forest land.

Nettles, cattail shoots, watercress limited

All three spread by underground stems and regrow from them after the tops are cut. No local harvest study exists. Cutting tops above the ground, rather than pulling, leaves the regrowing part in place. Watercress is one of the few greens allowed on DNR land.

5. Berries, fruit and nuts

Who else eats them strong

In northern Wisconsin scat studies, blueberries, raspberries and gooseberries were key summer foods for black bears, and black cherries and wild sarsaparilla were important in late summer (Norton 1981; Payne et al.). Farmers in that study area reported bear damage to oat fields was highest in years when black cherries and blueberries were scarce.

What this means here
Picking fruit does not harm the shrub, but in a poor berry year your pail competes with animals that need it to fatten for winter. Pick from many bushes rather than stripping one patch, and leave more in lean years.
Seeds
Berries exist to have their seeds carried off. Birds and mammals do that spreading; a picker who eats them at home does not.

Hazelnuts and other nuts limited

Squirrels, jays, grouse and deer rely on hazelnuts and acorns going into winter. Nuts are also the plant's only way to start new shrubs. Taking a modest share from many shrubs leaves the most behind for both.

6. Chaga and other slow growers

Chaga moderate

How it grows
A parasitic fungus inside living birch; the black mass on the trunk is a sterile growth, not a mushroom.
Measured facts
On inoculated Korean birch, chaga grew less than 1 cm a year over nine years (Ryu et al. 2017). In New Hampshire's White Mountain National Forest only about 2 percent of birch trees carried a visible chaga growth, though it was present in over half of the stands surveyed, and it was found more often on older, larger trees (Brydon-Williams 2019).
What this means here
A growth may take many years to form, and few trees carry one. Take only part of a growth, leave the base on the tree, and never strip bark. County limit: 1 lb.

7. What you can carry in: invasive species

Jumping worms strong

Jumping worms (Amynthas) are a restricted invasive species under Wisconsin's rule NR 40, first found in the state in 2013; it is illegal to buy, sell, release or move them. They and their tiny cocoons travel in soil on boots, tires, tools and moved plants. Infested soil looks like coffee grounds. The DNR's rule of thumb is "arrive clean, leave clean": brush soil off boots, gear and vehicles before moving between sites (Wisconsin DNR).

What this means here
Clean boots and tires between areas, carry nothing out with soil on it, and never dig up plants to move them. Report suspected jumping worms to the DNR.

Seeds on clothes moderate

Invasive plant seeds ride on socks, pant cuffs and dog fur. Brush off before you leave a site, especially in late summer and fall when seeds are ripe.

8. Where the food comes from: disturbance and succession

Most of the berries on the map are sun plants. Raspberries, blackberries, pin cherry, juneberry and blueberries peak in the first years after logging, fire or windthrow, then fade as young trees close the canopy. Ramps, wild leeks and many mushrooms do the opposite: they need mature, shaded hardwood or conifer forest. A county forest worked on a timber rotation therefore keeps moving its berry patches around, while the maple woods and old floodplain forests hold the long-lived greens and wood-rotting fungi. Our habitat model reads this from land cover, soils, canopy and slope; it cannot see a clearcut made after 2016, so newer cuts will have more berries than the map shows.

9. Field practice checklist

  • Take fruit, nuts and mushrooms first; they cost the organism the least. strong
  • Ramps: one leaf per plant, no bulbs. strong
  • Fiddleheads: under half a crown, once a season. strong
  • Limit trampling: stay on the same paths in mushroom patches; never rake. strong
  • Spread your picking across many plants and patches instead of stripping one. moderate
  • Leave more in poor years for bears, birds and other animals. moderate
  • Arrive clean, leave clean: brush boots, tires and gear between sites. strong
  • Leave dead wood where it lies; it grows next year's oysters and chicken of the woods. limited
  • Keep notes: date, place and how much you took. Watching your own patches year to year is the only local measurement there is. limited

11. Rules and safety

Who may pick what depends on the owner: county forest allows edible plants, berries, nuts and mushrooms for personal use (with 1 lb limits on mushrooms, wild leeks and chaga); DNR land allows only fruit, nuts, mushrooms, wild asparagus and watercress; the national forest allows personal-use gathering; private land needs the owner's permission. Full table with sources on the map's Rules tab.

Identification. This guide and the map say where and when, never what a thing is. Learn each plant in person. Never eat a wild mushroom unless an experienced identifier has checked that specimen in person; some of Wisconsin's deadliest mushrooms grow in the same woods. Poison Control: 1-800-222-1222.
