**Premise.** In the far future, "the City" is a megastructure that has swallowed the Earth and much of the solar system. Long ago, humans controlled its builder robots through the Netsphere, a network that only people with a genetic marker, the Net Terminal Gene, could enter; a cult called the Order released a retrovirus that erased the gene (per the prequel NOiSE), so the Builders build without end and the Safeguard, an automated security force, kills any human without it. Killy, a silent wanderer with an immensely powerful Gravitational Beam Emitter, climbs through the layers searching for a human who still carries the gene. He is joined by Cibo, a scientist who moves between bodies; they meet the Electro-Fishers, the Safeguard agent Sanakan, the AI Mensab and many hostile cyborgs (Silicon Life). Cibo is turned into a Level 9 Safeguard and later carries a sphere holding a child made from her and Sanakan's genes; both die protecting it. Killy carries the sphere to the City's edge, where water and starlight wake it; on the last page he walks the corridors again beside a small child in a hazmat suit (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Keep going and keep protecting the fragile spark of human life, even in a vast world that no longer cares whether people exist; persistence and care for the next generation matter more than understanding everything.**

| Pragmatic | Killy says little and acts; engineering, hacking (Cibo) and firepower solve problems. Systems that keep running after their purpose is lost (the Builders, the Safeguard) are the picture of a pragmatism without human aims. |
| Religious | No religion in the main story. The catastrophe began with a cult, the Order, in the prequel (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). The Authority is god-like but is an AI (TV Tropes). Faith readers weigh a godless, decaying world against its hope in a child and its protection of the helpless. |
| Socio-cultural | A cyberpunk warning about technology and bureaucracy out of human control: a security system that kills people for lacking the right "credentials" (the gene). Opus reads it as a nightmarish counterpoint to hopeful transhumanist visions; Gizmodo stresses the self that can be uploaded and erased. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the child with the Net Terminal Gene survives and wakes at the City's edge, but Cibo, Sanakan and almost everyone Killy meets are dead, Killy is shot in the head on the last log, and the final page returns him to the same endless corridors (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). Common Sense Media says of the film that the ending undercuts its hope.
Hope source: P — the way through is protecting others: Killy guards humans who cannot protect themselves from the first log to the last, and Cibo and Sanakan give their lives to protect the sphere that holds the child (Wikipedia; TV Tropes); endurance against impossible odds (E) is the close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a human being worth in a world that no longer needs humans? The City keeps building and its guardians keep killing, long after anyone remembers why. Killy walks through it with one task: find a person who still carries the gene that would let humans take back control.

**The answer the story gives.** Enough to keep going. Blame! gives almost no speeches, but its acts are clear: Killy shields a child in the first log, Cibo and Sanakan give their lives for a sphere that holds a new child, and the ending shows Killy again beside a small child (Log 65). Human life is worth protecting even when no one can explain why, and even when the protector cannot see the result.

**Characters as arguments.** Killy argues for duty without words. Cibo argues for knowledge and sacrifice; her mind outlives several bodies. Sanakan, built to exterminate, becomes a protector and a "mother" (Wikipedia). The Safeguard is law that has lost its purpose. Silicon Life (Davine) wants the Netsphere for its own kind and destroys a world to get it. The Bio-Electric Company treats people as parts.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Megastructure and its endless corridors; the Gravitational Beam Emitter, which can pierce anything but also breaks its user's arm; the Net Terminal Gene as a lost birthright; water and starlight at the edge of the City (Log 65); the child in a hazmat suit.

**Traditions it echoes.** Cyberpunk and its fear of systems without people (Blade Runner, Ghost in the Shell), H. R. Giger's biomechanical art, and the wordless "dungeon crawl" (Thompson compares it to a video game). Religious readers may find a picture of creation subjected to futility and hope carried by a child; secular readers a defense of human life in an indifferent universe.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is explored as nightmare rather than promise (Opus). Naturalism is the world's unstated frame. Nihilism hangs over the setting but is answered by persistence. Authoritarian systems are condemned.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (Logs 1-18): Killy alone, Cibo freed from the Bio-Electric Company, the Electro-Fishers and Sanakan. Volumes 4-6 (Logs 19-37): Toha Heavy Industries, Mensab and Seu. Volumes 7-8 (Logs 38-50): Dhomochevsky, Iko and Davine; Cibo becomes the Level 9 Safeguard. Volumes 9-10 (Logs 51-65): fourteen years later, the sphere, the deaths of Cibo and Sanakan, and the edge of the City.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Killy almost never speaks. How do you learn who he is?
2. The Safeguard kills people who lack a gene. What real systems decide who counts, and how should they be checked?
3. Cibo moves her mind from body to body. Is she still the same person?
4. Sanakan begins as a killer and dies as a protector. What changed her?
5. The story ends without explaining much. Does a story need to answer its questions?
