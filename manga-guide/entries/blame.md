**Premise.** The story takes place in "the City," a gigantic megastructure of stacked levels that has swallowed the Earth and much of the Solar System. Robot Builders keep extending it at random. The machine system that once ran it, the Netsphere, can only be reached by a human who carries the "Net Terminal Gene." A prequel (NOiSE) explains that a cult wiped that gene from humanity with a retrovirus; after that, the Builders built without direction and the Safeguard, the City's security system, began killing every human who lacks the gene as an illegal resident. Killy, a nearly silent wanderer with a devastating Gravitational Beam Emitter gun, travels the levels searching for anyone with the gene. He is joined by Cibo, a scientist whose mind moves from body to body. Their enemies include the Safeguard and Silicon Life, cyborg factions who want the gene for themselves. Very little is ever explained in words.

## Life message

**Persistence matters even when the world is vast, hostile and indifferent. Humanity, however altered, is worth restoring, and hope is handed on in the form of a new life rather than won by a final victory.** (Our summary; the series states no message in words.)

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Humans survive in scattered, shrinking tribes (the Electro-Fishers) hunted by a system that treats them as intruders. The whole quest is for one genetic trait. Reproduction becomes the story's resolution: the last volume centers on an orb holding a new child with the Net Terminal Gene, made from the genes of two women, Cibo and Sanakan (fan wiki, Logs 64-65). There is no romance. |
| Pragmatic | Killy solves problems with a single weapon and endurance. Systems built to protect people (the Safeguard) keep enforcing a rule after its purpose is lost, which reads as a picture of automation and bureaucracy that no one can correct. Survival depends on scavenged tools, data backups and alliances of convenience (Toha Heavy Industries, provisional Safeguards). |
| Religious | No religion is practiced on the page in the sources opened. The Authority that runs the City is "essentially a god" in TV Tropes' description, absent and unreachable. A cult (the Order, in the prequel) causes the catastrophe. A child born from two women carrying the saving gene invites messianic readings (our framing). Religious traditions would most likely weigh the mind-uploading and body-swapping, which treat the self as data. |
| Socio-cultural | Japanese cyberpunk with Western influences (Giger, Moebius, Blade Runner, per Nihei's Wikipedia page). The individual faces a world built by no one in particular. Gender is nearly irrelevant: women (Cibo, Sanakan, Mensab) are scientists, warriors and leaders. Family life is almost absent. Death is constant and often temporary, since minds can be backed up. |

## Creator and publisher context

- Tsutomu Nihei was born in 1971 in Koriyama, Fukushima. He worked in construction, studied in New York (Parsons School of Design, listed under The New School), and was an assistant to manga artist Tsutomu Takahashi before his debut (Wikipedia, opened).
- Influences named on his Wikipedia page: Akira, Nausicaa, Ghost in the Shell, H. R. Giger, Moebius, Clive Barker, bandes dessinees, hard science fiction, Blade Runner, and the games Metroid and Halo. Wikipedia notes that his early works were mostly wordless and that huge structures recur, linking this to his construction and design background.
- On-record statements: in a 2016 Barnes & Noble interview he said New York "influenced the way I think. I [began] to see Japan from outside" (opened). No statement of intended themes, politics or religion for BLAME! was found in the pages opened. Interviews in ANN (Deb Aoki, 2016) and French outlets are cited by Wikipedia but were not opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- The film was written by Nihei and Sadayuki Murai; Polygon Pictures also adapted his Knights of Sidonia (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Japan). Vertical, which published the master edition, is now part of Kodansha USA, whose store sells the volumes (opened; ownership detail from general knowledge; to verify).
- No reputable reports of controversy involving the creator or the series were found.

**Profile notes for parents:** The series says very little and argues almost nothing. Its weight comes from mood: isolation, decay and constant violent danger. The questions it raises are about what a person is when minds can be copied and bodies replaced, and what happens when systems outlive their purpose. The violence and body horror, not any message, are what set its age level.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is left of humanity when the world it built no longer needs it? The City grows on its own. The Safeguard kills people for lacking a gene. Most of the characters are cyborgs, clones or copied minds. BLAME! asks, mostly without words, whether "human" still means anything, and whether it is worth saving.

**The answer the story gives.** The story answers with action rather than argument. Killy never explains why he searches; he simply keeps going for ten volumes. The ending does not defeat the City. Instead, the genes of Cibo and Sanakan survive in an orb that Killy carries to water and plants (fan wiki, Logs 64-65). On the last page he protects a child in a hazmat suit (TV Tropes; Wikipedia). The answer seems to be that humanity is worth continuing, even if it cannot be fully restored, and that hope passes forward through new life rather than final victory. This reading is ours; the text states none.

**Characters as arguments.** Killy is persistence without speech: he embodies the will to go on. Cibo is the scientist who remakes herself, and the story shows the price; she becomes the Level 9 Safeguard and is left an "Empty Shell" (TV Tropes). Sanakan begins as an agent of the system that kills humans and ends as Cibo's protector, an argument that even the machine's servants can turn. The Safeguard is rule without purpose: it is never cruel by choice, only relentless. Silicon Life stands for power and self-enhancement pursued for their own sake (Yokogao: "obsessed with evolution through violence").

**Symbols and motifs.** The endless megastructure stands for growth without design. The Net Terminal Gene is a key that proves who belongs. The Gravitational Beam Emitter is overwhelming force that cannot fix anything by itself. Bodies, backups and orbs stand for the self as information. Water and plants at the edge of the City stand for a return to life.

**Traditions it echoes.** Cyberpunk and gothic science fiction (Thompson calls it "one of the greatest gothic/cyberpunk settings ever drawn"). The biomechanical art of Giger and the open landscapes of Moebius, both named as influences. Lovecraftian cosmic horror (Yokogao). Older stories of a quest through a ruined land for a lost key. Benford's Great Sky River gave Killy and Cibo their names (TV Tropes).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is shown in full, with its losses: copied minds survive, but personalities can be wiped and bodies are disposable. Naturalism is the unspoken frame; nothing supernatural appears in the sources opened. Authoritarian systems are shown as murderous when cut loose from human purpose. Nihilism is the mood, but the ending resists it. Religion is almost absent, apart from the prequel's destructive cult and the remote Authority.

**Shifts across the series.** Early logs (vols. 1-2) are fragmented exploration with almost no context. The middle (vols. 3-6) brings recurring allies, the Electro-Fishers and the Toha Heavy Industries arc. The later volumes (7-10) add the provisional Safeguards, Silicon Life's bid for the Netsphere, a fourteen-year jump, and the final battle. Nihei's art style changes "dramatically" across the run (TV Tropes).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Killy almost never speaks. What do you learn about him from what he does?
2. Cibo moves into new bodies many times. Is she still the same person each time? What would your family's tradition say makes a person?
3. The Safeguard kills people for not having the right gene. Can a system be dangerous even if no one in it means harm?
4. Killy ends a suffering woman's life in Log 36. How do you judge that choice?
5. The story ends with a child, not a victory. Why might the author end it that way?
