**Premise.** Fifth-grader Arié Kimura tells her classmates a fairy tale: a girl sent by God warns a village of a monster that will destroy the world, seven villagers sacrifice her to it, and she is reborn to repeat the cycle (Wikipedia). She says the monster lives in the Nijigahara tunnel near their school, where her mother's body was found. Their teacher, Miss Sakaki, tells them of the kudan, a cow with a human face whose birth foretells disaster. Seven classmates push Arié down a well into the tunnel as a "sacrifice," the children call it an accident, and she lies in a coma. Swarms of glowing cabbage butterflies spread through the town. Told across two timelines about ten years apart, the book follows the children as damaged adults (the bully Kohta, his friend turned policeman Hayato, the jealous Maki, the suicidal transfer student Amahiko Suzuki) and the adults around them (Arié's abusive father, the blinded and bitter Sakaki, the charming, predatory Makoto) as hidden abuse, revenge and murder unfold. The last chapter reveals that Amahiko and Arié are twins (TV Tropes).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to a group of children who do something terrible together and agree to call it an accident? The book follows that question for ten years and gives a bleak answer: the act never stops happening. It repeats in their lives, their violence and their children.

**The answer the story gives.** Guilt that is buried becomes a monster. Deadshirt's reviewer puts it as the possibility that the menace is internal; the Hooded Utilitarian argues that no one in the book truly forgets. There is no confession, no justice and no healing, only the slow return of what was done. A father's remorse is the closest thing to repentance (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Arié, who tells the fable and becomes its victim, is the scapegoat, nearly voiceless, onto whom everyone projects guilt (Deadshirt). Sakaki argues for self-protection: after trying once to save a child and losing an eye, she vows never to intervene, and becomes an abuser herself. Kohta argues for love as possession and revenge. Maki argues that removing a rival wins love, and is never loved. Makoto shows evil that wears a friendly face and escapes the law. Amahiko, who longs for the world to end, learns at the end that Arié is his twin.

**Symbols and motifs.** The tunnel and the well, where the 'monster' lives and where victims are thrown. The kudan, a portent of disaster, and its twins. Glowing cabbage butterflies, multiplying through the town, which the Hooded Utilitarian ties to Zhuangzi's dream and Fantagraphics' guide leaves as a question. The umbrella Makoto carried on the day of the assault and that kills him. The necklace halves and the box. The title's holograph: an image that shifts with the angle.

**Traditions it echoes.** Lynch's Blue Velvet and Eraserhead, Slaughterhouse-Five and Time's Arrow (Thompson); The Butterfly Effect (Paste); the scapegoat ritual, which faith readers may set beside Leviticus 16 and the innocent sufferer; and Japanese folk portents.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is the book's atmosphere, explored rather than argued for: the Hooded Utilitarian borrows the line that nothing is true and everything is permitted to describe it. Postmodern play with time and reality is its method. Existential choice is present but mostly fails. The supernatural is never confirmed or denied.

**Shifts across the series.** The book has no arcs in the usual sense: its 13 chapters cut back and forth between the fifth-grade year and ten years later, and some events happen differently in different versions (Thompson). The final chapter reveals the twins and hints that the world-ending prophecy may be coming true (TV Tropes).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did the children call what they did an accident? What did keeping the secret cost them?
2. Sakaki tried once to help and was hurt. Is her vow never to get involved understandable? What did it lead to?
3. Arié's fable says the sacrificed girl is reborn and the monster grows. How does the book act out that fable?
4. Critics disagree about whether the book shows too much. What would make a dark story worth reading, and what would not?
5. What could a classmate, a teacher or a parent have done at any point to break the cycle?
