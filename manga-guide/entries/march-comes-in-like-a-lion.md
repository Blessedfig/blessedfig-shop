**Premise.** Rei Kiriyama lost his parents and little sister in a car accident as a child. His father's friend, the shogi professional Masachika Koda, took him in as a foster son and pupil, and Rei became a professional in middle school. Koda's children, Kyoko and Ayumu, resented him; Kyoko hit and insulted him (TV Tropes; fan wiki). At 17 Rei lives alone in a bare apartment in Tokyo, a 5-dan player who has started high school a year late and feels he has nothing but shogi. Across the river live the Kawamoto sisters, Akari (early 20s, who runs the household and works evenings as a hostess in her aunt's Ginza bar), Hinata (a middle schooler) and little Momo, with their grandfather, a confectioner. Akari found Rei collapsed in the street after older players got him drunk and took him home (Wikipedia). Their warm, noisy home, and fellow players such as his rival Nikaido and his mentor Shimada, slowly draw him out of depression. Major arcs include Hinata's stand against bullying at school and her own bullying (vols. 5-7), the return of the sisters' father, Seijirou, whom Rei exposes (vols. 10-11), Rei's sudden declaration that he means to marry Hinata (ch. 104), Kyoko's realization that she treated Rei unfairly (ch. 139), and Rei and Hinata becoming a couple (kiss, ch. 171) (fan wiki; TV Tropes; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life is rebuilt through other people: grief and depression are not cured by talent or effort alone, but by letting others in, being cared for and caring in return, and finding a family where you can belong.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | An orphan without kin is adopted twice: first into a rival-filled foster home, then into the Kawamoto household, which feeds and shelters him. Status competition is constant (shogi ranks, sibling rivalry for a father's attention). Pair-bonding comes slowly and late (Rei and Hinata, ch. 104, 171). Seijirou, the father who left his children and other women, is the story's clearest failed parent. |
| Pragmatic | Shogi is work: ranks, salaries and promotions are counted, and Rei pays his own rent. Outlook: Hopeful — a boy who believed he had nothing but shogi ends (so far) with a family who calls his house home, friends, a better ranking and a girlfriend, and even the foster sister who hurt him wishes him happiness (ch. 139).
Hope source: F — the story's way through is found family and a support network: the Kawamoto home, Nikaido, Shimada and his teacher carry Rei, and Rei and the Kawamotos carry Hinata through her bullying (AniList tags "Found Family"; Anime Feminist: "our burdens can always become a little more bearable with the support of the people that care about us").

## Creator and publisher context

- Chica Umino (pen name; born August 30, 1965, Adachi, Tokyo) is a manga artist and illustrator, best known for Honey and Clover (2000-2006). She drew character designs for Eden of the East and Fate/Grand Order (Wikipedia).
- Why shogi: her editor suggested shogi or boxing for her next work; she had no shogi experience, so a professional supervises the games (Wikipedia). She said this was "a story based on researching and hearing various stories about worlds [she] didn't know," unlike Honey and Clover, and that she chose something different so a failure would not mark her a "one-hit wonder" (Wikipedia, citing interviews). She agreed to an anime only if Akiyuki Shinbo and Shaft made it (Wikipedia).
- Her own experience, on record (FRaU interview with Go Yoshida, March 23, 2017, opened): she says she was left out in kindergarten and played alone in the sandbox; she had three hard years at an industrial high school's design course where she did not fit in, and an art teacher supported her; and she describes herself as someone who "never dressed fashionably, had no friends and never went to art college," who was hurt when Honey and Clover was labeled a "fashionable manga." She does not, in what was opened, tie these memories directly to Rei or Hinata.
- Western children's books such as Anne of Green Gables shaped how she portrays unusual families (Wikipedia, citing a 2019 Takara-bako report). In 2013 she was hospitalized and the series paused (Wikipedia). In vol. 17 (2023) she said the story had entered its final stretch (Wikipedia).
- No statements on religion or politics were found. A Japan Media Arts Festival interview is listed as "no longer available." **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (especially her statements on the bullying arc and on Kyoko).
- Publisher: Hakusensha (Young Animal). English: Denpa, a small independent publisher; Denpa's page lists no age rating (opened).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a person need to go on living after losing everything? Rei lost his family as a child, was taken into a home where he felt he had stolen a place, and left it as soon as he could. At 17 he has a profession, a salary and an empty apartment. The series asks whether talent and work are enough to live on, and answers no.

**The answer the story gives.** People are needed. Akari picks Rei up from the street; the Kawamotos feed him; Nikaido insists on being his friend; Shimada invites him to a study group; a teacher eats lunch with him. Anime Feminist's Marion Bea puts it plainly: shogi "merely helped him survive," while care made his burdens "a little more bearable." The story then turns the gift around. When Hinata is bullied for standing by a friend (from ch. 52), Rei, who was bullied as a child, finds that he can be the one who helps. Later he uses his own skills to research and expose the father who abandoned the sisters (vol. 10-11). Being needed is part of the healing.

**Characters as arguments.** Rei is the case that loneliness is a wound, not a choice. Hinata is the case for moral courage: she says she will never regret defending Chiho, because she knows she was not wrong (ch. 52, fan wiki). Akari is the cost of care: a young woman who mothers her sisters and works nights. Kyoko is the argument about neglect: a girl who wanted her father's love, hated the boy who got his attention, abused him, and attached herself to a married older man; she finally admits to herself that Rei "just wanted to be part of" a family (Wikipedia; ch. 139). Seijirou is the counter-example to every caring adult: charming, selfish and unrepentant. Shimada shows that a plain, patient person can be great.

**Symbols and motifs.** Water: Rei's depression as drowning, "Black River" (ch. 25-26), and the river and bridge between his apartment and the Kawamoto home (Wikipedia). The cuckoo, a bird raised in another's nest (ch. 10, "Over the Cuckoo's Nest"). The title's proverb, "March comes in like a lion and goes out like a lamb," which Senzaki tied to the promotion season (Wikipedia). Food and the seasons: the Kawamoto kitchen, festivals and confectionery mark time and warmth. Cats, as comic chorus.

**Traditions it echoes.** The orphan's tale (Anne of Green Gables, which Umino names as an influence on her unusual families); the Japanese master-disciple story; the sports manga of rivals and ranks, slowed down into a character study. Christian readers have used it to talk about hope (Beneath the Tangles).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian and virtue lenses are shown working. Secular humanist readers find a picture of healing through human care and, for Chiho, through treatment. Feminist readers find Kyoko's lost career and Akari's burden quietly noted rather than argued. Religious frames find no hostility to faith and weigh mainly the affair, alcohol and the age gap in the romance.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4: Rei's loneliness, the Kawamotos, Nikaido, Shimada and Kyoko. Vols. 5-7: Hinata's bullying arc. Vols. 8-9: matches, the Kawamoto family's past and Rei's growth at school. Vols. 10-11: Hinata enters Rei's school; Seijirou returns and is exposed; Rei's sudden marriage declaration (ch. 104). Vols. 12-16: shogi tournaments, Kyoko's turn (ch. 139), and Rei and Hinata as a couple (ch. 171). Vols. 17-18 and later: not covered by sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rei is good at shogi and earns his own living. Why is that not enough for him?
2. Hinata says she will never regret defending Chiho. What did it cost her, and what would you have done?
3. Kyoko hurt Rei for years. What do you think made her that way, and does her later admission change how you see her?
4. Why do the sisters refuse to take their father back? Is that forgiveness withheld, or something else?
5. Rei announces he wants to marry Hinata when she has just started high school. How does the story treat that, and how does your family think about age and timing in a relationship?
