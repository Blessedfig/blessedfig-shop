**Premise.** Arata Kaizaki is 27. He quit his first job after three months, keeps failing interviews and works part-time at a convenience store. His parents are about to stop sending him money. A researcher, Ryō Yoake, offers him a place in the ReLIFE experiment. A pill makes him look 17, and he spends one year as a transfer student at a high school while the company pays his living costs. He makes friends, helps classmates through their problems and slowly faces why he left his job: his senior colleague, Michiru Saiki, was harassed by coworkers and died by suicide. When the year ends, the other students' memories of him will be erased. He falls for a socially awkward classmate, Chizuru Hishiro, who turns out to be a test subject too.

## Life message

**A good life is not lost when you fall out of society. You can restart it by facing your past honestly, letting others in, and helping the people beside you, even when it hurts.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Yayoiso (夜宵草) is a pen name. Japanese Wikipedia says the story draws on the creator's own experience. In a 2017 interview (animeanime.jp, opened), Yayoiso said they left the first company they joined after three months and were deeply discouraged, and that this experience made ReLIFE possible. They wanted to show dropping out of society not as shame but "positively, as a restart in life" (人生の再スタートとしてポジティブに). The high school setting came from asking what helps a NEET return to society: routine, a group and thinking about the future.
- Japanese Wikipedia: the creator hoped it could be enjoyed by both students and working adults; classroom seating shows psychological distance (Oita Godo Shimbun interview); main surnames come from Oita Prefecture station names, and the anime's school draws on the creator's alma mater in Oita.
- In a 2025 Oricon interview about a later series (Secret Star Live, LINE Manga), Yayoiso said their creative mind tends toward negative ideas and their editor often asks them to be more positive. Later works: Blue Hearts (Comico, 2018; ANN), Tsugihagi Mirai (LINE, 2023; ANN headline, not opened), Secret Star Live (2025).
- No statements on religion or politics found. No personal controversies found.
- Publishers: Comico was run by NHN Japan (part of South Korea's NHN group) (ANN; from general knowledge for the parent group). Print by Earth Star Entertainment.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person who has "failed" at adult life start again, and what does starting again require? Kaizaki is 27, jobless and ashamed. The story asks whether his life is over or only interrupted.

**The answer the story gives.** It answers that a restart is possible, but not by forgetting. Kaizaki must face why he left his job: his senior, Michiru Saiki, was harassed by coworkers and died by suicide, and his attempt to defend her made it worse. The story does not make him a hero for quitting, nor a coward. It shows the cost. On the anniversary of her death he visits her grave and tells a younger worker that the black company was "a living death" (Lost in Anime, ep. 11). The creator said the aim was to present dropping out "positively, as a restart in life" (animeanime.jp, 2017).

**Characters as arguments.** Kaizaki argues that experience and humility can help others even when you are still hurting. Hishiro argues the opposite of the black company's lesson: she did everyone's work without asking and was fired for it (fan wiki), and she must learn that cooperation matters more than efficiency. Ohga's brother (ch. 165) stands for the people who work hard and break. Yoake stands for patient support. Saiki, never on stage in the present, is the argument that diligence alone, without help, can kill.

**Symbols and motifs.** The necktie is the clearest symbol: Kaizaki cannot bear it tight, and his hand goes to his throat in memory of Saiki (fan wiki; TV Tropes). The blue pill is the second chance. Writing on the palm (ch. 216) is the attempt to hold on to love against erasure. Classroom seating shows emotional distance, the creator said (Japanese Wikipedia). Fireworks stand for fleeting youth (Lost in Anime).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese "do-over" school story; workplace dramas about black companies; the Buddhist sense of impermanence in fading memory; Western second-chance stories. Christian writers at Beneath the Tangles have read it through Luke 10 and the command to love one's neighbor.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue, friendship and community are shown as the path to healing. Despair and nihilism are where Kaizaki starts and are overcome. Critiques of exploitative labor and workplace sexism are sincere. Utilitarian reasoning lies inside the premise: the company erases memories because subjects and classmates are "not harmed" if they never know. Beneath the Tangles' guest writer rejects that: "the morality of an action is not contingent on whether the victim knows he's been harmed." The story itself softens the problem with a happy reunion.

**Shifts across the series.** The early reports (about 1-50, likely) are workplace-weary comedy about an adult in school. The middle (about 50-110) adds classmates' problems and Kaizaki's past, peaking at the grave visit (ch. 89-92, likely). From Report 111 onward, the reveal that Hishiro is a subject shifts it toward romance and the coming memory erasure (ch. 155, 186, 216). The ending (ch. 221-222 and the vol. 15 epilogue) turns fully hopeful.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kaizaki's attempt to help Saiki made things worse. When does helping someone need their permission?
2. Is quitting a harmful job a failure or a brave choice? What does your family believe?
3. The company erases people's memories "for their own good." Is that ever right?
4. Ohga hides his brother's struggles because of shame. Why do families hide mental health problems, and what helps?
5. Hishiro was good at work but lost job after job. What does the story say matters besides being efficient?
