**Premise.** Neneko Satō, a clumsy spy raised from childhood by her organization, goes undercover as the girlfriend of Omi Amai, the top assassin of a rival organization, to learn his weaknesses. He unmasks her at once but, instead of killing her, proposes a marriage of convenience: she can keep "spying" on him, report harmless leaks to her bosses and stay alive, and he gets the woman he adores (vol. 1). They marry and move in together; he cooks, shops and pampers her, takes her to meet his coworkers, and invites her to a wedding night; his boss suspects her, a party turns into a gunfight, Omi is hurt shielding her, and the boss holds Neneko at gunpoint (vol. 1; ciatr). Her handler orders her to use a honeymoon to break him (vol. 2). Omi asks her to quit and live as an honest couple; she hesitates, because the organization was the only family an orphan had; he shows her the orphanage where he and his colleague Setsu grew up, and it emerges that he and Neneko met years before (vol. 3). Her handler and adoptive father, Himuro, now treats her as Omi's weak point, plants an agent at her day job (vol. 4) and has her kidnapped to destroy Omi (vol. 5); volume 6 brings a rescue (Square Enix synopses; Siliconera; Japanese store blurb).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a person worth when she is no longer useful? Neneko has been raised to believe that a spy who fails is thrown away. Omi's proposal turns the question around: he wants her not for what she can do for him, but because she is herself.

**The answer the story gives.** Love that asks nothing in return is what makes a person free. As Omi pampers and protects her, Neneko comes to accept being loved as she is (ciatr; Siliconera), and by vol. 3 the choice is between the organization that "raised" her and an honest life together. The story does not ask whether Omi's own work is wrong.

**Characters as arguments.** Neneko argues the case of the used person who learns her worth. Omi argues for devotion, but also, as reviewers notice, for a possessive, overwhelming love that ANN's Silverman finds hard to read and Anime UK News finds pushy. Himuro, handler and adoptive father, is the case for people as instruments. Setsu, who escaped the orphanage with Omi's help, shows what Omi is willing to sacrifice for those he loves (vol. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** Cinderella: a mistreated girl rescued by a prince, here a killer; the midnight bell in the vol. 6 blurb, marking the couple's stolen time. Cooking and gifts as love; the wedding dress Otaku USA describes as made of roses; the orphanage both leads came from.

**Traditions it echoes.** The spy-marriage comedy (Spy x Family, Mr. & Mrs. Smith) and the dangerous-lover romance of josei and shōjo manga; Cinderella and fairy-tale rescue. For Christian readers, the theme of a worth that is given rather than earned echoes grace; most faiths would ask more of the hero than the story does.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and a freedom from controlling groups are affirmed. Collectivism of the organization is the villain. Hedonism is the texture: gifts, food, holidays, fan service. Might makes right is present but unexamined, since Omi's power is always used for Neneko.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 are a sweet and sexy romantic comedy with spy trappings. Volume 3 turns to backstory and the organization's menace (Siliconera). Volumes 4-6 bring infiltration, kidnapping and rescue, with intimacy between the spouses continuing.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Neneko thinks she is only worth what she can do. Where do people get that idea, and how does Omi change it?
2. Is Omi's love romantic, controlling or both? What would a healthy version look like?
3. The story never asks whether Omi should stop killing. Should it?
4. Can a marriage that starts as a deal become a real marriage?
5. Why would someone stay loyal to a group that uses them?
