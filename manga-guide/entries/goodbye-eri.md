**Premise.** For his birthday Yuta Ito gets a smartphone, and his terminally ill mother asks him to film her last days and make a movie from them. After she dies he screens the film at school; it ends with him running from a hospital as it explodes, and classmates and a teacher condemn it as disrespectful. Bullied, Yuta goes to the hospital roof to kill himself and is stopped by Eri, a girl who loved the film and pushes him to make another. The two watch films together and shoot a half-documentary in which Eri plays a vampire. Eri collapses and turns out to be terminally ill too. Yuta's father, urging him to finish, reveals that Yuta's mother was abusive to her son and husband and that the first film was her attempt to be remembered well. Yuta finishes the film before Eri dies; it is praised. One of Eri's friends tells him Eri was not the person he edited, and both agree they prefer his version. Years later the adult Yuta loses his wife, child and father in a car accident and returns to the old screening room to end his life, where he finds Eri alive, a real vampire whose memories reset, who has kept his film to remember him. He chooses to live, walks away, and the building explodes behind him, leaving open how much was real (Wikipedia; AniList; reviews).

## Life message

**Grief, love and memory are all things we partly make up, and that is not a lie but a way to go on: a person can choose to live, and remembering someone at their best, while knowing it is edited, can be an act of love.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** When someone dies, what do we owe their memory: the truth, or the version that lets us love them? Yuta is handed a phone and asked by his dying mother to make her film. Only later does he learn that the woman he filmed was abusive, and that the film was her way of controlling how she would be remembered.
**The answer the story gives.** It does not choose between truth and kindness. Yuta and Eri's friend admit his film is not the real Eri, and both choose to keep it anyway (Wikipedia). The book suggests that all memory is edited, and that editing with love, while knowing you are doing it, is different from editing to deceive. Its clearer answer is about living: twice Yuta comes to the edge of suicide, and twice he walks away.
**Characters as arguments.** Yuta argues that grief can be worked through by making something. Eri argues that fiction is a way to be remembered and to survive, and her plan (answer mockery with a better film) is the book's alternative to revenge. The mother is the dark mirror: she also used film to shape memory, but for herself. The teacher and the grieving classmate argue for respect for the dead, and the book lets them be right about the first film's cruelty. The father argues for truth told late but in love.
**Symbols and motifs.** The phone camera and the first-person framing (WWAC; ANN on its found-footage style). The explosion that ends both the first film and the book. The vampire whose memory keeps resetting and who keeps a film to remember who she should be. The screening room where the two marathon movies.
**Traditions it echoes.** Satoshi Kon's films about memory and acting, and Slaughterhouse-Five's editing (WWAC); the found-footage film; memento mori. For Buddhist readers it echoes attachment and letting go; for Jewish readers, zikaron (sacred memory); for Christian readers, honest grief without, in this book, the hope of resurrection.
**How each lens is portrayed.** Postmodernism is explored, not preached: the book plays with reality but lands on a human choice to live. Existentialism is affirmed: meaning is made, not found. Expressive individualism is affirmed in the craft of filmmaking. Nihilism, the despair after loss, is what the story turns away from.
**Shifts across the series.** One volume: the mother's death and the first film (opening third), Eri and the second film (middle), Eri's death and the friend's confession, then the time skip with the adult Yuta's losses and the reunion (final pages).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yuta's first plan was to make his bullies remember him. What did Eri offer instead?
2. Was the teacher right to object to the first film? Was Yuta right to make it?
3. Yuta and Eri's friend choose to remember Eri as he edited her. Is that honest?
4. What did Yuta's father risk by telling the truth about Yuta's mother?
5. The book never says what was real at the end. What do you think, and why does it matter or not?
