**Premise.** Nagata Kabi, in her late twenties, has just published My Lesbian Experience with Loneliness, an honest comic about her depression, her sexuality and her visit to a women's escort service, and it is a hit. This sequel is written as letters to her past and future self. In vol. 1 (ch. 1-12) her parents read the first book and her mother scolds her; she tries several times to move out and finally gets her own apartment; she checks negative reviews compulsively, makes friends online, returns to the escort service where the hugging alone helps her (Manga Bookshelf), and goes on a nervous first date with a woman (Publishers Weekly). In vol. 2 (ch. 13-24) she moves back home for a time and comes to understand her mother better, then begins drinking heavily, to the point of wetting the bed, and checks herself into a hospital, where she cuts herself again (Manga Bookshelf). She leaves the hospital, publishes her first original (fiction) manga, and decides to end the public diary; an epilogue asks what it means to package her life for readers, and she finally receives a long-awaited hug (The Lesbrary). Chapters 12 and 18 describe suicide attempts (Queer Comics Database).

## Creator and publisher context

- Nagata Kabi (pen name; born May 28, 1987) began reading manga in fourth grade with Slam Dunk and names Rurouni Kenshin as her middle-school favorite (Wikipedia). Her autobiographical books continue with My Alcoholic Escape from Reality (2019), My Wandering Warrior Existence (2020), My Wandering Warrior Eating Disorder (2021) and My Pancreas Broke, but My Life Got Better (2022); Seven Seas licensed My Sketchy Roommate in January 2026 (Wikipedia).
- On-record method: she writes events and feelings as a bulleted list and rearranges them into a story, and tries neither to lower nor to glorify herself (Wikipedia, on the first book).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do after the thing you hoped would save you happens and you still feel the same? Nagata's first book was a success, but success brought her parents' disapproval, online critics and the same loneliness. The diary asks how a person goes on when there is no single cure.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep going in small steps, and stop waiting for someone else to make you whole. Leaving home, admitting she needs a hospital, accepting a friend's help and seeing her mother as a person are the real progress. The book does not promise she will be fine; Women Write About Comics notes there is no big cathartic release, and the diary ends because she chooses to stop publishing it.

**Characters as arguments.** Nagata argues with herself: one self who drinks and one who cuts, as the vol. 2 cover shows (Manga Bookshelf). Her mother stands for duty and appearances and is gradually understood. The imagined 'capable Nagata' stands for perfectionism and is let go. Friends and the escort service stand for touch and care that she did not get at home.

**Symbols and motifs.** The exchange diary, normally shared between friends, kept with herself. The pink and grey palette. The rope around her heart. The hug: physical comfort as a turning point, both at the escort service (vol. 1) and at the end (vol. 2).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese comic essay and the confessional memoir; in older traditions, the lament psalm and the spiritual journal, though the book is secular. Its stress on seeing the parent as also suffering echoes Buddhist compassion; its stress on independence echoes Western individualism.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and secular humanism are affirmed: she must become herself and find help among people. Confucian family duty is explored with growing sympathy. Traditionalism is the pressure she resists. No faith lens is present.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-12): the success of the first book, her parents' reaction, moving out, the escort-service visit, the first date, a suicide attempt described in ch. 12. Vol. 2 (ch. 13-24): back home for a time, a better understanding of her mother, heavy drinking, a suicide attempt described in ch. 18, hospital, the first original manga and the end of the diary, plus an epilogue and extras.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nagata thought her first book's success would fix things. Why didn't it?
2. What does her mother want for her, and why do they keep hurting each other?
3. Why does a hug help her more than advice?
4. What warning signs come before her drinking leads to hospital?
5. If a friend felt like Nagata, who could they talk to? (Parents may want to share a crisis line such as 988 in the US.)
