**Premise.** Touka Scott, a 17-year-old radish farmer in Cheza village with a crude, lecherous streak, digs spiked pit traps against demons. The returning Legendary Hero, Sion Breydan, falls into one and dies (ch. 1). Sion's companion Anri Haysworth, a 14-year-old necromancer, puts Touka's soul into Sion's corpse so the world still has a hero, keeping Touka's own body as a hostage (fan wiki; Wikipedia). With his childhood friend Yuna Eunice (17), Touka fakes being Sion: he fends off Sion's jealous former partner Kyle (ep. 1-2), rescues the princess Marguerite Farom (16) from a doctor who turns people into skeletons (ep. 3-4), and gathers allies including his old survival teacher Ethel and the adult necromancer Diana Filion (from ch. 63). The story darkens: necromancers are humans born in Hell and persecuted on earth; King Larcvalt of Farom is a thousand-year-old necromancer who killed the gods, eats human souls and takes over his descendants' bodies; and Sion is revealed to have let himself die in the trap after losing his sister and friends and learning truths he could not fix (fan wiki). Sion's spirit guides Touka from the Sacred Sword. After Larcvalt's defeat and a four-year time skip, Touka is back in his own body and has accepted the hero's role (fan wiki).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a hero: being chosen, or choosing? The real hero dies in the first chapter, and a coward with no gifts has to wear his body.

**The answer the story gives.** Heroism is taken up, not given. Touka keeps his crude, selfish habits, but his willingness to do anything for the people he loves makes him the hero the chosen one could not finish being. Sion's secret shows the cost of the "perfect hero" image: he hid despair until it killed him (fan wiki).

**Characters as arguments.** Touka argues that results and loyalty matter more than chivalry. Sion argues for selfless duty, and his collapse shows its limit when no one shares the burden. Anri argues that outcasts can be good, and learns mercy. Larcvalt argues that power justifies any lie, and loses. Friedrich argues for his own people at humanity's cost, a villain the story partly understands.

**Symbols and motifs.** The pit trap (accident, guilt and cunning); the borrowed body, rotting until Touka grows into it; the Sacred Sword that carries Sion's spirit; Hell's Gate, sealed imperfectly out of love for a friend.

**Traditions it echoes.** Parodies of the chosen-hero quest (from general knowledge; to verify); the trickster hero (Odysseus, Jack and the giant); stories of a burden handed on. Christian readers may see the calling of the unlikely; necromancy and the lechery humor sit hard with most faith traditions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Occult power is the furniture of the world and is used by the heroes; hedonism is the comedy; virtue grows; tyranny is defeated. The persecution of necromancers is the story's most serious social theme.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-6 (anime): comic cover-up, Kyle, the skeleton doctor, the princess. Vols. 6-12: necromancer arcs, Diana, Ethel and the hero's past (to verify). Vols. 13-20: the king's truth, Sion's secret, war and the four-year time skip. Sequel (2 vols.): a new adventure.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sion seemed perfect but hid his despair. Why do people hide that, and what could his friends have done?
2. Touka stays crude and selfish in many ways. Can someone be a hero and still have big flaws?
3. People treat necromancers as monsters. Is that fair? Who gets treated that way in real life?
4. The story jokes about stripping an enemy. Is it funny? Would it be if she were your friend?
5. Touka never wanted to be a hero. What made him keep going?
