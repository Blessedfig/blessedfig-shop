**Premise.** England is torn by the Wars of the Roses. Richard, third son of the Duke of York, is intersex; his deeply religious mother Cecily calls him a demon child and his secret is known only to his father and his servant Catesby. Richard lives for his father's cause, is haunted by visions of Joan of Arc and, in the forest, falls for a gentle shepherd who is really the Lancastrian king Henry VI, a devout man who hates war (v1-3). His father is humiliated and executed by Queen Margaret (v2); Richard turns to vengeance as his brother Edward IV takes the throne (v3), and is drawn into the schemes of Warwick, Prince Edward of Lancaster and Anne Neville (v4-7). When he learns who his shepherd was, Richard stabs Henry in the Tower at the end of act one (v7; Japanese Wikipedia; ANN, vol. 8), though Henry secretly survives and returns as the assassin James Tyrrell (TV Tropes; fan wiki). In the second act, ten years on, Richard allies with the ambitious Duke of Buckingham, who learns his secret and loves him; together they destroy George and the Woodvilles and make Richard king (v9-13). Richard marries Anne and raises her son as his own; rumors about his body, a pregnancy by Buckingham (TV Tropes) and Buckingham's rebellion tear his reign apart (v14-16), and Richard dies fighting Henry Tudor at Bosworth (v17) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; VIZ and Ki-oon blurbs).

## Life message

**Hatred, revenge and the hunger for a crown destroy everyone they touch, yet even a person told from birth that he is a monster can love, be loved and choose to protect others to the end.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A war over bloodlines: legitimacy, heirs and who fathered whom drive the plot (Edward IV's heir, v6; the true father of Anne's son, v14). Richard's body sets him outside the rules of mating and succession, which is the source of both his danger and his rivals' fascination. |
| Pragmatic | Schemes win battles (Richard's fog tactics, likely v6-7; Buckingham's staged "play" for the crowd, v13) but every gain costs a loved one; by the end the practical calculus of power leaves Richard alone (v17). |
| Religious | Religion is everywhere: Cecily's fanatical faith condemns Richard as a demon (Wikipedia); Henry VI is a devout, praying king who refuses to kill (fan wiki); witches and curses (Jane Shore's potions, v8-9) and the ghost of Joan of Arc torment Richard. Faith readers will see a harsh religion as one cause of Richard's suffering and a gentle one in Henry, set against sexual sin, murder and occult imagery. |
| Socio-cultural | A shojo retelling that makes Shakespeare's "deformed" villain an intersex outsider (CBR). Anime Feminist finds his anger at being denied as a man deeply resonant, but warns the threat of exposing his body is overused and "fetishy," and that scheming women are drawn more harshly than men. ANN's reviewer also notes his intersex body can be exploited by the story (vol. 8). |

Outlook: Bittersweet, near bleak — Richard dies at Bosworth after losing Henry, Buckingham, Anne and his throne (v16-17), but he chooses to save his son by sending him away in secret (v17, Ki-oon blurb), Henry is alive under another name, and Richard meets his end standing for what he loves.
Hope source: P — what hope there is lies in protecting others: Richard fakes his son Edward's death so the boy can live (v17), and Catesby stays loyal to the end; love between two (L) is the story's other light, in Henry and in Buckingham.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every tradition can read the series as a warning about vengeance, ambition and rumor, and most honor Henry VI's mercy and Catesby's loyalty. They split over Richard himself: progressive, secular, Reform and Unitarian readers welcome a sympathetic intersex lead and a story that faults religious cruelty (B), while most Christian, Jewish, Muslim and Latter-day Saint readings weigh the sympathetic same-sex romances, sexual content and occult powers (C or D). Buddhist readers find a clear picture of craving and hatred. Anime Feminist's caution about the handling of Richard's body is shared, for different reasons, by traditional frames concerned about sexual content.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Almost no one wins. Those who seize power by schemes and murder (Richard, Buckingham, Elizabeth, Edward IV) lose it or die; the gentle (Henry) loses everything but his life, the innocent (Anne, the princes) are destroyed, and the cold schemer Henry Tudor takes the crown.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is a person born a monster, or made one? Richard's mother calls him a demon from birth; Shakespeare made him a villain; Kanno asks what it would be like to be that child, and whether fate or his own longings lead him to ruin (vol. 1 copy).

**The answer the story gives.** Both, and neither. The world gives Richard every reason to hate it, and he chooses vengeance and power, but he also loves Henry, Anne's son and Buckingham truly. The tragedy is that his love and his ambition destroy each other. At the end he chooses to protect his son and dies fighting (v17); Henry, whom he thought he had killed, has lived on in the shadows.

**Characters as arguments.** Henry VI argues for meekness: he hates war and would rather be a shepherd, and the world breaks him. Cecily argues for a religion of purity and judgment, and the story treats her as the first cause of Richard's pain. Buckingham argues for love joined to ambition; Catesby for quiet loyalty without reward. Richmond is power without love.

**Symbols and motifs.** The white and red roses; thorns and briars (anime opening titles); the forest as a place of freedom; the shepherd; Joan of Arc as an accusing double of Richard; masks and plays (Buckingham's staged crowd scene, v13; Richmond the actor).

**Traditions it echoes.** Shakespeare's Henry VI, Part 3 and Richard III; gothic romance; the shojo tradition of gender-crossing heroes (The Rose of Versailles is a common comparison; from general knowledge; to verify). Readers of Ricardian history will notice the princes' murder is moved onto Stanley and Richmond (v15 blurb).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right is shown to devour everyone. Christian faith appears in two faces, cruel (Cecily) and gentle but powerless (Henry). Pacifism is honored and pitied. Progressive readers find a rare intersex lead (CBR) and a sharp critique of rejection, inside a story that also uses his body as a source of danger (Anime Feminist).

**Shifts across the series.** Act one (ch. 1-31, v1-7) is the war with Lancaster, Richard's love for Henry and his revenge. Act two (ch. 32-78, v8-17), ten years later, is court intrigue: George's fall, the purge of the Woodvilles, the crown, Buckingham, rumors and Bosworth (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Cecily believes her son is a demon. How does that belief shape who Richard becomes?
2. Henry VI refuses to fight. Is he a coward, a saint or both?
3. Richard kills the person he loved most. Why?
4. Rumors bring Richard down. How do rumors work in our own lives?
5. Compare this Richard with Shakespeare's. Which do you believe, and why?
