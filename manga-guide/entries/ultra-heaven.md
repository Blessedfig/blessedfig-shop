**Premise.** In a near future where any mood is "a pump away," mind-altering drugs are legal, advertised on television and mixed to order in "pump bars" by bartenders with medical licenses; only a few drugs are restricted, policed by hygiene offices (MangaUpdates; Last Gasp; The Comics Journal). Kabu (Cub), a small-time dealer and heavy user with suicidal tendencies, is bored of his favorite euphoric, "Peter Pan," and mixes dangerous combinations to chase near-death highs. A bar's master gives him "Nova Express," a drink that simulates dying, while two men in suits note he would be a perfect subject for an experiment. After a failed attempt to quit, a walleyed pusher in a park gives him a free dose of something new. Kabu names it "Ultra Heaven": in the trip he cannot separate his body from objects, kills two sanitation officers, is shot in the head, wakes as an overdose patient whose brain seems to be dying, flies over a mountain paradise and wakes in a park pond with the pusher laughing (vol. 1; Wikipedia). In vol. 2 he is still hallucinating hours later and turns to his friend Sally, who uses an "amp," a device that changes brain waves; vol. 2 also gives Kabu's backstory (Last Gasp; AniList review). In vol. 3 Sally suggests the Drome, a shared virtual world built from its users' thoughts, as a way to find the source of his visions, or a way to "Heaven" (Last Gasp vol. 3; Barnes & Noble listing). The story stops there.

## Life message

**Chasing a perfect, manufactured heaven dissolves the line between dream and life until there is no solid self left to come home to; the unfinished story asks what is real without settling it.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | The reward system hijacked: tolerance builds, pleasure centers burn out (The Comics Journal), and each high needs a stronger one. Kabu has no partner, family or work in the summaries; the drive for pleasure has replaced every other drive. |
| Pragmatic | Pharmacology has replaced psychiatry: bartenders prescribe moods, and a few drugs are banned by hygiene offices with sweeping powers. The story shows the practical results: overdose, crime in a trip, relapse after trying to quit, and a person who can no longer tell whether he is awake. |
| Religious | No religion appears in sources. The title and the trips borrow the language of heaven and near-death experience: a drink that simulates dying, an out-of-body view of one's own body, a mountain paradise (vol. 1). The Comics Journal notes the book raises the idea that drug visions may be glimpses of an afterlife. Faith readers will see a counterfeit transcendence, sought in a substance or a machine. |
| Socio-cultural | A dystopia of legal, advertised drugs. MangaUpdates' summary says the "Pompies" movement won legalization as a liberating change, yet nothing improved; Publishers Weekly reads the city as one where people are sedated into compliance. The Comics Journal places it with Philip K. Dick and A Scanner Darkly: possible government experiments on users. |

Outlook: Bleak — the hero sinks further into hallucination volume by volume, shadowy experimenters watch him, and the series stops without an answer or a way back.
Hope source: N — the story, as far as it goes, finds little hope: the only 'heaven' on offer is a drug, a brain-wave device or a virtual world, and none is shown working. Readers may draw a warning from it, but no character finds a way out in the three published volumes.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is real, and does it matter if a dream feels truer than life? Kabu lives for the next high, and when a stranger hands him a drug whose dream, the pusher claims, is truer than waking life (vol. 1, Wikipedia's summary), he loses the ability to tell the difference.

**The answer the story gives.** None, yet. The three volumes move from drugs (vols. 1-2) to brain-wave devices and a shared virtual world (vol. 3), each promising a way to 'Heaven' or to the source of the visions. The series stops before any answer. What it shows is that each new door leads further from solid ground.

**Characters as arguments.** Kabu is the argument from desire: if the high is the best thing, why not live in it? The pusher argues that the dream is truer than waking life and that the only cure for a bad trip is another trip. The pump-bar master argues for controlled, respectable use, and the story's world shows where that leads. Sally, with her amps and the Drome, argues that technology can do better than chemicals. The men in suits and the hygiene offices are the system watching.

**Symbols and motifs.** The pump. Eyes, bloodshot and swelling (The Comics Journal). Nova Express, named after a William Burroughs novel (The Comics Journal). The mountain paradise and the park pond where Kabu wakes. Heaven as a brand name.

**Traditions it echoes.** Philip K. Dick and A Scanner Darkly (The Comics Journal), Akira (Publishers Weekly), William Burroughs, Paprika (AniList review), and the old literature of 'artificial paradises' (Baudelaire; from general knowledge; to verify). For religious readers it echoes warnings about false visions and idols that promise heaven.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism is the engine and the trap. Postmodern uncertainty about reality is the form of the book itself. Transhumanism appears as the next, cleaner high. Libertarian legalization is shown as having changed nothing. Spiritual longing is real but has only chemical and technical outlets.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the pump-bar world, Nova Express and the first Ultra Heaven trip. Vol. 2: lingering hallucinations, Sally and the amps, Kabu's backstory. Vol. 3: the Drome, a shared virtual world, and the search for 'Heaven.' Then the hiatus (since 2009).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Kabu keep needing a stronger high? What does that say about pleasure?
2. The pusher says the dream is truer than waking life. How would you answer him?
3. Legal drugs were supposed to make life better in this world. Why didn't they?
4. Is a 'heaven' made by a drug or a machine a real heaven? What would make it real?
5. If a friend were in Kabu's place, what could you do for them?
