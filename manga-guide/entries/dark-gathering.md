**Premise.** As a boy, Keitaro Gentoga and his childhood friend Eiko Hozuki were cursed by a spirit, leaving nerve endings growing from their hands; Keitaro, who naturally attracts ghosts, became a shut-in. Now 18 and starting university, he takes a tutoring job with Eiko's cousin Yayoi Hozuki, an eight-year-old with double pupils, an IQ over 160 and the ability to see ghosts since the car accident that killed her parents. Yayoi saw her mother's soul taken by a powerful spirit, Kuubou, and is capturing dangerous ghosts in stuffed toys and objects to build an army strong enough to win her mother back. Eiko, who loves both the occult and Keitaro, helps them while secretly monitoring him with bugs, hidden cameras and GPS; the two become a couple early on (VIZ vol. 3). Keitaro's second student, Ai Kamiyo, 15, is marked as the bride of a malevolent star god, Taisui Xingjun (VIZ: Yin Jiao), bound to her clan by an ancestor's contract and destined to take her on her 20th birthday. The Tokyo arc (ch. 1-33) collects ghosts from Japan's worst haunted sites; the Kyoto arc (ch. 34-67) is Yayoi's "Operation Bellflower Regicide" to defeat the god with seven "Graduate" ghosts, helped by the ghost of the court diviner Abe no Seimei; later chapters reveal Yayoi's hospital past and turn back to Kuubou (Wikipedia; VIZ; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Fear and grief can be faced, and a life that trauma took can be won back, when people who love each other stand together and plan carefully; even a god's claim on a family can be broken.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes Yayoi's love for her mother, Keitaro's recovery and the breaking of a clan's pact that sacrificed its daughters. The divide is the method: the heroes capture, bind and command spirits and finally ally with a god. Traditions that forbid necromancy and spirit contact (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness, Orthodox Jewish, Muslim) grade F, and most other Christian frames D, because the occult is the story's solution, not only its setting. Shinto, secular, Satanist and Nothing in particular frames read the spirits as setting or as meaningful folk tradition and grade higher; progressive readers object mainly to Eiko's stalking and the treatment of women in the horror.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with fear and grief that will not go away? Keitaro hid from the world after a ghost scarred him; Yayoi turned her grief into a plan. The series asks whether facing the dark, carefully and together, can win back what it took.

**The answer the story gives.** Face it, plan for it, and do not face it alone. Each haunted site is studied and beaten by preparation (VIZ vol. 5, 14-17 blurbs); Keitaro's courage grows because others need him. The darker answer under it is that power is what protects you, so Yayoi gathers ever more dangerous spirits.

**Characters as arguments.** Yayoi argues for reason and resolve: she will use anything, even evil ghosts, but puts others' safety first (fan wiki). Keitaro argues that fear and courage can live together. Eiko argues for love as possession and for the thrill of fear; the story enjoys her rather than judging her, which reviewers question (ANN's Silverman). The god Taisui argues that the strong may claim the weak, and loses to a child's plan.

**Symbols and motifs.** Hands: the cursed hands with exposed nerves that bind Keitaro and Eiko. Stuffed toys as prisons for ghosts. Double pupils, eyes that see the other world. Stars: the god's mark in Ai's eyes and on Yayoi's hand after the contract (fan wiki).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese ghost stories and urban legends, the onmyoji tale (Abe no Seimei), D.Gray-man's gothic action (from general knowledge; to verify), and Junji Ito-style horror (Comics Beat). The Route 1 chapters borrow the Buddhist six realms; the Kyoto arc borrows a Chinese Taoist star god.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan/occult practice is the heroes' craft and is shown as effective and perilous. Might makes right is explored and finally checked by planning and loyalty. Buddhist and Taoist imagery supply structure and villains rather than teaching. Communitarian loyalty is the story's real heart.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-9 (ch. 1-33): the Tokyo arc, haunted-site episodes, the most child jokes and the bullying and suicide backstories. Volumes 9-17 (ch. 34-67): the Kyoto arc against the god, larger battles, history and religion woven in. Volume 18 on (ch. 68-81): Yayoi's past in the hospital, a prototype Graduate, and a return to Kuubou.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Keitaro is terrified of ghosts but keeps going. What makes someone brave?
2. Yayoi uses evil spirits to fight other spirits. Is that wise? Is it right?
3. Eiko secretly tracks Keitaro's phone. Why does the story make it funny? Would it be funny in real life?
4. A family traded its daughters for power. What does the story say about that bargain?
5. Many ghosts were victims first. Should the heroes feel sorry for them?
