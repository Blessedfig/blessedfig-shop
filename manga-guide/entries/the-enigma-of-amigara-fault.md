**Premise.** An earthquake exposes a fault on Amigara Mountain whose face is covered in human-shaped holes that seem to have been dug from inside the mountain. Crowds and scientists come to look. Two hikers, the young man Owaki and the young woman Yoshida, meet there; Yoshida is searching for a hole shaped exactly like herself. Another visitor, Nakagaki, finds his own hole, strips to his underwear and crawls in; rescuers cannot follow. More people find their holes and go in. Yoshida feels her hole calling her name; Owaki blocks it with rocks and stays with her, but after a nightmare of being an ancient criminal sentenced to crawl into a hole carved in his shape, he wakes to find she has gone in. He then finds his own hole and, entranced, undresses and enters it. Months later, a second fault on the far side of the mountain is found, with long, distorted holes, and something deformed is slowly crawling out of one (Wikipedia, Gyo; AniList).

## Creator and publisher context

- Junji Ito (story and art), born July 31, 1963, in Sakashita (now Nakatsugawa), Gifu; read Kazuo Umezu as a child; worked as a dental technician from 1984 while drawing; his debut work became the long-running Tomie (Wikipedia, Junji Ito).
- Ito cites Umezu, Shigeru Mizuki, Hideshi Hino, Edogawa Ranpo and H. P. Lovecraft as influences, and critics link his work to cosmic horror (Wikipedia, Junji Ito).
- On-record intent for this story (ANN mini-interview, 2020): it started from a single image, a hole in the shape of a person, which led him to ask what it would mean if a hole fit you, who made it and where it leads; he imagines being trapped in it as intensely claustrophobic; he ties it to his own discomfort at seeing himself on video and to his fondness for doppelgangers.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if there were a place made exactly for you, and it called your name? The story asks whether people can resist something that fits them perfectly, even when they know it will destroy them.

**The answer the story gives.** They cannot. Nakagaki goes eagerly, others go in a panic, Yoshida goes in the night despite her fear, and Owaki, the skeptic who tried to save her, goes last (Wikipedia). The story gives no reason, no rescue and no meaning; its "answer" is the horror of a compulsion with no exit.

**Characters as arguments.** Owaki stands for doubt and for care; Yoshida for belief in a destined place; Nakagaki for eager surrender; the scientists for reason. All four positions fail. The skeptic's fall is the sharpest point: knowing better does not protect him.

**Symbols and motifs.** The human-shaped hole as identity, fate or the grave; the mountain as something that consumes; the narrowing tunnel that stretches the body; the second fault with distorted holes, showing where the path ends. Critics read the holes as the "call of the void" and Freud's death drive (ANN feature), and CBR adds Otto Rank's idea of a return to the womb.

**Traditions it echoes.** Lovecraftian cosmic horror (an ancient, unexplained order indifferent to people), which Ito names as an influence (Wikipedia). For religious readers, the dream of an ancient crime and a punishment made to measure echoes ideas of sin and judgment or of karma; for Buddhist readers, the pull of the holes is a picture of craving. These readings are the reader's, not the story's.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism and absurdism are explored, not argued for: the universe of the story is meaningless and unexplained, and the horror comes from that. Reason (the scientists) and love (Owaki) both fail. No faith is shown, mocked or praised.

**Shifts across the series.** A single 31-page story: discovery and the crowd; Nakagaki's disappearance; the night with Yoshida; Owaki's nightmare; Owaki's fall; the epilogue on the far side of the mountain (Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think people went into the holes even though they were afraid?
2. Owaki did not believe in the holes at first. Why do you think he still went in?
3. Owaki tried to protect Yoshida. What else could he have done?
4. Is there anything in real life that "pulls" people toward harm? What helps them resist it?
5. Some stories explain their mystery and some do not. Which kind frightens you more, and why?
