**Premise.** Hinako Yaotose, a 16-year-old high school student (Japanese Wikipedia), lives alone in a quiet seaside town. When she was six, her parents and older brother died when their car went off a cliff into the sea on the way home from a family trip; she was thrown clear and survived with large scars on her shoulder (ch. 5; fan wiki). Since then she has quietly wanted to die so she can be with her family, but has kept living because she remembers a voice telling her to live, and because her cheerful friend Miko would grieve (Japanese Wikipedia). In ch. 1 a sea yokai drags her under the water; she barely resists. A mermaid yokai, Shiori Oumi, rescues her and says she has come to eat her: Hinako's flesh now draws monsters, and Shiori will guard her until she is at her most delicious. Hinako is glad. Shiori then insists she will only eat Hinako once Hinako is happy (ch. 5). Miko turns out to be Okitsune-sama, a fox yokai once feared as a man-eater, tamed by a monk and now worshipped as the town's guardian deity, who has protected Hinako since the accident and planted false memories of a childhood friendship (ch. 7-11). Later volumes reveal that Shiori met six-year-old Hinako years ago, was fed and cared for by her, and gave her mermaid blood with a prayer for her happiness, which let Hinako survive the crash and also made her scent draw yokai; the voice telling her to live was Shiori's (ch. 23-27; anime episodes 11-12). Hinako walks into the sea to die; Shiori stops her and binds herself to Hinako by a blood pact (ch. 27-28). Later arcs follow Miko's past (ch. 32-41) and Azami, an immortal girl who once ate Shiori's flesh and wants revenge on her (ch. 49-55) (fan wiki; Wikipedia; anime reviews).

## Life message

**Being truly seen and loved, even by someone who seems a monster, can slowly draw a grieving person back toward life; but love must reckon with the other person's own will, and saving someone is never simple.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the story's direction: a grieving girl drawn slowly back toward life by people who love her. The split is over three things the story treats as good or neutral: a romance between two girls (affirmed by progressive, secular, Reform and UU readings, a concern for most Christian, Muslim, Latter-day Saint, Orthodox Jewish and Sikh readings, and noted but not decisive for Historically Black Protestant families), yokai and a fox deity shown as kindly powers (welcome for Shinto, Pagan and secular readers, a concern for Pentecostal, Adventist and Witness families), and a pact built on the promise of death, which every frame watches carefully.

## Creator and publisher context

- Sai Naekawa (story and art) grew up in Ehime Prefecture and set the story there (Shiomi Bridge, Dogo Onsen, the Seto Inland Sea). Before this series she drew fan works for Kantai Collection and Touhou Project; she cites Arina Tanemura, Shigeru Mizuki's yokai books, Rozen Maiden and Maria Watches Over Us as influences (Wikipedia, citing Comic Walker and The Beat interviews).
- On-record intent: she set out to explore love and obsession through the idea of "consuming" another person; named mutual dialogue and change as key themes; made Shiori a beautiful but ominous ningyo rather than a Western mermaid (Wikipedia, citing AniTrendz, Anime Feminist and Ehime Film).
- Her editor, Takumi Kamemaru, advised a darker direction than her first, lighter plan, feeling darker themes would appeal more; she originally planned for Hinako's family to be alive (Wikipedia, citing Comic Walker and Pony Canyon).
- No statements on religion or on the story's suicide content were found beyond the above. Neither has she spoken about the romance as political. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: ASCII Media Works / Kadokawa (Dengeki Maoh, seinen). Yen Press publishes the English edition, rated T.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What can bring back someone who no longer wants to live, and does anyone have the right to try? Hinako is not dramatic about her wish to die; she simply waits for the sea. The series asks whether love, honesty, or time can change that, and what it costs the ones who love her.

**The answer the story gives.** So far: love, imperfectly. Shiori's promise to eat Hinako once she is happy is a trick to make her want to live (ch. 5; character page), and Miko's friendship is built on altered memories (ch. 8). Both are well meant and both are lies; when the truth comes out, Hinako nearly dies (ch. 27). The renewed pact in ch. 28 is honest at last, but it still binds love to death. The series is ongoing, so the final answer is not in.

**Characters as arguments.** Hinako argues that grief can make death look like rest. Shiori argues that love may override another's will to keep her alive, and pays for it in trust. Miko argues that a nature can change: a fox who once tortured people for pleasure now gives up part of her own power rather than harm a friend (ch. 11, 37). Azami, made immortal against her will, is Hinako's dark mirror: a girl who was kept alive and hates the one who did it (ch. 23, 49-55).

**Symbols and motifs.** Water and drowning stand for depression (Anime Feminist and ANN critics, via Wikipedia); the sea that killed the family is also where Shiori comes from. Eating stands for love, possession and death at once. Blood carries life (the gift that saved Hinako) and binding (the pact). White lilies in the anime signal the yuri genre and lost innocence (Wikipedia).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese yokai folklore and the Yaobikuni legend; Gothic romance, where love and death are tangled; and the shojo and yuri lineage the author cites (Maria Watches Over Us, Rozen Maiden). For religious readers, it echoes the lament psalms and Elijah's wish to die, and for Buddhist readers the craving for non-existence; but the story's answer comes from friendship, not from faith.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is the frame: the choice to live is Hinako's own work. Nihilism is shown as illness. Pagan and folk elements are the furniture and are treated respectfully. Progressive critics read the romance as queer representation; the story itself stays personal, not political. Duty and promise ethics are tested: a promise kept by lying is still questioned.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-13) set up the pact, the festival and Miko's true identity. Volumes 4-5 (ch. 14-22) bring the beach, the basketball camp and Ayame, and Hinako begins to change. Volumes 6-7 (ch. 23-31) reveal Shiori's past and the truth of the accident, Hinako's attempt in the sea and the blood pact; the anime ends here. Volumes 8-10 (ch. 32-46) turn to Miko's past and her tanuki rivals; volumes 11-13 (ch. 47 onward) bring Azami and older manipulations of Hinako's memory.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hinako looks fine to most people. What signs of her sadness could a friend notice?
2. Shiori lies to keep Hinako alive. Was that right? What would you have done?
3. Miko changed from a killer to a protector. What helped her change?
4. Why does the story use the sea and drowning so often?
5. If a friend told you they wished they were dead, who would you tell, and what could you say?
