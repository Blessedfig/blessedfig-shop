**Premise.** October 1986. The wealthy Ushiromiya family gathers on their private island, Rokkenjima, to fight over the dying patriarch Kinzo's fortune, and a typhoon traps eighteen people there. This arc tells the story from the servants' side. Shannon, a 16-year-old maid raised to think of herself as "furniture," is in love with George, the family's 23-year-old eldest grandson; the witch Beatrice says her magic made the romance possible and asks Shannon to smash a sacred mirror in a shrine on a nearby reef (ch. 1-5). Her brother Kanon, also 16, despises the witch and his own feelings for the heiress Jessica, 18 (ch. 6-8). The aunt Rosa slaps and neglects her 9-year-old daughter Maria (ch. 9). Then Beatrice herself arrives as a guest, and the murders begin: six adults found in the chapel (ch. 16), then Jessica, Kanon, the doctor, the cook, George and Shannon, each with a stake (ch. 17-24). Above it all, on a "meta" game board, Beatrice and Battler (the 18-year-old grandson) argue whether a witch could have done this or a human must have; Beatrice introduces truths spoken in red that cannot be false. At the end Battler concedes, is humiliated, and fights back; Rosa and Maria are reconciled as goat-headed servants overrun the island, and in the Tea Party Beatrice tortures Rosa with visions of her family until Battler intervenes (ch. 25-Tea Party) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Gaffney; Yen Press synopses).

## Life message

**Love is what makes a person fully human, and no witch's bargain, inheritance or pile of gold can stand in for it; what you see is not always what happened, so keep looking for the human truth behind the "magic."**

| Pragmatic | The puzzle is the point: locked rooms, master keys, a "wolves and sheep" riddle (ch. 20) and Beatrice's red truths (ch. 22). Gaffney notes the arc teaches readers not to trust everything they are shown. Rosa's practical survival plan (canned food, a barricade, a rifle) still fails. |
| Religious | A witch, a pact, a smashed shrine mirror that releases evil spirits (Shannon's guilt, ch. 23), magic circles, a chapel used as a murder scene (ch. 16), goat-headed servants and a Purgatorio where the dead are tormented. Faith readers weigh occult power shown as seductive and real within the game against a plot that shows the bargain bringing ruin and love as the only true value. |
| Socio-cultural | A study of class and family power in an old rich household: servants trained to be "furniture," an arranged marriage planned for George (ch. 2), a matriarchal aunt who threatens a maid, children shaped by abuse. Gaffney calls it a badly broken family. |

Outlook: Bleak — everyone on the island dies; Shannon and George die after exchanging vows, Rosa and Maria are reconciled only as the goats close in, and the witch's game resumes; the meta-world ends with Battler rallying, so the arc is bleak with a thread of hope.
Hope source: L — the only light is love: Shannon becomes "human" through her love for George and dies beside him (ch. 24), and Rosa and Maria choose each other at the end; mother-daughter love (F) is the close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person who has been told all her life that she is furniture become human, and what would she give to do it? The second question, staged above the island, is the series' signature: when something seems impossible, do you believe in magic or keep looking for the human hand?

**The answer the story gives.** Love makes a person human, and its price is real. Shannon's bargain gives her love and also ruin; she dies with her promise kept (ch. 24). On the meta-board the witch wins this round, but the arc leaves its own hint, as Gaffney notes: if the story is realistic, some of what we were shown cannot have happened.

**Characters as arguments.** Shannon argues that love is worth any cost. Kanon argues that a servant should not reach beyond his station, then finds he loves too. Rosa argues for suspicion and self-protection and loses everything until she chooses her daughter. Maria argues for belief in the witch, and for forgiving her mother. Beatrice argues that magic is real and cruelty is play. Battler argues that witches cannot exist, but, as Gaffney puts it, his proof is simple denial.

**Symbols and motifs.** Furniture. The engagement ring. The shrine's spirit mirror and the spider webs that ward off spirits (ch. 23). Golden butterflies. The Halloween candy. Red truth. The goats.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Western locked-room mystery (Agatha Christie's And Then There Were None is the obvious model) and Japanese honkaku detective fiction; Faust's pact; Grand Guignol horror (Gaffney compares the Tea Party to Mario Bava's films). My own framing in part.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan/occult is the surface: the witch is everywhere. Postmodernism is the method: the story questions its own telling. Critical theory's class critique sits in the servants' story. Naturalism is Battler's position, defeated in this round. Love, not any of these, is the final value.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-12) are the servants' and Maria's stories before the murders. Volume 3 (ch. 13-18) brings the first twilight and the meta-game's new rules. Volumes 4-5 (ch. 19-26) are the killings, Battler's defeat and humiliation, Rosa and Maria's end, and the two Tea Parties that set up Episode 3.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Shannon believe she needed a witch to be loved?
2. Kanon calls himself furniture. What does the story say about how people see themselves?
3. Rosa treats Maria badly and then dies protecting her. Does that make up for it?
4. Battler says witches cannot exist but cannot prove how the murders were done. Is "I cannot explain it" a good reason to believe in magic?
5. Why might the author want readers to doubt what the pictures show?
