**Premise.** On the first day of summer vacation, Ruka Azumi, a middle-school handball player who cannot put her feelings into words, deliberately injures a teammate who tripped her and is banned from her club for the summer (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Her parents live apart: her father works at an aquarium, and her mother, from a family of ama (women divers), is distant. Ruka meets Umi, a boy who dives into the dark water of Tokyo Bay, and his older brother Sora; the two were found ten years earlier off the Philippines living among dugongs, cannot thrive on land, and are being studied by the tattooed marine biologist Jim Cusack and by his former partner, the prodigy Anglade (Japanese Wikipedia; Viz blurb). As fish vanish from the oceans, sea animals gather in the wrong places, bodies wash ashore and fish rain from the sky (Garrity), Ruka is drawn into a 'festival' of the sea: Sora passes her a small meteorite and vanishes at the end of volume 2 (Slings & Arrows); the mystic sailor Dede, the scientists and old island rites all point toward a birth of life at the bottom of the sea, which Ruka is chosen to witness in the nearly wordless fifth volume (Japanese Wikipedia; Comic Attack; Manga Bookshelf). The ending is deliberately open (Slings & Arrows; Garrity).

## Life message

**Life is one vast, connected mystery that joins the sea, the stars and every living body; it should be met with wonder and humility, not explained away or controlled, and even a lonely, angry girl belongs to it.**

Children raised by animals (Igarashi cites the "feral girls" Amala and Kamala, ANN 2019) and the boys' adaptation to water test what is innate and what is learned. ## Creator and publisher context

- Daisuke Igarashi (born April 2, 1969, Saitama) began drawing to capture the old trees of a shrine grove near his home; studied oil painting at Tama Art University; lived three years farming in rural Iwate, the basis of Little Forest (Wikipedia, Daisuke Igarashi).
- On-record intent (ANN, 2019): the idea grew from doodles of fish and the working title "Fish Girl"; mermaid myths and the sight of dugongs holding their young suggested children raised by dugongs; he consulted marine-mammal researcher Yuko Tajima but took liberties, saying "I didn't want to make it like a documentary." He drew it for female readers despite the seinen magazine.
- Wikipedia (creator) records that he aims to show the beauty of nature, that many of his works draw on folklore and mythology shared across the world, and that he links femininity with nature; he moved from the mountains to the seaside after starting the series.
- No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's religious views (none needed for grading).**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Monthly Ikki, closed 2014; from general knowledge). VIZ Media (owned by Shogakukan and Shueisha; from general knowledge) published it under VIZ Signature, rated T+.
- No controversies found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Where do we come from, and where do we belong? Ruka cannot find a place at school or at home. The sea seems to answer for her: it has a "ghost" she saw as a child, two boys who belong to it more than to land, and an event that the whole ocean is gathering for. The series asks whether a person, a species, or life itself can know its origin, and what it means to be part of something too large to understand.

**The answer the story gives.** Not an explanation but an experience. Scientists offer theories and myths offer stories (Garrity); the book shows both and then lets volume 5 unfold almost without words. Ruka is chosen to witness, not to understand. Life is presented as one great process of birth and return, and wonder and humility are the right response. Slings & Arrows suggests the message may be that change begins with one person.

**Characters as arguments.** Ruka argues for feeling over words: she cannot explain herself, and the story honors what cannot be said. Umi argues for joy and belonging in nature. Sora, frail on land and eager to know what he is, argues for the cost of seeking answers; he gives the meteorite away and vanishes. Jim argues for patient science and its limits. Anglade, the prodigy who could not connect with people as a child (Japanese Wikipedia), argues for knowledge detached from care, and the story leaves him ambiguous. Dede argues that the sea, and the female body, are the way life enters the world.

**Symbols and motifs.** The meteorite as a seed of life; the whale as womb (Ruka wakes inside one, v4); the goddess riding a whale (compared to Venus, ch. 34); light that dissolves bodies (the whale shark, the boys); the ghost of the sea; rain of fish; the ama diver's promise to a voice in an underwater cave that Kanako cannot remember (Comic Attack).

**Traditions it echoes.** Mermaid and selkie tales and Indonesian shadow-puppet plays (Garrity); Japanese beliefs about gods and ancestors visiting from the sea; Buddhist "paramita" (the far shore); Hermetic "as above, so below" and Carl Sagan's "star stuff" (Cinemaholic and Looper, film). Critics compare it to the ecological comic The Puma Blues (Douresseaux via Wikipedia) and to Miyazaki, whose My Neighbor Totoro Igarashi credits with his decision to draw manga (Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pantheist and nature-mystical ideas are affirmed by the shape of the story. Environmental concern is present as awe and unease rather than argument. Science is respected and accurate in its details but cannot reach the heart of the mystery. Religious readers will find reverence for creation joined to a picture of the sacred that is the cosmos itself rather than a Creator.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-8) is a quiet summer: Ruka, the aquarium, Umi and Sora. Volume 2 (ch. 9-16) brings the omens and ends with Sora's disappearance. Volume 3 (ch. 17-25) turns to the boys' past, sea myths and Anglade. Volume 4 (ch. 26-35) gathers the world toward the festival: Dede, Kanako, the Antarctic. Volume 5 (ch. 36-42/43) is the festival itself, told mostly in pictures, followed by Ruka's return.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ruka hurts a teammate on the first page. What changes in her by the end, and what changed her?
2. The scientists cannot explain what happens. Is it all right for a story to leave questions unanswered?
3. Dede and the scientists see the sea differently. Which view do you find more convincing, and why?
4. Sora agrees to be studied to learn what he is. Was that brave, or did the adults ask too much of him?
5. What does your family believe about where life comes from, and how does that compare with the festival in the story?
