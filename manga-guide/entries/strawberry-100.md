**Premise.** Middle-schooler Junpei Manaka, who dreams of directing films, glimpses a girl's strawberry-print underwear on the school roof and sets out to find her. He finds Aya Tojo's notebook of a secret novel and bonds with her over stories, but asks out the popular Tsukasa Nishino, thinking she was the girl. In high school (vol. 3 on) he revives the film club with Aya as writer and the forward Satsuki Kitaoji as star, while his younger childhood friend Yui Minamito boards at his family's home. Through three school films, part-time jobs and trips, Junpei wavers among Aya, Tsukasa and Satsuki. In the third year he and Tsukasa get back together (vol. 17), Aya confesses that she has loved him since middle school (vol. 17), and he chooses Tsukasa. Tsukasa leaves to study pastry-making in Paris and Junpei breaks off the relationship so he can grow up first; four years later Aya is a prize-winning novelist, Junpei has a film-studio job, and he and Tsukasa reunite (Wikipedia; chapter-list volume summaries).

## Creator and publisher context

- Mizuki Kawashita, born August 30, 1971, began as a girls'-manga artist under the pen name Mikan Momokuri (Margaret, Bouquet), then wrote romantic comedies for Weekly Shonen Jump: Lilim Kiss (2000), Strawberry 100% (2002-05), First Love Limited (2007-08) and Anedoki (2009-10) (Wikipedia, Mizuki Kawashita). She is a woman writing in a boys' magazine (MangaUpdates tag).
- On-record intent (afterwords, via English and Japanese Wikipedia): the editors asked for a romantic comedy; the hero's surname Manaka means "center," with the heroines named for east (Tojo) and west (Nishino), later north and south; she meant to end with Aya, but the high-school continuation changed the story; she thinks Aya grew the most by learning love's unfairness.
- Anime director Osamu Sekita said he wondered whether it could be animated at all, since TV stations then banned showing underwear, and noted that the heroines' figures are drawn from a male point of view (Japanese Wikipedia, citing a 2012 Livedoor interview).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Shonen Jump). VIZ Media published 14 of 19 volumes under Shonen Jump Advanced, rated T+ with a Mature Content label. VIZ is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge).
- No statements on religion or politics, and no controversies involving the creator, were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Which dream do you follow, and which person? Junpei wants to make films, and he wants every girl who smiles at him. The series asks whether a boy who cannot decide can still be a good person, and what his indecision costs the people who love him.

**The answer the story gives.** Choose, and grow up. For most of 19 volumes the comedy runs on Junpei's wavering among Aya, Tsukasa and Satsuki, and on the fan service that keeps him (and the reader) distracted. In the last volumes the story turns serious: Aya's confession (vol. 17) is the moment he can no longer pretend, Yui tells him he is hurting the two people he loves most (vol. 19), and he breaks off with Tsukasa so that he can become someone worthy of her. Four years later, the reward is a job in film and a reunion.

**Characters as arguments.** Aya argues for quiet, faithful love and for art; the creator says she is the one who grows the most by learning that love is unfair (afterword). Tsukasa argues for honesty and independence: she leaves for Paris rather than wait. Satsuki argues for boldness, and loses gracefully. Yui is the conscience who is never a love interest. Junpei is the case study: kind, talented and weak, until he isn't.

**Symbols and motifs.** The strawberry underwear, an adolescent fixation that turns out to belong to the girl who shares his dream. Aya's notebook novel, finished and handed back at the end (vol. 19). The pull-up bar where Junpei first asks Tsukasa out and where she later confesses back (vols. 1, 16). The four heroines' names as the points of a compass around the "center."

**Traditions it echoes.** The Jump romantic comedy and the harem story, which Kawashita bends toward a single, earned choice. Critic Tsuyoshi Iwabuchi, writing as the series ended, said its excessive fan service was hard to bear but that it asked seriously how to pursue dreams and a future together (Japanese Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is celebrated: everyone ends up doing what they love. Hedonism drives the comedy and is never condemned, but the ending values commitment over pleasure. Feminist and faith readers will both object to how the girls are drawn, for different reasons.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: middle school, the mystery girl and entrance exams. Vols. 3-9: the film club, two school films, Yui's arrival and Junpei's wavering. Vols. 10-16: second and third years, new girls (Chinami, Kozue), Tsukasa's Paris plan and the third film. Vols. 17-19: the choice, the night together, Aya's farewell, graduation and the four-years-later ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Junpei dated Tsukasa while thinking about Aya. Who got hurt, and how?
2. Why does Junpei break off with Tsukasa when she leaves for Paris? Was it the right choice?
3. The series makes jokes about seeing girls' underwear. How do the girls in the story feel about it? How would you?
4. Aya does not end up with Junpei but becomes a famous novelist. Is hers a happy ending?
