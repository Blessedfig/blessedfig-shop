**Premise.** Detective Yōsuke Kobayashi hunts a serial killer who dismembers his victims; the killer sends him his girlfriend, mutilated but alive. Kobayashi shoots the killer and is imprisoned. In prison he lives as Kazuhiko Amamiya, a calm criminologist with glasses, while a third personality, the callous Shinji Nishizono, surfaces under stress (Wikipedia; Dark Horse vol. 1). Released, Amamiya works for Machi Isono's criminal research lab with her schoolgirl sister Miwa and the bumbling detective Sasayama. New killers keep appearing with barcodes in their left eyes, and so does Amamiya. The trail leads to Gakuso, a secret company with advanced genetic technology, which programs killer personalities into hosts to recreate the soul of Lucy Monostone, a dead 1960s singer and terrorist whose songs drove followers to mass suicide; it also grows clones as spare organs for the powerful, and works with Tatsuo Onihigata, Japan's Minister of Justice (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; CBR). Shinji takes over and disappears (vol. 5), Miwa absorbs the Amamiya personality on a hijacked plane and passes it to Tetora, a teenage barcode host, who promises to keep Amamiya alive inside him (vols. 6-8). The untranslated second half (vols. 12-24) follows Tetora and the political conspiracy to a final volume about protecting the soul of someone precious and finding one's true self (Kadokawa, vol. 24).

## Life message

**A self can be copied, programmed and used by people with power, so no one simply owns their soul; what survives is the choice to protect someone else's.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Predators everywhere: serial killers, a company that breeds people for organs (Kitou as Onihigata's "spare"), and hosts engineered to kill. Kin ties are thin; Machi and Miwa are the main family bond, and even that is engineered. Survival belongs to those who control others. |
| Pragmatic | Detective work and profiling solve cases, but every solution reveals a larger conspiracy; Sasayama's plain decency and stubbornness survive where cleverness fails (CBR; Wikipedia). |
| Religious | No real religion guides the story; its spiritual furniture is cultic: Lucy Monostone's mass suicide, an "angel cult" of serial suicides promising angelhood (vol. 9), songs that bend minds (TV Tropes; Dark Horse vol. 9). Faith readers will see a world where the soul is treated as data and bodies as material, and where no one is redeemed by grace. |
| Socio-cultural | Written by a critic of otaku culture and "narrative consumption" (Wikipedia, Ōtsuka), it reads as a commentary on manufactured identity, media spectacle (the profiler reality show, vol. 5) and state power hidden behind companies. ANN's reviewer argues the gore often outweighs the ideas. |

Outlook: Bleak — killers, cults and a state-linked conspiracy dominate, most major characters die, and identity itself is shown as manufactured; the final volume's blurb points to a quiet ending in which someone's soul is protected (vol. 24, Kadokawa; to verify).
Hope source: P — what hope there is comes from protecting others: Miwa makes Tetora promise to keep Amamiya's personality alive (vols. 7-8), and the final volume is framed as a battle to protect a precious person's soul; Sasayama's stubborn sense of justice is the other light.

## Creator and publisher context

- Eiji Ōtsuka (story), born 1958 in Tokyo; studied folklore at the University of Tsukuba; a critic, folklorist, novelist and media theorist, professor at the International Research Center for Japanese Studies in Kyoto; doctorate from Kobe Design University (2007). Author of the theory of "narrative consumption" (1989) and of books on otaku culture; also writes The Kurosagi Corpse Delivery Service (Wikipedia, Eiji Ōtsuka).
- Ōtsuka's early editorial and anime work, per Wikipedia, included editing the 1980s magazine Manga Burikko and a bishōjo manga series, and creating an adult OVA; these are lolicon (sexualized child-like girls) works by Wikipedia's description. He defended otaku readers after the 1989 Tsutomu Miyazaki case. These are public, work-related facts; no statement by him on this series' nudity was found.
- Shō-u Tajima (art), known for character design on the anime segment of Kill Bill and on Production I.G works (Anime UK News). His Wikipedia article could not be opened. Tajima announced on Twitter that the series would end with vol. 23 (ANN news, 2016); it ended with 24.
- On-record intent: Ōtsuka's volume 1 afterword says our relationship to corpses is a central theme (Anime UK News, paraphrased). No interview was opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Kadokawa Shoten (Shōnen Ace, then Young Ace). Dark Horse published vols. 1-11 uncut, 18+ and shrink-wrapped, after another U.S. publisher declined it as too shocking (Dark Horse).
- Controversy: in 2007 Japanese media linked a 17-year-old's murder and dismemberment of his mother in Fukushima Prefecture to a manga matching MPD Psycho; ComiPress notes the boy's mental state and other factors were unclear. Many in Japan considered the series harmful (ComiPress). Formal "harmful publication" designations by prefectures were not confirmed in sources opened (to verify).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who are you, if your personalities can be installed, copied and moved? A detective who seems to be three people learns that none of them may be the original, and that a company made them on purpose.

**The answer the story gives.** No one owns a self outright: identity is shown as a construct that the powerful can engineer. Against that, the story offers one thing to hold to: a promise to protect someone else's soul (Miwa and Tetora, vols. 7-8; the final volume's blurb). It is a thin hope in a bleak world.

**Characters as arguments.** Amamiya argues for reason and justice, though he may be a program. Shinji argues that a self without conscience is freer, and the story both fears and enjoys him. Gakuso argues that people are material. Sasayama argues, without knowing it, that ordinary decency still matters. Miwa and Tetora argue that a promise can carry a person forward when nothing else is certain.

**Symbols and motifs.** The barcode in the eye: a person as a product. Glasses that mark which personality is in control (Wikipedia). Lucy Monostone's songs, which bend minds. Flowers planted in living heads (vol. 5): beauty grown from violence. The "candies" that keep Tetora stable.

**Traditions it echoes.** The serial-killer thriller (The Silence of the Lambs era), cyberpunk's question of whether minds are software, Japanese cult anxieties, and Ōtsuka's own theory that stories are consumed as interchangeable parts of a larger world (Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Postmodernism's constructed self is the premise, played as horror. Authoritarian and transhumanist projects are the villains. Nihilism hangs over the world, with suicide cults and killers who kill for nothing. Existentialism appears in the search for a true self at the end.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4: episodic serial-killer cases and the barcode mystery. Vols. 5-9: the Lucy 7, Shinji's takeover, the hijacked plane, the experiment ship and the angel cult. Vol. 10 and the flashback vol. 11 ("dead man's galaxy days"): the past behind the first chapter (Wikipedia). Vols. 12-24 (untranslated): Tetora's flight, Gakuso's political aims and the conclusion (2016).

**Discussion questions.**
1. If someone copied your memories into another body, would that be you?
2. Why do you think the artist shows violence and nudity so openly? What does that do to the reader?
3. Sasayama is not clever, but he keeps going. Why does that matter in this story?
4. Gakuso treats people as material. Where do you see that attitude in real life?
5. Is it ever right to read a book mainly for its shocks?
