**Premise.** Players of Rabbit Doubt, a phone game like Mafia in which one rabbit is secretly a wolf, meet offline: Yū Aikawa, his childhood friend Mitsuki, the brash Eiji, the cheerful Haruka and the frail Rei. They wake in an abandoned psychiatric hospital with barcodes printed on their bodies, beside a medical student, Hajime, and what looks like Rei's hanged body. To get out they must find the "wolf," the liar among them, as they are picked off one by one (Wikipedia; Yen Press). Eiji loses a hand and dies, Haruka's head is found inside a rabbit mask, and a book exposes everyone's secrets. The wolf is Mitsuki, who has killed to punish liars on behalf of her father; Hajime is really a detective. Then Rei, alive, reveals that she is the true wolf: discredited as a fraud hypnotist, she lost her parents to suicide and has used hypnosis to control Mitsuki and others. Rei escapes blame, Hajime is killed, and the book ends with Mitsuki, triggered by a phrase, coming at Yū with a knife (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Lies, even small ones, breed suspicion and revenge; the story warns that trust is fragile and that those who punish liars become something worse, but it ends with the manipulator winning and offers no way out.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you trust anyone, when everyone has something to hide? The game asks the players to find the one liar; the story reveals that they are all liars, and that the people hunting liars are the worst of all.

**The answer the story gives.** Trust is fragile and lies breed revenge, but the book gives no remedy. Mitsuki kills a classmate over a lie Yū told to hide a birthday present for her (Wikipedia); Rei turns her own humiliation as an exposed 'liar' into a network of killers. The honest detective dies, and the manipulator walks free.

**Characters as arguments.** Mitsuki argues that liars deserve death, and her own love and loyalty are bent into murder. Rei argues that a world that called her a liar deserves no mercy. Hajime argues for evidence and law, and is strangled. Yū is the ordinary person who tells small, kind lies, and is caught between them. Eiji, Haruka and Hajime (as Mitsuki tells it) each hide a grave sin, so no one is simply innocent.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rabbit mask, scarred and staring, which carries on into Judge and Secret. The barcodes printed on bodies, each opening one door. The abandoned psychiatric hospital. The book of secrets. The trigger phrase that turns love into a weapon.

**Traditions it echoes.** The party game Mafia/Werewolf; the death-game genre of Battle Royale, Saw and Cube (Publishers Weekly); the locked-room mystery. Faith readers may set the rule 'the liar must die' beside teaching that vengeance belongs to God and that all have sinned.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is the mood. A rigid duty-ethics rule (never lie, or die) is shown as monstrous. Social critique of media shaming and cover-ups is present but in the background.

**Shifts across the series.** Yen Press vol. 1 (Japanese vols. 1-2, likely) sets up the hospital, the barcodes and the first deaths, with clue-heavy mystery (ANN). Vol. 2 (Japanese vols. 3-4, likely) turns to chases, the revelations about Mitsuki and Rei, and the bleak ending (Slings & Arrows).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yū lied to Mitsuki to keep a birthday present secret. Was that lie wrong? Why did it matter so much to her?
2. Mitsuki believes liars should be punished. What is the difference between justice and revenge?
3. Rei was publicly called a fraud, and her family was destroyed. Does that explain what she does? Does it excuse it?
4. The players met people from an online game in person. What would you do to stay safe?
5. Hajime uses evidence and reasoning. How does someone tell who is telling the truth?
