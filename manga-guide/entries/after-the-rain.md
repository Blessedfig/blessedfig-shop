**Premise.** Akira Tachibana, 17, a second-year student at a Yokohama high school, was the ace sprinter on her track team until she tore her right Achilles tendon and stopped running. On her way home from a check-up she shelters from the rain at a family restaurant, where the manager, Masami Kondo, a timid, apologetic 45-year-old divorced father, gives her a free coffee and a small magic trick. She takes a part-time job there and falls for him. She confesses twice, the second time soaked in the rain; Kondo first takes it as admiration, then tries to talk her out of it, and agrees to one outing to show her they do not fit (vol. 1; anime ep. 2-4). A college-age cook, Kase, finds out her secret and blackmails her into a date (vol. 1). Kondo, who loves literature and gave up writing novels while his college friend Chihiro Kujo became famous, slowly starts writing again; Akira drifts from her best friend Haruka, who wants her back on the track. Across the series each pushes the other toward the dream they abandoned. In the final volume they spend a snowy day together; when Akira visits Kondo's apartment on New Year's Day he turns her down outright, she leaves the restaurant and starts rehabilitation, and the last chapter shows her at her first race since the injury (TV Tropes; AIPT; Anime Feminist essay; Kodansha vol. 5 synopsis). Her love stays unrequited (MangaUpdates).

## Life message

**After a setback, the way forward is to return to the passions you gave up; a good adult protects a young person's future instead of taking the love she offers, and both can move on with gratitude.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Jun Mayuzuki, born April 27, 1983, is a Japanese woman manga artist; After the Rain (2014-2018) won the 63rd Shogakukan Manga Award, and her next series, Kowloon Generic Romance, ran in Weekly Young Jump from 2019 to April 2026 (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: she calls her work diary-like and says After the Rain "represents my past self" (Yen Press TCAF interview, 2023); it was where she found her pacing. In ANN's 2023 interview she says it ran in a young men's magazine but readers sometimes called it shojo, that from her view it has few romantic elements, and that her liking for older characters is a personal preference. Akira, like Mayuzuki, is from Yokohama (Yen Press).
- She has not explained the age-gap premise or the ending in any source opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's intent.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Big Comic Spirits, seinen); since 2025 Shueisha holds the Japanese rights (AniList note). English: Vertical, now part of Kodansha USA, rated 13+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with a life that stopped? Akira's ended on the track when her tendon tore; Kondo's ended, he thinks, when he put down his pen. Each fills the empty place with something else: she with a fierce, wordless love for him, he with routine and apologies.

**The answer the story gives.** Go back to what you gave up. The love story turns out to be the means, not the end: Akira's feelings move Kondo to write again, and Kondo's refusal sends Akira back to the track. ANN's Nick Creamer says that as the anime goes on "any potentially romantic framing is discarded" in favor of their shared unhappiness; the manga ends with a rejection, a farewell and a race.

**Characters as arguments.** Akira argues that love needs no reasons. Kondo argues for restraint and, slowly, for a second try at a dream. Kase argues, cynically, that she should date someone her own age, and is half right while being wholly wrong in method. Haruka argues for loyalty: a friend will not let you hide. Chihiro shows that success without love for the work is its own failure.

**Symbols and motifs.** Rain (every anime episode title names a kind of rain) and the clearing sky after it; Akira's scarred heel and bare feet (TV Tropes); a swallow that nests by the café but longs for the sky (ep. 10); Victor Hugo's "?" and "!" letters (ep. 10); Kondo's manuscript titled After the Rain.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese pure literature (junbungaku) that Kondo loves, and the mono no aware of seasonal art. For Christian readers it echoes the call to use one's talents (the parable of the talents); for Buddhist readers, the release of attachment; for secular readers, the existential choice to begin again.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and expressive individualism carry the ending; virtue ethics carries the middle, in Kondo's restraint. Feminist critics are split: Anime Feminist objects that the premise presents a minor's desire for an adult as something to sympathize with, while the essay by Olive St. Sauver on the same site reads it as a story about an athlete recovering her identity.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2: the confession, the outing, Kase's blackmail and the typhoon. Volumes 3-6: friendships widen (Haruka, Yui, Yoshizawa), Kondo meets Chihiro again, Akira learns her injury can be treated (Gaffney, vol. 3). Volumes 7-8: Kondo feels something and pulls back (vol. 4 synopsis). Volumes 9-10: winter, the snowstorm day, New Year's rejection, farewell and the race.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kondo says no. Why was that the right choice, and was it hard for him?
2. Kase tells Akira to date someone her own age. Why is he right about that and wrong in how he acts?
3. What dream did each main character give up, and what helped them pick it back up?
4. Haruka was hurt when Akira stopped running. How can a friend tell you a hard truth?
5. Is this a love story? Why or why not?
