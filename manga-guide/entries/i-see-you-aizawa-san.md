**Premise.** Michi Kurotori, a high-school girl, has seen ghosts all her life and pretends she cannot, so they will leave her alone. Then a ghost she cannot ignore appears in her classroom: Honami Aizawa, a classmate and former member of the hugely popular idol group De:LPhinus, who died recently and lingers out of unfinished attachment (Wikipedia; official page). As a ghost Honami has lost all restraint and acts, the official page says, like a grade-schooler; her antics and showers of idol "fan service" (in idol culture, the winks, poses and attention idols give fans; from general knowledge) keep drawing Michi's eyes, and Michi's heart starts racing (official page; Seven Seas). Michi's only friend, Kasumi Namoto, is an outsider too (official page). Chapter titles point to an exorcist (ch. 13, title only), a school festival arc (ch. 15-17), jealousy and a love declaration (ch. 21-22), and online trouble (ch. 24) (MangaDex titles). A user review describes a yuri slice-of-life start that turns into a mystery with semi-horror elements and heavy foreshadowing (MyAnimeList). The vol. 6 blurb calls the series the story of the time until Honami dies, and says the new arc reaches into De:LPhinus's past, a spirit possessing another idol, a psychic hired to help, the truth of Honami's death and a "fifth" member (official page).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it safer to pretend you do not see? Michi has survived by ignoring ghosts, and people. A ghost who demands to be seen asks whether a closed heart is protection or a prison.

**The answer the story gives.** So far, that seeing and being seen is worth the risk. Michi falls for Honami and starts to make friends (official page; MyAnimeList). Because Honami is already dead, the love is shadowed from the start; the vol. 6 blurb frames the series as the story leading to her death.

**Characters as arguments.** Michi argues for self-protection and is slowly argued out of it. Honami argues for wanting to be seen as a person, not an idol. Kasumi, the other outsider, shows that friendship can begin with the excluded. The exorcist and the psychic (ch. 13; vol. 6) stand for the world's way of dealing with the dead: remove them. The idol group stands for image over self.

**Symbols and motifs.** Sight (the title), the idol's stage persona, the classroom seat a ghost still occupies, and touch that is impossible (ch. 8, title only).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese ghost story and the ghost-romance (a living person loving the dead), the yuri school romance, and the idol drama. Readers from many faiths will meet a folk-Buddhist idea: the dead held by unfinished attachment (from general knowledge; to verify).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism (be seen as you are) is affirmed; the occult is horror atmosphere; progressivism appears as ordinariness, not argument.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-6): the haunting and comedy. Vols. 2-3 (ch. 7-17): friends, an exorcist and a school festival. Vols. 4-5 (ch. 18-30): jealousy, confessions and darker turns. Vol. 6 onward (ch. 31-36): curses, De:LPhinus's past and the truth of Honami's death (titles and blurb only; to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Michi pretends not to see ghosts so they will leave her alone. Do people ever do that with living people? Why?
2. Honami wants to be seen as she really is, not as an idol. What is the difference?
3. Can you love someone you know you will lose? What makes it worth it?
4. How do different families and faiths think about what happens after death? How does this story's idea compare?
5. What would you want people to remember about you?
