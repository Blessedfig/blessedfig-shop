**Premise.** Nai, a gentle, unworldly boy, searches for the man who raised him, Karoku, whose only trace is a bracelet left beside a trail of blood. In ch. 1 Nai has been sold to Lady Mine, a wealthy woman who is secretly a Varuga, a human turned monster who eats people; Gareki, a 15-year-old thief who was sold by his own parents at eight, breaks into her mansion and frees him (fan wiki; Japanese Wikipedia). Framed as criminals, the two are taken in by Circus, the country's elite defense agency, whose airship crews (Captain Hirato, the childlike fighter Yogi, the serious acrobat Tsukumo) raid criminals and then put on circus shows to apologize to frightened citizens (Wikipedia). Circus's doctors find that Nai is not human but a Niji, a small forest creature given human form by Karoku's cells (ch. 8; ANN). Their enemy is Kafka, a secret organization doing illegal experiments with "Incuna" cells to force human "evolution," which turns people into Varuga (Wikipedia; fan wiki). Gareki's foster sister Tsubaki is killed and her twin siblings are experimented on by a Varuga named Meiga (vols. 3-4). Circus rescues Karoku from Kafka, but there are two Karokus, the real one and Kafka's copy, who wants Nai for himself (vols. 6-7). Gareki enters the government academy Chronomé and later trains as a combat medic; Nai is drawn into Kafka's headquarters; Kafka's heiress Eliška tries to save Nai at the cost of her own life (omnibus vols. 12-14, Yen Press). The final chapter is titled "Welcome Home, I'm Home" (ch. 160, Japanese Wikipedia); the ending was not confirmed.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone a person, and where does a person belong? Nai is an animal given human form; Gareki was sold by his parents; Yogi survived experiments that killed his sister. The series asks whether people made, bought or broken by others can have a home.

**The answer the story gives.** Belonging is given, not earned: Circus takes the boys in, and they choose each other again and again. Against this, Kafka claims to serve humanity by forcing evolution, and makes monsters. The story sides with care over progress.

**Characters as arguments.** Nai argues for trust and gentleness, even toward enemies. Gareki argues that you cannot trust anyone, and slowly loses the argument. Hirato argues for calculated protection and keeps his feelings hidden. Uro argues that people are material for evolution. Eliška, spoiled and selfish, ends by giving her life for Nai, which the story treats as redemption.

**Symbols and motifs.** The circus: terror followed by a show of apology. The bracelet (Circus I.D.): identity and the thread back to Karoku. Two Karokus: the real self and the copy. The Niji forest: innocence and origin.

**Traditions it echoes.** Frankenstein and the mad-scientist tale; found-family adventure in the manner of shōjo and josei fantasy; the circus as a place for outsiders.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism is affirmed; transhumanism, in Kafka's form, is critiqued; utilitarian calculation by the heroes is shown with unease. No faith is argued, but the defense of human dignity matches many traditions.

**Shifts across the series.** Omnibus 1-2 (ch. 1-24): Nai, Gareki and Circus; Tsubaki's family tragedy. Omnibus 3-4 (ch. 25-48): the Smoky Mansion raid, Karoku rescued, Gareki at Chronomé. Omnibus 5-10 (ch. 49-116): Kafka's agents, Yogi's past, Gareki's growth. Omnibus 11-14 (ch. 117-160): Nai taken to Kafka, the other Karoku, Eliška's sacrifice and the end, which was not confirmed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nai is not human by birth. What makes him a person?
2. Gareki was sold by his parents and trusts no one. What helps him change?
3. Circus uses Nai as bait to catch Kafka. Is that right if it keeps people safe?
4. Kafka says its experiments help humanity. Why does the story disagree?
5. Eliška starts out selfish and ends by protecting Nai. What changed her?
