**Premise.** Haruna Nagashima gave everything to softball in junior high. Now in high school, she gives everything to a new goal: a boyfriend and a real first love, as in the shojo manga she reads. Magazine advice fails her, so, treating romance like sport, she recruits a coach: Yoh Komiyama, a good-looking second-year who knows what boys notice and who distrusts girls. He agrees on one condition, that she must not fall for him (vol. 1). Helped by his sharp-tongued younger sister Asami, Haruna learns to dress and carry herself, but stays herself; she falls for Yoh anyway, and he returns her feelings (vols. 1-3). As a couple they face awkward dates, a Christmas she over-plans, Yoh's ex-girlfriend, his friend Asaoka's confession and a sports-day bet over her, and a girl obsessed with Yoh who schemes to break them up (vols. 4-12; VIZ). In the finale Yoh leaves to attend university in Tokyo and they keep a long-distance relationship (vol. 13; extra volumes 14-15) (Wikipedia; VIZ).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you learn to be loved? Haruna treats romance as she treated softball: study, practice and a coach. The series asks what part of attraction can be learned (clothes, manners, confidence) and what cannot (honesty, kindness, being yourself), and whether a relationship built on coaching can become one between equals.

**The answer the story gives.** Some things can be learned, but the heart of love is character. Yoh's advice helps Haruna look better, yet he falls for her earnestness, discipline and kindness, the parts no magazine taught her (Comics Worth Reading vol. 1). As a couple, they keep learning: to handle jealousy, to say what they mean, to trust each other across distance (vols. 4-13).

**Characters as arguments.** Haruna argues for sincerity and effort. Yoh argues for clear-eyed realism and, over time, faithfulness. Asami argues for being admired, then learns from Fumiya's steady kindness that a few good bonds outweigh many (Comics Worth Reading vol. 7). Miyabi, bitter because her parents compared her looks with her brother's, shows what happens when worth is tied to appearance (AniList). Mami shows quiet loyalty.

**Symbols and motifs.** The coach-and-athlete relationship. The softball and the pitch that once lost a game (Wikipedia, Mami). Fashion magazines that give the wrong advice. Christmas, over-planned and saved by understanding (vol. 4). The long-distance phone call at the end.

**Traditions it echoes.** The makeover romance (from Cinderella to teen films), the sports-manga training arc, and the shojo love triangle. Its moral, that character matters more than appearance, matches teaching found in many traditions, such as 1 Samuel 16:7 and Proverbs 31:30.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and classical virtue are affirmed together: be yourself, and become a better version of yourself through effort. Consumer advice about beauty is gently mocked. Friendship and loyalty are the steady background.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 are the coaching and first love; volumes 4-9 the couple's trials (Christmas, the ex-girlfriend, new first-years, Asaoka's confession, the obsessed rival); volumes 10-13 move toward graduation and Yoh's university choice; volumes 14-15 add side stories and the long-distance sequel (VIZ; Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. What did Haruna change about herself, and what did she keep? Which mattered more to Yoh?
2. Why do you think Yoh set the rule "don't fall in love with me"?
3. How do the characters deal with jealousy? What works and what does not?
4. Miyabi was hurt by how her family talked about her looks. How does that shape what she does?
5. Is having a boyfriend or girlfriend a good goal for high school? What else could Haruna's goal have been?
