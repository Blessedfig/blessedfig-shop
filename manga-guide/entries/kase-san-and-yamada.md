**Premise.** In the prequel, shy Yui Yamada, who loves tending her high school's garden, and Tomoka Kase, the track team's star, start dating; when Kase wins a place at a sports university in Tokyo, Yamada changes her plans and applies to a women's university in Tokyo too (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; Anime Feminist). This sequel follows them as university students and adults: Yamada studies horticulture, Kase trains and lives in a sports-university dorm, and classes and a long train ride keep them apart (vol. 1). Their first summer break brings a fireworks festival and a beach trip, and Yamada is overwhelmed with jealousy of Kase's beautiful roommate (vol. 2). In the fall, Yamada builds a garden for the university festival; a gardener she admires and a handsome young actor nicknamed the "flowerbed prince" make Kase jealous in turn (vol. 3). They decide to move in together, and Kase's roommate Fukami challenges her to a 100-meter race to stop her leaving the dorm (vol. 4). Living together at last, Yamada works extra shifts to save for a cozy bed and matching rings and enters a gardening contest, risking burnout (vol. 5) (Seven Seas volume copy). MangaUpdates tags a secret relationship and trust issues.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hiromi Takashima (story and art). In 2009 a Shinshokan editor asked her to draw for Hirari, a "pure yuri" anthology; she had not drawn yuri before, and the editor chose her for the romance in her earlier manga Miman Ren'ai (Japanese Wikipedia, citing the 2018 film pamphlet).
- On-record intent (Japanese Wikipedia, citing the pamphlet): she wanted to draw a cool senpai who makes your heart skip even though she is not a boy, and Yamada was created as Kase's counterpart. She describes "pure yuri" as the can't-stop feeling of loving someone in your student days.
- Publisher: Shinshokan (Wings, a shōjo magazine). English: Seven Seas, rated Teen.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What keeps love alive after "getting together"? The prequel ends with the couple choosing the same city; this series asks what happens when real life, with classes, dorms, rivals and money, crowds in.

**The answer the story gives.** Talking. Each volume sets up a fear (the train ride, the roommate, the actor, the dorm, the money) and resolves it when the two say what they feel. Anime Feminist calls this the series' center: a relationship is ongoing work.

**Characters as arguments.** Yamada argues for humble devotion, but her habit of hiding worries makes things worse until she speaks. Kase argues for confident love that reassures. Fukami and the "flowerbed prince" are not villains but tests.

**Symbols and motifs.** Gardens: love tended slowly (Yamada's festival garden, the contest in vol. 5). Running: Kase's drive, and the 100-meter race for her future (vol. 4). The train ride: distance. The bed and rings: a shared future.

**Traditions it echoes.** Shōjo romance and the yuri tradition of the admired "cool" senpai; for many readers, ordinary wisdom about love being patient and not jealous (1 Corinthians 13 for Christian readers), though the story draws on no faith.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is gentle: each partner follows her calling without leaving the other. Progressivism appears only as normalcy, never as argument. Virtue ethics is the working engine: patience and honesty win.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 is distance; vols. 2-3 are jealousy in both directions; vol. 4 is the decision to live together; vol. 5 is the strain of building a home (Seven Seas copy).
