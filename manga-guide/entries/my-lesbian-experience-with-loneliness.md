**Premise.** At 28, Kabi Nagata has never dated, kissed anyone or had sex. Years earlier, at about 18, she dropped out of art college; depression, anxiety, an eating disorder and self-cutting followed, and she drifted through part-time jobs while living with her parents and trying to win their approval (Otaku USA; Manga Bookshelf; The OASG; Autostraddle). Seeking a change, she books a session with a lesbian escort service and meets the worker in a love hotel. The book opens on that scene, then goes back about a decade to show how she got there (Autostraddle; The Slings & Arrows). The encounter is awkward and does not fix her loneliness; she realizes that what she wanted was connection and that she had never loved herself. She then posts the story as a manga, and its warm reception online is part of the ending (Anime Feminist; Otaku USA; Manga Bookshelf). The publisher calls it "the candid tell-all of a young woman's struggles with depression and sexuality" (Seven Seas page).

## Creator and publisher context

- **Kabi Nagata, on record:** born May 28, 1987; writes under a pen name; began drawing her own experiences after struggling to find work following her debut, reasoning this was how to make something interesting enough to earn a living; drafts by listing events and feelings as bullet points and arranging them; aimed to neither lower nor glorify herself so readers could relate; said there were things she "couldn't touch on" in this book (Wikipedia, citing interviews not opened here). In a 2021 Anime News Network interview she said she lacks "a really clear grasp or understanding of my own sexuality," does not set out to make yuri, regrets how she portrayed her family early on, later felt "they really do accept and value me," and tries to "make sure that what I'm creating isn't hurting anyone" while staying honest. Her later published books cover alcoholism, hospitalization for pancreatitis, an eating disorder and burnout (Wikipedia). No statements on religion or politics found. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a lonely person actually need? Kabi believes the answer is sex, or at least touch: if she can get through a first sexual experience, perhaps she will become "normal" and able to talk to people (Anime Corner). The book tests that belief against the record of her own life.

**The answer the story gives.** Not sex, and not approval. The encounter in the love hotel is awkward; she freezes, and the purchase does not include "intimacy, gratification, or comfort" (Anime Corner; Anime Feminist). What she discovers is older and harder: "Because I couldn't love myself, I'd always treat myself and my accomplishments like they were crap" (Otaku USA, quoting the book). The way forward is to treat herself as someone worth caring for: clean up, dress well, keep working, tell the truth in her manga (Anime Feminist). Sex is "not the end, only the beginning of more discoveries" (Otaku USA).

**Characters as arguments.** Kabi is the whole argument: the depressed young woman who wants her parents' approval, and the older narrator who looks back with irony and kindness. Her parents stand for a conventional life she cannot live, though reviewers say they are left thin (The OASG), and the author later said she regretted how she drew them (ANN interview). The escort worker stands for the limits of a transaction: kind and professional, but not a friend.

**Symbols and motifs.** The love hotel in daylight (The Slings & Arrows); the bald spot and scars as visible marks of inner pain (Autostraddle); pink screentone on black and white (Wikipedia); the book itself as the turn, since publishing the story and being received kindly is part of its ending (Manga Bookshelf).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese I-novel and essay manga, with their candid self-exposure; the recovery memoir; the coming-of-age story moved to the late twenties (Autostraddle). Religious readers may see a picture of a soul seeking in the wrong place; Buddhist readers may see craving for approval loosened. These are readings, not the book's claims.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and secular humanism are affirmed: the self is where healing starts, without religion. Hedonism is tested and found wanting. Traditional family expectation is shown mainly as pressure. Progressive themes are present but quiet: reviewers stress it is a mental-health story first (Otaku USA; Anime Feminist).

**Shifts across the series.** The book opens at the hotel, flashes back about ten years to college dropout, depression, self-harm and the eating disorder, returns to the encounter and its aftermath, a later visit, and ends with Kabi beginning to care for herself and publishing the story; an extra chapter tells what happened next (The Slings & Arrows; AniList). Exact chapter boundaries were not confirmed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kabi thought one experience would make her "normal." Why do people hope a single event will fix them?
2. What is the difference between being touched and being cared for?
3. How can wanting a parent's approval help a person, and how can it hurt?
4. The author later said she regretted how she portrayed her family. What do we owe the people in our own stories?
5. Your family's tradition has its own teaching on sexuality, the body and despair. Where does this book agree with it, and where does it not?
