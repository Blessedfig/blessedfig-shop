**Premise.** Hina Katagiri, a cheerful high school student in the kendo club, has dreamed since childhood of sheltering from the rain with a little boy in a garden she has never seen. During a music class she is wrapped in light and lands in that garden in 1907 (Meiji 40) Tokyo. Takaaki Hongō, heir of the Hongō trading house (adopted as heir because his older brother was sickly, and resented by his stepmother), takes her for his fiancée, Hinako Kitamine, the youngest daughter of a count, who is Hina's exact double. Hinako loves the family's live-in student, Taketora, and wants out of the match (vols. 1-2). Hina impersonates Hinako to search with Takaaki for her great-grandmother's necklace, the key to going home, and slowly realizes Takaaki is the boy from her dreams; Kikunoshin, a star magician who performs as the woman "Tenkiku," joins them (vols. 3-5). Hina gives up hope of returning, works in a café and refuses Takaaki's confession to protect his engagement (vol. 6); they finally admit their love (vols. 7-8) and become lovers (vols. 8-9). The mystery then widens: Takaaki's father and a foreign merchant, Albright, were involved in human trafficking; Kikunoshin's sister took her own life; the troupe leader, addicted to opium, burns the Albright mansion (vols. 9-10); Taketora turns out to be from the future (vol. 11); Takaaki's stepmother arranges attacks and kidnappings (vols. 11-13). Hinako discovers the joy of study, and she and Takaaki tell both families they are ending the engagement; her father locks her up and beats her until Takaaki and Taketora rescue her (vols. 13-14). Returning home means Takaaki's memory of Hina must be erased (vol. 9) (Kodansha Japan synopses; Japanese fan reference).

## Life message

**True love seeks the other's good even when it means letting go, and each person deserves to choose her own path rather than have family, rank or fortune choose it for her.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the self-giving love, the rescue of an abused daughter and the exposure of trafficking. The divide is over two things: the leads' implied premarital union while Hina is a high school student (vols. 8-9), which Baptist, Pentecostal, Latter-day Saint, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness, Orthodox Jewish and Muslim readings weigh as an endorsed message (C), and the story's verdict on arranged marriage and parental authority, which progressive, secular and Reform Jewish frames welcome while conservative and Bahá'í readers ask whether duty to parents is given enough weight. Progressive and secular readers who welcome the feminist thread still raise the age and power gap between the leads.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The darker material (trafficking, a suicide, opium, a beating) sits in the later volumes.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can love be real if it means taking another person's place, and what do we owe the family and the century we are born into? Hina is literally living Hinako's life, wearing her name, sleeping in her room, standing in for her at her own betrothal. The story asks whether a life borrowed in this way can become one's own, and whether the person you love may have to be given back.

**The answer the story gives.** Love proves itself by being willing to let go. Hina refuses Takaaki's confession to protect his engagement (vol. 6) and later accepts that going home means he must forget her (vol. 9). At the same time, the story will not let families force love: Hinako's match is broken and her father's violence is answered with rescue (vols. 13-14). Readers report that both couples end happily after a painful separation (to verify).

**Characters as arguments.** Hina argues for cheerful courage and self-giving. Takaaki argues for duty: an adopted heir who honors his dead brother and still must decide whether his family's name is worth its crimes. Hinako is the story's quiet revolutionary, a sheltered count's daughter who discovers study and her own voice. Kikunoshin, who lives in two genders on and off stage, argues for devotion without possession. Noa, the troupe leader, shows how a real grievance becomes ruin through opium and revenge. The parents, a resentful stepmother and a beating father, are the case against authority without love.

**Symbols and motifs.** Rain (every dream and every slip comes with rain); the garden where two children sheltered; the great-grandmother's necklace that ties four generations together; doubles and disguises (Hina as Hinako, Kikunoshin as Tenkiku); a cello recital invitation as a farewell (vol. 14); memory itself, kept by one lover and lost by the other.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo and josei time-slip romance (Red River and similar stories, from general knowledge; to verify), the Western double-identity tale (The Prince and the Pauper), and the Meiji-era romances of duty and love. Its question of consent in marriage echoes teaching shared by many faiths (Catholic canon law, Islamic law and halakha all require a bride's consent), even as its premarital union departs from them.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins in the end, but it is tempered by sacrifice rather than set against it. Traditionalism is treated with affection for its forms and severity toward its abuses. Feminism appears in Hinako's turn to study and in the exposure of men who exploited women. Might makes right belongs only to the villains.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 are a comic-romantic mystery of mistaken identity and the hunt for the necklace. Volumes 6-8 are the romance proper, ending in the leads' union. Volumes 9-12 turn darker: trafficking, suicide, opium, arson, kidnappings and a shooting, and the revelation that Taketora is from the future. Volumes 13-14 resolve the families, rescue Hinako and bring the farewell.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hina refuses Takaaki even though she loves him (vol. 6). Was that right? What would you have done?
2. Hinako finds joy in studying (vols. 12-13). Why does learning give her the courage to speak to her family?
3. Can a family arrange a marriage well? What went wrong with Hinako's?
4. Takaaki keeps his memories, and Hina loses hers. Which would be harder?
5. The leads become lovers before marriage while Hina is still in high school. What does your family think about that choice?
