**Premise.** Winter 1929, Robinsonville, Mississippi. Robert Johnson (RJ), a plantation worker whose girlfriend Virginia Travis is pregnant, wants to play the blues but cannot (Wikipedia; Brienza calls Virginia his wife). After a humiliating night at a juke joint, friends tell him of the crossroads ritual: play at midnight and the devil will give you the blues in exchange for your soul. A mysterious bluesman leaves a wooden guitar; RJ performs the ritual. He studies with Son House and Willie Brown without progress, then, drunk, finds himself in a cemetery by a church hearing an eerie tune, and returns to play astonishingly. Six months have vanished: House and Brown never met him, and Virginia has died in childbirth (Wikipedia; Brienza; ch. 1-4). A year later, training with mentor Ike Zimmerman, he meets the young outlaw Clyde Barrow, who insists RJ travel with him; Clyde uses him as a decoy in a burglary, white townspeople kidnap RJ to lynch him, and they cross the South against white supremacists led by a child-murderer, Stanley O. McDonald, while RJ's right hand grows ten fingers, a curse of the bargain (Wikipedia; Brienza; ch. 5-14 and after). Bonnie Parker also appears (Mania). The story stops mid-journey; Jason Thompson thought it lacked a real ending (via Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A gift bought at the crossroads costs what you love most, and in the Jim Crow South a Black man's talent and life are never safe.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Status and mating through display: RJ wants to be a great bluesman partly to win respect and to write a lullaby for his coming child (Mania). The cost falls on the next generation: Virginia and the child die (ch. 4). Groups defend boundaries violently (the lynch mob). |
| Pragmatic | The bargain is a shortcut: talent without the years of practice his teachers demand. The story shows the shortcut costing more than the slow road, while Ike Zimmerman's steady training is what RJ returns to (ch. 5 onward). |
| Religious | The supernatural frame is Southern folk religion: hoodoo (the prologue is titled "Hoodoo"), the crossroads devil, a cemetery by a church, a soul sold and a cursed hand. The devil is never made comic. Many Black churches of the era called the blues "the devil's music" (from general knowledge; to verify); the manga takes the legend literally, though Goboiano notes readers are often unsure whether RJ's visions are real. |
| Socio-cultural | Jim Crow Mississippi and the Depression-era South: plantation labor, juke joints, Prohibition (vol. 3's Japanese title means "the dry town"), lynching as a community event (Comic Book Bin). Critics split on the portrayal of Black characters: Brienza saw caricature close to minstrel imagery; Douresseaux praised its authenticity; Goboiano said it shows racism directly without softening. |

Outlook: Bleak — RJ gains his gift and loses his partner, child, months of his life and perhaps his soul; the road with Clyde is violence and flight, and the story stops without resolution.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a gift worth if it is bought, and what does a world that hates you allow you to keep? RJ wants to play the blues; the crossroads gives him that, and takes his partner, child and months of life.

**The answer the story gives.** The bargain is ruinous. RJ's talent is real but tainted; he is cursed (the ten-fingered hand) and haunted. The violence of the Jim Crow South follows him everywhere. In the published chapters the story offers no rescue: no repentance, no deliverance, no justice yet.

**Characters as arguments.** RJ argues for art at any price, and pays. Virginia stands for the life he traded away. Son House and Ike Zimmerman argue for the slow road of practice. Clyde Barrow argues for outlaw freedom and drags RJ into crime. McDonald and the mob are the case against racial hatred; the story gives them no sympathy.

**Symbols and motifs.** The crossroads; the wooden guitar left by a mysterious bluesman; the cemetery by a church; the lost six months; the ten-fingered hand (a curse and perhaps the source of his playing); song titles as chapter titles.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Faust legend; the Robert Johnson crossroads myth (which historians often trace to other bluesmen and to West African trickster figures (from general knowledge; to verify)); Southern Gothic (Graphic Novel Reporter, via Wikipedia); Bonnie and Clyde road stories. Christian readers will see a sobering parable about the soul; Black church readers may recall the old tension between the blues and the church.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult is real and terrible. Racial domination is condemned. Expressive individualism is tested and found costly. Critical attention to race cuts both ways: the story exposes Jim Crow, and one critic accuses its art of caricature.

**Shifts across the series.** Book One (JP vols. 1-2, ch. 0-13): the bargain, Virginia's death, Clyde, the lynching attempt and the curse; Southern Gothic horror. Book Two (vols. 3-4, ch. 14-28): the dry town and the road with Clyde and Bonnie (Mania), the white supremacists. Vol. 5 and ch. 35-38 (2015-2017, untranslated) continue the road; the story is unfinished.

**Discussion questions.**
1. RJ wanted to be great quickly. What did his shortcut cost, and who paid?
2. Son House and Ike Zimmerman taught him the slow way. Why do people prefer the devil story to the practice story?
3. How does the story show the racism of the 1930s South? Did any scenes make you uncomfortable, and why?
4. One reviewer felt the Black characters were drawn as caricatures; another praised the book's authenticity. Look at the art: what do you think?
5. What is the difference between Robert Johnson the real musician and RJ in the manga?
