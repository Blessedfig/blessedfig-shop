**Premise.** Kakushi Gotō is a popular manga artist whose hit is a crude, lewd gag series. He is also a devoted single father to Hime, about ten (fourth grade), and he is terrified that if she learns what he draws she will be teased or ashamed of him. So he leaves each morning in a suit as if he worked in an office, disguises his studio and keeps his assistants, editor and Hime's teacher (a fan of his work) in on the secret, which drives the comedy (Wikipedia; ANN Manga Guide). Hime's mother is gone: lost in an accident at sea (Wikipedia). Short flash-forwards show Hime at eighteen finding a hidden house in Kamakura full of her father's manuscripts and of birthday gift boxes prepared for every year up to her twentieth, and hint that something has happened to Kakushi (anime ep. 1-2, 5; Wikipedia). The ending reveals that Kakushi quit manga after tabloids exposed him and his long search for his missing wife, took a warehouse job, and was left in a coma by an accident there. He wakes with amnesia, thinking Hime is still ten, and begins drawing again; seeing his old manuscripts with Hime restores his memory, and he says his happiness is seeing her grown. Hime starts drawing manga herself, and Kakushi reunites his studio (anime ep. 12; Wikipedia; ANN finale review).

## Life message

**A parent's love does not depend on a respectable job, secrets kept out of love still need to be let go, and a child can accept a parent as he really is.**

| Socio-cultural | A satire of the manga industry (deadlines, editors, assistants) and of tabloid and online cruelty (ANN finale review), from an author who says he draws things he is embarrassed about (ANN, Hopper). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Hime's mother is never found, and Kakushi loses years to a coma and amnesia, but father and daughter are reunited with the secrets set aside, and both turn to drawing (ending, via anime ep. 12).
Hope source: F — the story's way through is family love: Hime accepts her father as he is, and his recovery comes from her and from their shared past (ANN finale review); small everyday joys (J) of their life in Nakameguro are the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What should a parent hide from a child, and why? Kakushi is a good father with a job he thinks will embarrass his daughter, so he builds his life around a secret.

**The answer the story gives.** Love matters more than the secret. Hime, at eighteen, finds the truth and is not ashamed; what hurt Kakushi was public shaming, not his daughter's judgment (ending). The secret, kept to protect her, ends up separating them for years.

**Characters as arguments.** Kakushi argues that a parent must shield a child from the world's judgment. Hime answers with acceptance. The tabloids argue that a private life is public entertainment and are the story's true villains. The absent mother argues through her gift boxes that love can plan ahead for every year (CBR).

**Symbols and motifs.** The suit as a disguise; the house in Kamakura that mirrors their old home (ep. 2); birthday boxes up to age twenty; the double-meaning title.

**Traditions it echoes.** The single-father comedy (Bunny Drop and others, Comics Beat); the Japanese gag manga about manga-making; the family secret revealed at the end.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Family loyalty is affirmed; shame-based reputation is gently critiqued; individual pride in one's work wins by the end.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes are almost all comedy with brief flash-forwards; the flash-forwards grow, and the final volume turns to the mystery and the reunion (Wikipedia; MangaUpdates).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Kakushi hide his job from Hime? Was he right to?
2. Hime is not upset when she finds out. Why do you think that is?
3. What do the mother's birthday boxes say about her?
4. The tabloids hurt Kakushi more than his secret did. What responsibility do people have when they share gossip?
5. Is there something you would feel embarrassed for your family to know? What would help you share it?
