**Premise.** Kurouzu-cho is a quiet, foggy seaside town. High-school student Kirie Goshima's boyfriend, Shuichi Saito, warns her that the town is cursed by spirals. His father becomes obsessed with them and dies by twisting his own body into a spiral; his mother, terrified of spirals, mutilates herself to be rid of them and dies (ch. 1-2). In loosely linked episodes the curse spreads: a classmate's scar becomes a devouring vortex (ch. 3), lovers twist their bodies together (ch. 5), Kirie's hair comes alive (ch. 6), slow students turn into snails (ch. 8), pregnant women drink blood (ch. 10-11), and a lighthouse burns those who climb it (ch. 9). Typhoons wreck the town; survivors crowd into old row houses, eat the snail people to survive, and cannot escape (ch. 12-17). Kirie and Shuichi finally reach the center, an ancient spiral city beneath Dragonfly Pond, where they embrace as their bodies twist together and the curse completes itself, to begin again when a new town is built (ch. 18-19) (Wikipedia; EBSCO; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**The story promotes no clear good life. It shows a universe that is indifferent to people, where obsession, fear and even escape all lead to the same end. What survives longest is loyalty between people who love each other, and the story does not say that loyalty wins.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Survival under total threat: the town devolves from neighbors to mobs, hoarders and cannibals (ch. 15-16), while the couple and Kirie's family protect each other to the end. Pregnancy and birth are turned into horror (ch. 10-11). |
| Pragmatic | Every practical response fails: fleeing, cutting off the cursed hair, destroying the kiln, organizing an escape. Shuichi's careful observation is right, but knowing does not help. |
| Religious | No god appears; the curse is impersonal and ancient. Some readers find echoes of fate and cosmic cycles; U.S. Catholic's reviewer values its sense of something beyond human understanding (2025). Faith frames weigh the despair of the ending against the story's refusal to make evil attractive. |
| Socio-cultural | A small town's conformity, gossip and family feuds become the curse's tools (ch. 5); in the row houses the crowd expels outsiders (ch. 13-17, anime summary). Ito subverts the friendly spiral of Japanese gag manga and draws on the long nagaya row houses of his childhood (Wikipedia, Development). |

Outlook: Bleak — nearly everyone in the town dies or is transformed, escape fails, and the curse completes itself and will repeat; only the lovers' last embrace softens the end.
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: no faith, effort or knowledge breaks the curse; the one thing that lasts is Kirie and Shuichi's love (L), and it does not save them.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

No one wins. Those who embrace the spiral, fear it or flee it all meet the same end; the story treats each response with sympathy and destroys them alike.

## Creator and publisher context

- Junji Ito, born July 31, 1963, in Sakashita (now Nakatsugawa), Gifu; drew from age 4, read Kazuo Umezu through his older sisters, worked as a dental technician from 1984, and debuted with Tomie (honorable mention, Kazuo Umezu Prize, 1987) (Wikipedia, Junji Ito).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, Development): he began with a story about people in a very long traditional row house, from his own childhood, and found the spiral in a mosquito coil; he researched spirals by staring at them, draining bathtubs, eating spiral foods and raising snails; in 2006 he said he was still unsure what the spiral stood for, perhaps infinity. He wanted to turn the warm spiral of Japanese gag manga into horror, and credits H. P. Lovecraft for the slow build of the curse.
- His other work includes Gyo, Junji Ito's Cat Diary (a self-parody) and Yukoku no Rasputin; he was a collaborator on the canceled game Silent Hills (Wikipedia).
- No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (no interview opened directly).
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Big Comic Spirits, a seinen magazine). VIZ Media in English; the deluxe edition is listed as 16+ (ANN). No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if the pattern behind everything, the shape of shells, galaxies and whirlpools, were hostile, or simply did not care about us? Uzumaki asks how people would live in a town that slowly realizes it is caught in such a pattern.

**The answer the story gives.** Nothing works. Mr. Saito embraces the spiral and is destroyed; his wife flees from it and is destroyed; the town fights, flees and adapts and is destroyed. The only thing the story leaves standing is love: Kirie and Shuichi hold each other as the curse finishes (ch. 19). Ito offers no moral beyond that; he has said he does not know what the spiral means (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Shuichi argues that seeing clearly is the beginning of wisdom; he is right and still lost. Kirie argues for staying loyal and staying human; she narrates, endures and chooses love at the end. The Saitos are the two extremes of obsession and phobia. Azami, Sekino and Yamaguchi argue for being noticed at any cost and are consumed by their vanity (ch. 3, 6, 7). The crowd in the row houses argues for survival over decency, and becomes monstrous.

**Symbols and motifs.** The spiral itself (Ito suggests infinity); hair; snails; the lighthouse; the row houses joined into one labyrinth; Dragonfly Pond and the ancient city beneath it; time that speeds up away from the center and stops at it.

**Traditions it echoes.** Lovecraft's cosmic horror, which Ito names as an influence; Kazuo Umezu's horror manga; Japanese tales of cursed places. Some readers hear Ecclesiastes or Buddhist teaching on attachment and cycles, though the story draws on neither.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Cosmic nihilism is the story's frame, but not moral nihilism: cruelty is never approved. Absurdism and existentialism appear in characters who keep acting in a senseless world. Vanity and crowd conformity are punished. No faith, science or politics is offered as an answer.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-6): the Saitos, Azami, the twisted lovers, Kirie's hair. Vol. 2 (ch. 7-12): episodic horrors (the snail, the lighthouse, the mosquitoes, the umbilical cord) and the first typhoon, which critics such as Greg Hackmann found weaker (via Wikipedia). Vol. 3 (ch. 13-19): the town's collapse, the row houses, escape and the ending; "Galaxies" is a side story set earlier.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mr. Saito loves spirals and Mrs. Saito fears them, and both are destroyed. What is the story saying about obsession?
2. Shuichi sees the danger first, but few listen. Why do people ignore warnings?
3. Is the ending hopeless, or is there something good in Kirie and Shuichi's last choice?
4. Why do you think Ito never explains the spiral?
5. Why do people enjoy being frightened by stories like this?
