**Premise.** Sumire Iwaya, 27, is a Tokyo University and Harvard graduate and a reporter at a major newspaper, admired at work as cool and capable (AniList). Then everything falls apart: she punches a superior who harasses her at a party and is demoted to the lifestyle section, and her boyfriend of five years leaves her for another woman he got pregnant (AniList; Wikipedia characters). She finds an injured young man in a cardboard box outside her building, nurses him, and, half joking, offers to let him stay as her pet; he accepts, and she names him Momo after her childhood dog (Rule 1). Momo, really Takeshi Goda, 20, is a dancer who left home after a fight with his parents (AniList). Sumire's college crush Hasumi, the ideal man on paper, joins her office and asks her out; she hides Momo from him by calling him her second cousin (v1). Over 14 volumes Sumire juggles Hasumi, Momo, romantic rivals (Momo's ex Rumi, Hasumi's admirer Fukushima), her sisters and her career, gets engaged to Hasumi (v10), and finally chooses Momo, who becomes her boyfriend and moves to Belgium for his dancing (v13). The last volume has Sumire meet Momo's family and the two talk about having a baby, while Hasumi proposes to Fukushima (v14) (Wikipedia chapter list; Kodansha USA).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do we really want in a partner? Sumire has always wanted to be admired; she gets the man everyone would admire, and finds she can only breathe at home with a stray she calls her pet.

**The answer the story gives.** A partner who lets you be yourself. Wikipedia's reception sums it up: Hasumi stands for the status ideal, Momo for comfort and compatibility, and Sumire chooses Momo. The story ends not in pure freedom but in commitment, family and talk of a child (v14).

**Characters as arguments.** Sumire argues for control and image, and slowly loses the argument with herself. Momo argues for openness, play and art. Hasumi argues for doing everything right and is not mocked for it; he finds a partner of his own (v14). Fukushima and Chinfei show love used as a strategy (v7-8, v14). Sumire's sisters frame the choices: conventional Shinobu, rebellious Akane (v10).

**Symbols and motifs.** The "Rules" (each chapter is a rule for keeping a pet), the cardboard box (Rule 1), the dog Momo was named for, Sumire's cigarettes, dancing, and the haircut Momo gives himself when he decides to grow up (v12, ANN).

**Traditions it echoes.** The fairy tale of the beast who is really a prince, the screwball comedy of mismatched lovers, and the josei story of a working woman's private life; Jason Thompson calls it "a fairytale romance" (via Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: Sumire's happiness depends on dropping her mask. Feminism is present in her career and her answer to harassment, but the story is more interested in her heart. Traditionalism is critiqued in its status ideals but honored in its ending.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3: the pet arrangement, Hasumi's courtship and comedy. Volumes 4-9: rivals, jealousy, Hong Kong (v7-8), and Sumire's growing confusion. Volumes 10-12: engagement to Hasumi, the vacation predator, Momo's family troubles and the turn. Volumes 13-14: Momo as boyfriend, Belgium, family and the future.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sumire calls Momo her pet. Why does that game work for them, and when does it stop working?
2. What does Hasumi have that Momo does not, and the other way around?
3. Sumire hides Momo from everyone. What does keeping secrets cost her?
4. Sumire was demoted for punching a man who harassed her. What should she have done, and what should her company have done?
5. Is the ending a choice for freedom or for commitment?
