**Premise.** When he was five, a young noble, Viktor (called 'the Duke'), was cursed by a witch: any living thing he touches dies. His mother disowned him and sent him to an old villa in the forest with only an elderly butler, Rob. Two years before the story opens, Rob brought in a maid, Alice Lendrott, the Duke's childhood playmate and daughter of the family's former head maid. Alice loves him and teases him constantly, flashing skin and leaning close enough that one touch would kill her; he loves her back but cannot touch her. Seeking the witch who cursed him, the Duke befriends the witches Cuff (half human, a circus performer) and Zain (who can manipulate time), attends a witches' sabbath (ch. 38-41), and meets the masked witch leader Daleth, who falls for his younger brother Walter. The culprit is Daleth's twin Sade, thought dead; she also put Alice's mother Sharon into an endless sleep. With Zain's time magic the friends travel into the past, where Alice reaches the lonely woman behind Sade's cruelty; Sade lifts the curse, heals her sister's scars and returns to the present to make amends. Freed, the Duke is offered his place in the family, but when Alice resigns so as not to hold him back, he chases her train and proposes (ch. 217). The last chapter shows Viktor and Alice, now married, leaving the villa with Rob a year after the curse was lifted (ch. 223; fan wiki).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you love someone you can never touch? The Duke kills whatever he touches; Alice stays within arm's reach every day. The series asks what love is when its most basic expression is impossible.

**The answer the story gives.** Love is presence and faithfulness. Alice's teasing is how she stays close; the withered white rose she takes from his hand means, she tells him, to stay together until death (Wikipedia). The curse is broken only when the same kind of patient closeness is offered to the villain: Alice enters Sade's thoughts and comforts the lonely woman there (fan wiki).

**Characters as arguments.** The Duke argues for gentleness under rejection; he never seeks revenge. Alice argues for bold, unashamed love. Rob is loyal service that becomes fatherhood. Gerbera is duty and appearances, softened in the end. Sade is the case for compassion toward the cruel: her curse came from grief and jealousy, and kindness, not defeat, ends it. Daleth, hiding a scarred face, is shame healed by being seen.

**Symbols and motifs.** Gloves that protect no one; the withered white rose; the piano the Duke plays alone and finally with Alice (ch. 223); the caged plague-doctor costume he wears to town; masks (Daleth) and mirrors as doors to the witches' world.

**Traditions it echoes.** Fairy tales of curses broken by love (Beauty and the Beast, Sleeping Beauty in Sharon's endless sleep), Gothic romance, and Japanese teasing romantic comedy. Its forgiveness of the villain echoes Christian love of enemies and Buddhist compassion for many readers, though the story names neither.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Found family is the reward; witchcraft is a varied world rather than an evil; virtue (patience, fidelity, forgiveness) carries the plot; choosing love over station is endorsed.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 (ch. 1-68) are mostly villa comedy and meeting the witches; vols. 6-10 (ch. 69-141) bring the mother, the circus, the wizard school and Sade's identity; vols. 11-16 (ch. 142-223) are the time-travel arc, the curse's end, the proposal and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Alice teases the Duke all the time. When is teasing loving, and when is it unkind?
2. The Duke's mother sent him away. Was she wrong, afraid, or both?
3. Sade did terrible things, and she is forgiven. Do you think she deserved it?
4. The Duke gives up being head of his family to be with Alice. What would you have chosen?
5. What does it mean to stay close to someone when you can't fix their problem?
