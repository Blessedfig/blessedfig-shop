**Premise.** A thousand years after the 'Seven Days of Fire' destroyed industrial civilization, a toxic fungal forest, the Sea of Corruption, guarded by giant insects called Ohmu, is spreading over the land. Nausicaä, the teenage princess of the small Valley of the Wind, studies the forest and can calm the insects. When her ailing father cannot, she leads the Valley's forces as a vassal of the Torumekian empire in its invasion of the Dorok lands, a theocracy ruled by a 'Holy Emperor' and his priests (vols. 1-2). The Torumekians slaughter the city of Pejite; the Doroks unleash an engineered mold that sets off a stampede of insects and floods their own country with the forest, killing huge numbers on both sides (vols. 2-4). Nausicaä learns from the Forest People that the forest is cleansing the poisoned earth (vols. 4-5). She refuses to treat powerful beings as gods, befriends her rival, the Torumekian princess Kushana, takes charge of a revived God Warrior who believes she is his mother and names it Ohma (vols. 6-7), and travels to the Doroks' holy city of Shuwa. In its Crypt she learns that the old world's scientists engineered the forest and today's humans, and stored eggs of a purified humanity to replace them. She rejects their plan and has Ohma destroy the Crypt, telling the survivors that they must live (vol. 7) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Every life matters, even an enemy's or an insect's, and people must choose to live and care for one another in a damaged world rather than hand their future to any power, ancient, scientific or divine, that promises a purified one.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | An ecology story: a forest and its insects adapt, die and regrow; humans have been engineered to survive pollution and cannot live in clean air (vol. 7). The Ohmu sacrifice themselves to contain the mold (vols. 4-5). Survival of peoples drives every war. |
| Pragmatic | Nausicaä is a scientist: she grows forest plants with clean water and finds the soil, not the forest, is poisoned (vol. 1). Kurotowa the cynic survives by switching sides. The story is skeptical of grand solutions; Miyazaki calls the idea of an ecosystem with a purpose naive (1994 interview). |
| Religious | Central and complex. The Dorok theocracy, its priests and its prophecy of the 'Blue-Clad One' (vols. 2-4) turn out to rest on the Crypt's manipulation (vol. 7). Scholars read Nausicaä both as a Christ-like savior (Sasaki, Shimizu, Napier) and as an animist (Aoi, Ogihara-Schuck, who argues Miyazaki makes animism prevail over a dualistic, Christian-style worldview) (Wikipedia). A parent's blog review found the closing speeches more pantheistic than the reviewer liked. |
| Socio-cultural | Written during Japan's ecology boom; Miyazaki cites the Minamata mercury poisoning (Wikipedia). Empires, refugees, an outcast people (the Worm Handlers, whom Miyazaki called the lowest caste, Wikipedia), and two warrior princesses. ANN's Jason Thompson calls it as grim as Grave of the Fireflies (Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the Crypt is destroyed and the wars end, but millions have died, Yupa and others are lost, and humanity's long-term survival is uncertain; the ending offers no tidy resolution (ANN), only the choice to live.
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting life: Nausicaä's care for every creature and her refusal to let anyone decide who deserves to exist; Miyazaki says hope means enduring hardship with the people you care about (1994 interview), so family and community (F) are close behind.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hayao Miyazaki (born 1941; from general knowledge) worked as an animator at Toei, Nippon Animation and TMS and directed The Castle of Cagliostro (1979) before Animage invited him to draw a manga on the condition it would never be filmed; the 1984 film followed anyway (Wikipedia).
- Influences he named or critics noted: the Heian tale The Lady Who Loved Insects, the Odyssey's Nausicaa, Le Guin's Earthsea, Aldiss's Hothouse, Moebius, the Minamata pollution case and the botanist Sasuke Nakao (Wikipedia).
- On the ending (1994 interview, nausicaa.net translation): he always expected it to end this way; he rejects the idea of an ecosystem with a purpose; for him hope means enduring hardship with the people you care about; he does not define God and speaks of spirit in nature in animist terms. He said working on the manga for 12 years changed his views on life and politics (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Tokuma Shoten (Animage). VIZ Media publishes it in English, rated T.
- No controversies involving the manga were found; translator Toren Smith had criticized the cut US film Warriors of the Wind before Miyazaki chose his studio for the manga translation (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If the world was ruined by people and can only be healed by forces beyond their control, who has the right to decide the future? Nausicaä begins by asking how to survive the poisoned forest and ends by asking whether a planned, purified world is worth the lives it would cost.

**The answer the story gives.** No one may decide for everyone who deserves to live. Nausicaä rejects the Crypt's plan to replace engineered humanity with a pure one and tells the survivors they must live (vol. 7). Miyazaki rejects an ecosystem with a purpose and locates hope in enduring hardship with those you love (1994 interview). The answer is costly: the war's dead are not restored, and humanity's long-term survival is uncertain (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Nausicaä argues for compassion that includes enemies and insects. Kushana shows a warrior who can change. The Vai Emperor and Namulith show power that eats itself; the emperor's dying warning that one killing leads to endless killing is the story's anti-war thesis (Wikipedia). Miralupa shows a holy ruler who hated his people; his redemption by mercy shows the story's hope. The Master of the Garden offers beauty and escape; the Master of the Crypt offers a perfect future. Ohma, a weapon who learns from a 'mother', asks whether a judge of humanity can learn love.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Ohmu's blue eyes and red rage; the glider and the wind; the clean soil under the forest; the blue-clad figure of prophecy; the Crypt as a tomb of the old world; the God Warrior as a nuclear weapon that thinks.

**Traditions it echoes.** Animism and Shinto reverence for nature; Buddhist compassion; for some Christian scholars, a Christ-like self-sacrifice and resurrection (Wikipedia); the anti-war tradition of postwar Japan; Le Guin's ecological fantasy; and a long Western debate about utopias engineered from above.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Environmentalism and compassion are affirmed. Transhumanism, authoritarian empire, theocracy and outcome-based sacrifice (Utilitarianism) are rejected. Pacifism is affirmed but tested: the heroine fights, kills once in rage and ends with a destructive choice. Spirituality is animist rather than doctrinal.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-14): the Valley, Pejite, Kushana and the invasion (close to the film). Vols. 3-4 (ch. 15-27): the mold and the great stampede. Vol. 5 and part of 6 (ch. 28-43): the Forest People, despair, Miralupa's end and the Garden. Part of vol. 6 and vol. 7 (ch. 44-59): Namulith, Ohma, Shuwa and the Crypt. The tone darkens and grows more philosophical as it goes.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nausicaä cares for the insects that everyone else fears. Why?
2. The forest turns out to be man-made. Does that change whether it is worth protecting?
3. The Crypt offered a perfect future if the present people were allowed to die out. Why does Nausicaä refuse? Was she right?
4. The Vai Emperor warns that killing one traitor leads to endless killing. Where do you see that in the story?
5. Nausicaä says people must live even without knowing the future. What gives her hope?
