**Premise.** Fuyuhiko Unoki has been bullied since elementary school and expects nothing better in high school. On his first day Kanade Toramaru, the school's most feared delinquent, tells him he is hers now: she means to ask him out, but he hears an order to become her errand boy. She believes they are dating; he believes he is her servant (vol. 1). The comedy runs on the misunderstanding as her loyal followers Matsuri (jealous and devoted to Toramaru) and Yutaka (the only one who sees the truth) watch, Toramaru's gentle giant of a younger brother Tasuku coaches her with a shōjo manga (vol. 2), and Aika Youdou, a childhood acquaintance who bullied Unoki, turns up as a rival (vols. 4-5). The two confess but Unoki asks for time (vol. 5); Yutaka confesses to Matsuri (ch. 67); Toramaru's disapproving mother breaks up their first real date (ch. 73); and Unoki finally confesses properly and they start dating for real (ch. 75), with Matsuri and Yutaka also together by the end (TV Tropes; The Outerhaven; Anime UK News).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when two people mean completely different things by the same words? Toramaru tells Unoki he is hers and means love; Unoki hears an order. The series asks how much of life is shaped by what we assume others mean, and what it takes to say plainly what we feel.

**The answer the story gives.** Honesty, and the courage it needs. The misunderstanding lasts until Unoki, who has never stood up for himself, makes a proper confession (ch. 75). Along the way he learns that his worth is not his usefulness to others (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Unoki argues that gentleness is not weakness. Toramaru argues that strength can protect rather than dominate. Matsuri's jealous loyalty and Yutaka's quiet patience argue for two kinds of love, and Yutaka's wins. Aika argues, badly, that bullying can be a twisted form of affection, and ends by letting go.

**Symbols and motifs.** The errand: what Unoki thinks is servitude is, to Toramaru, time together. The shōjo manga Tasuku gives his sister as a romance guide (vol. 2). Unoki's kitten named Toramaru (vol. 2).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese delinquent comedy and the misunderstanding rom-com (reviewers compare Toramaru to Taiga of Toradora!); the old fable of the fearsome creature with a gentle heart.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue and community are affirmed; reversed gender roles are played for warmth rather than argument; the same-sex couple is simply part of the cast.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 are pure misunderstanding comedy; volumes 3-4 bring progress (she calls him her boyfriend to his face, vol. 4); volume 5 adds the rival Aika and a stalled confession; volumes 6-7 bring the festival kidnapping, family complications and the resolution (ch. 58-77).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Unoki think Toramaru wanted an errand boy? What would you have assumed?
2. Toramaru fights people who hurt Unoki. Is that a good way to help?
3. Aika bullied Unoki but says she liked him. Can bullying ever be affection?
4. What helped Unoki finally say what he felt?
5. How do you know when someone means something different from what they said?
