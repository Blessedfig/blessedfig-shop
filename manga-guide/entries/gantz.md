**Premise.** High-school student Kei Kurono and his childhood friend Masaru Kato are killed by a subway train while trying to save a drunk man. They wake in a Tokyo apartment with other people who have just died and a large black sphere called Gantz. Gantz sends them, in black suits and with strange weapons, to hunt aliens hidden in the city. Each kill earns points; 100 points lets a player go free with memories erased, revive a dead player, or take a stronger weapon. Many die. Kurono, at first selfish and sex-obsessed, slowly becomes a leader. In Phase 2 a joint mission with a brutal Osaka team ends in a battle with the alien Nurarihyon. Then the "Catastrophe" arrives: a race of giant humanoids invades Earth, farms and eats humans, and Kurono and Kato fight their way through the mothership. Near the end, god-like beings who supplied the Gantz technology tell the humans that God does not exist and that human life matters no more than an ant's (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life is found in caring for particular people (a lover, a little brother, a friend) and protecting them, even in a universe that shows no sign of caring back. Selfishness, cruelty and pleasure-seeking are shown as both common and ugly; altruism is shown as rare and costly.**

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, most theistic frames land at C or D: the story honors altruism and love, but its final word on God comes from beings who deny Him and treat souls as worthless, and the fan service is not framed as wrong. Secular humanist and Satanist frames grade it best, reading the ending as humans making meaning in an indifferent cosmos. Hindu and Buddhist readers see an echo of rebirth in ch. 370 but no path beyond it, and Buddhist readers may object to the temple arc. On depiction, almost every frame rates the explicitness Significant; only Shinto, Pagan, Scientologist, secular humanist and Satanist frames, which have no content codes, rate it Discuss.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Love and stubborn survival win; the god-like powers are not defeated, and the story never answers whether human life has worth beyond what people give each other.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hiroya Oku (born 1967 per general knowledge; to verify) also created Hen and Inuyashiki. In a 2016 GIGAZINE interview he said Gantz came from his love of the Hissatsu period dramas and science fiction, that he aimed for a "little mysterious" SF feel, and that he mixes humor, romance, parody and "grotesque scenes, erotic scenes" to keep readers engaged. He said the reader he writes for is himself. He said he draws large-breasted characters to express three-dimensional form and motion (GIGAZINE, opened).
- Wikipedia (opened) reports that Oku sought realism, said some events reflected his views on world issues, said he did not self-censor, and told assistants before serialization that all main characters except Kurono would die.
- No statement from Oku about religion, God or the meaning of the final reveal was found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (on the final arc's intent).
- In August 2026 Oku said he plans to retire from manga after his current series Kanrekihime, saying he no longer has the energy to draw (Popverse, opened; ANN headline, not opened).
- Publisher: Shueisha. English: Dark Horse Comics, which shrink-wrapped the series for mature readers and printed more than 20,000 copies of vol. 1; its licensing director cited the weapons, suits, bike and "fan service" as appeal (Publishers Weekly, opened). Dark Horse rates it 18+.
- No reputable reports of personal controversy involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you die and wake up in a game run by something that does not care about you, what is left to live for? Gantz asks this from its first chapter, where two teenagers die saving a stranger nobody else would help, and it asks it again at the end, when the powers behind the game tell humanity it is no more important than ants.

**The answer the story gives.** The story gives two answers and does not fully reconcile them. The cosmic answer, voiced by the beings in ch. 369-370 (per TV Tropes), is that God does not exist, emotions are electrical signals, souls exist and reincarnate but are nothing special, and human acts are meaningless. The human answer is acted out rather than argued: Kurono fights his way through an alien mothership to get back to Tae, Kato protects his little brother, and the survivors go home to the people they love (ch. 383). Grimoire of Horror sums up the human answer as "our apparent insignificancy does not diminish our value." Critics disagree on whether the ending earns it; several call it rushed.

**Characters as arguments.** Kato is the argument for altruism: he dies helping strangers, prefers capture weapons to lethal ones, and is styled by fans as a messianic figure. Kurono is the argument that a selfish person can become a loving one through suffering. Nishi and Izumi are arguments for self-interest and violence as identity; the story makes them dangerous and, in Izumi's case, gives him a redemptive death. The Osaka team is hedonism without limits and ends torn apart by Nurarihyon. The giants are a civilization that treats humans as livestock, yet Sakurai's killing of a giant infant (ch. 340) breaks him, so the line between human and monster blurs. The beings behind Gantz are the argument from cosmic indifference.

**Symbols and motifs.** The black sphere: a blank, mocking god-substitute with a man inside it. Points: life turned into a video game score (Hypercritic). Copies and clones: the question of whether a rebuilt person is the same person. The subway platform: the bystander's choice. The giants' farms: humanity on the other end of the food chain.

**Traditions it echoes.** Existentialist fiction about meaning in an absurd world. Japanese death-game fiction. Cosmic horror, where human beings are small before vast indifferent powers. Buddhist rebirth, in the claim that souls reincarnate. The Hissatsu period dramas that Oku cites as an influence (GIGAZINE).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism and naturalism are given the last cosmic word but are lived against by the heroes. Existentialism and secular humanism are what the heroes act out. Hedonism and might-makes-right are shown in villains who die. Religion appears as monsters (the temple statues), as helpless prayer, and in the beings' denial of God; no character defends a faith. Feminist critics argue that the narrative's own gaze contradicts its moral center.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes (ch. 1-58) are shock horror with heavy fan service and a hateful lead. The temple arc (to ch. 91) kills most of the cast. Middle volumes build Kurono into a leader and end Phase 1 with his death (ch. 237). The Osaka arc (ch. 238-280) is the bloodiest set piece. The Catastrophe (ch. 303 onward) turns the series into planetary war and, at the end, metaphysics.

**Discussion questions.**
1. In chapter 1, everyone on the platform watches and only Kato jumps down. What would you have done, and why?
2. The beings behind Gantz say there is no God and human life means nothing. The heroes go home to the people they love. Which answer does the story leave you believing, and what does your family believe?
3. Sakurai breaks down after killing a giant child. Does it matter that the enemy has families too?
4. Many reviewers say the women in Gantz are drawn mainly to be looked at. How does that sit with the story's message about caring for people?
5. Kurono is the original and also a copy. If a perfect copy of you were made, would it be you?
