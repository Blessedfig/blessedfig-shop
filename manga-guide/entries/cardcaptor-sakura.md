**Premise.** Sakura Kinomoto, a ten-year-old fourth grader in the town of Tomoeda, opens a book in her father's basement and scatters the Clow Cards, magical spirits made by the sorcerer Clow Reed. The book's guardian, the small winged beast Kero (Cerberus), makes her a Cardcaptor (v1). Her best friend and second cousin Tomoyo films her captures and sews her a new costume for each one; Syaoran Li, a rival from Clow's Hong Kong lineage, becomes an ally (v2). Sakura's widowed father Fujitaka and teasing older brother Toya look after her. After she captures every card, the judge Yue, who is secretly Toya's best friend Yukito, tests her (v6). In the second half Eriol, a transfer student who carries Clow Reed's memories, makes her change the Clow Cards into her own Sakura Cards (v7-11). Along the way the series shows many kinds of love: Yukito and Toya become a couple (v9-10), Tomoyo loves Sakura, Sakura's classmate Rika is engaged to her teacher, and at the end Syaoran confesses and Sakura realizes she loves him too (v12). Two years later he comes back to stay (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Love in all its forms, given freely and never forced, together with kindness and doing your best, carries you through anything; nobody has to be defeated for things to turn out all right.**

## Creator and publisher context

- CLAMP: writer Nanase (Ageha) Ohkawa and artists Mokona, Tsubaki Nekoi and Satsuki Igarashi; formed as a twelve-member doujinshi circle in the mid-1980s and debuted with RG Veda (1989); other works include Tokyo Babylon, X, Magic Knight Rayearth, Chobits, Tsubasa and xxxHolic (Wikipedia, Clamp; later works from general knowledge; to verify).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, Production): Ohkawa wanted a magical-girl series for Nakayoshi with a heroine the readers' age; the planned theme was that if you try your best it will work out; the central theme is love and human relationships, presented without judgment; Tomoyo and Sakura did not end up together because Sakura did not love Tomoyo romantically. Ohkawa planned the plot alone and gave the artists one chapter at a time; Mokona drew Tomoyo blushing at Toya before learning Tomoyo loved Sakura.
- Satsuki Igarashi confirmed in 2022 that Toya and Yukito are a couple in the sequel (Wikipedia, character list). No statements by CLAMP on the teacher-pupil romance were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators' comments about the age-gap romances.**
- Publishers: Kodansha (Nakayoshi). English: Tokyopop and Dark Horse (expired); Kodansha USA Collector's Edition, rated 10+.
- Controversies: the American Family Association's 2002 objection to a Taco Bell promotion (cards like tarot) and the censorship of same-sex love in the Nelvana dub (Wikipedia). No controversy involving the creators was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is love, and who is it for? Sakura begins with a crush on her brother's best friend, a fondness for a teacher, a devoted cousin and a rival. By the end she has to tell apart loving someone and loving someone most (v10-12).

**The answer the story gives.** Every love is real and none has to be defeated. Yukito tells Sakura gently that he is not the one she loves most (v10); Tomoyo is content that Sakura is happy; Yue learns to love a new master as a friend. The final judgment is won because Sakura will not hurt Yue (v6). The planned theme, Ohkawa says, is that if you try your best it will work out (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Sakura argues for kindness and effort. Tomoyo argues for love that asks nothing back. Syaoran argues that rivals can become the people you love. Toya argues that protecting someone can mean giving up your own power (v9). Yue argues for loyalty to the dead and learns to live for the living. Rika and Terada are presented as one more sweet love, which is the place where most readers part ways with the story.

**Symbols and motifs.** The cards as personalities to befriend, not tools; Tomoyo's costumes and camera; the sun and moon (Cerberus and Yue); the star (Sakura's own power); handmade teddy bears exchanged by Sakura and Syaoran; flowers on almost every page (Wikipedia).

**Traditions it echoes.** The magical-girl genre (refreshed, critics say), Western magic and Chinese sorcery, Japanese school romance, and CLAMP's own interest in fate. Its "it will be all right" optimism echoes a feel-good faith without a god.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian love and self-expression are celebrated. Magic is fantasy furniture, not a teaching. Progressive acceptance of same-sex love is part of the series' warmth. Nothing is critiqued, which is both its gentleness and its blind spot: the story does not distinguish a ten-year-old's romance with her teacher from any other love.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 are the Clow Card captures and the final judgment, with the teacher romances introduced early (ch. 9, v4). Volumes 7-12 turn to Eriol, the Sakura Cards and the resolution of every romance, ending in Syaoran's confession and return.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sakura will not hurt Yue, even to win. Why does that matter?
2. Tomoyo loves Sakura and wants her happy even when Sakura loves someone else. What kind of love is that?
3. Rika's teacher gives her a ring. What do you think about a teacher and a student in a romance? Why do adults have rules about this?
4. Toya gives up his powers for Yukito. What would you give up for someone you love?
5. The story says if you do your best it will work out. Is that always true?
