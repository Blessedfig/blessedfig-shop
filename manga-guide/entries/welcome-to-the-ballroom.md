**Premise.** Tatara Fujita, a third-year middle schooler with no dream and no hobby, is being shaken down by bullies when Kaname Sengoku, a towering professional ballroom champion on a motorcycle, scares them off and drags him into the Ogasawara Dance Studio. There Tatara finds that his quiet classmate Shizuku Hanaoka is the national amateur champion with her partner, the genius Kiyoharu Hyōdō. The studio lets him pay late, and he practices the basic box step all night (ch. 1, anime ep. 1) (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Tatara's gift is copying what he sees. He stands in for an injured Hyōdō with Shizuku at the Mikasanomiya Cup (ch. 4-7), then partners the shy Mako Akagi and frames her so well that she is named ballroom queen (Tenpei Cup, ch. 8-16). In high school he pairs with Chinatsu Hiyama, a strong-willed girl who used to dance the leader's part; the two fight, learn to share the lead, and grow into a real couple on the floor under coach Marisa Hyōdō (ch. 18-54), against rivals such as the traditionalist Masami Kugimiya. Later chapters move to a training studio called Bres and ask Tatara what he dances for (ch. 55-72) (Japanese Wikipedia; fan wiki; AniList reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is worth giving your life to, and who are you when you find it? Tatara begins with nothing he can say he loves (Japanese Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** A craft practiced wholeheartedly, and a partner. Tatara's gift is reading and reflecting others, which first makes Mako shine (ch. 16) and later forces him to find his own expression with Chinatsu (ch. 18-54) and in the Bres arc (ch. 55-66).

**Characters as arguments.** Hyōdō argues for genius and devotion. Sengoku argues for raw talent and force. Kugimiya argues for tradition and perfection, while hiding insecurity. Gaju argues for dominance and learns its cost. Chinatsu argues that she can lead; she and Tatara find a "double lead" (ch. 47).

**Symbols and motifs.** The box step (the humble basic done all night, ch. 1); the flower and the picture frame (ch. 11); the "Wild Horse" tamed (ch. 46, "The Taming of the Shrew"); lead and follow.

**Traditions it echoes.** Sports manga like Hajime no Ippo and Baby Steps (AniList review); the apprentice-and-master story. Religious readers may hear Colossians 3:23 or the Hindu image of dance as devotion; Buddhist readers may ask about attachment to winning.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and self-expression are celebrated together; feminism's question about gendered roles is raised inside the sport; traditionalism is respected and debated.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-16): discovery and the first cups. Volumes 5-9 (ch. 17-41): the new partnership with Chinatsu. Volumes 10-11 (ch. 42-57): the Tokyo tournament finals. Volume 12 and uncollected chapters (ch. 58-72): Bres, a studio change and new questions (fan wiki; Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tatara practiced the box step all night. What would you practice that hard?
2. What is the difference between leading and dominating a partner?
3. Sengoku helps Tatara but also hits him. Is he a good mentor?
4. Chinatsu used to lead. Why is it hard for her to follow, and why is it hard for Tatara to lead?
5. What does Tatara mean when he says he wants something he can say he loves?
