**Premise.** An alternate Sengoku Japan, "Hinomoto," has been ruled for some 150 years by oni, giant demons who killed the old warlords. People are taught that the oni are kind gods and that the bushi (samurai) were cruel tyrants; bushi descendants are made to carry chained swords as a badge of shame and live as outcasts (fan wiki, Oni; Wikipedia). Fifteen-year-old Musashi, about to become a miner, secretly keeps a childhood promise with his friend Kojiro Kanemaki, a despised bushi descendant, to become the greatest warriors and overthrow the oni (ch. 1-7). Leaving town, they free Tsugumi Hattori from the abusive leader of the Kosameda band (ch. 8-17), seek kitetsu swords that can cut an oni's horn (ch. 18-42), and join the Uesugi alliance's war on the eight-headed Yamata no Orochi (ch. 43-115). Musashi carries the "Obsidian Goddess," a being who once split from the greatest demon god, and is hunted by the Obsidian Eight, a band of corrupted warriors led by Shiro Inukai (ch. 28 onward). In the last volumes the Black Demon God swallows the land and the united bushi clans fight Shiro and his band to save humanity (vols. 19-22) (fan wiki; Kodansha synopses).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do when everything you have been taught is a lie? Musashi's town thanks the oni as kind gods and despises the bushi as tyrants. The first chapters ask whether he will accept a safe life in the mines or keep a childish promise to fight.

**The answer the story gives.** Seek the truth, keep your promise, and do not try to do it alone. Musashi and Kojiro leave town (ch. 7); they help Tsugumi leave her abuser (ch. 15-17); and Musashi earns the Goddess's power only when he says he cannot win alone (ch. 38-40).

**Characters as arguments.** Musashi argues for freedom and friendship. Kojiro argues for pride in a heritage the world despises. Tsugumi argues, by leaving, that loyalty to an abuser is not loyalty. Hideo argues for control and loses. Shiro argues that power can build an ideal world and becomes a monster (vol. 21). The Five Great Generals argue that rival clans must unite.

**Symbols and motifs.** The chained sword as a badge of shame. Kitetsu blades that reject a soul (ch. 22-23). Soul colors. The motorcycle-like kitetsuki. The Obsidian Goddess. The black heart.

**Traditions it echoes.** The samurai epic (Musashi and Kojiro are named after Miyamoto Musashi and Sasaki Kojiro; my own framing), shonen adventure, the Yamata no Orochi myth, and stories of exposing false gods.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Bushido is honored as the people's lost dignity, with Shiro as its corruption. Authoritarianism is the enemy. Communitarianism wins the war. Buddhist imagery is borrowed for the false religion without Buddhist teaching being argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-17): Tatsuyama and the Kosameda band. Vols. 3-5 (ch. 18-42): kitetsu swords and the Obsidian Goddess. Vols. 6-13 (ch. 43-115): the Awaji Island war on Orochi. Vols. 13-18 (ch. 116-137): the Hojo arc and the Obsidian Eight. Vols. 19-22 (ch. 138-156): the Black Demon God and the final battle.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The people of Hinomoto thank the oni for ruling them. How could a whole town believe something so false?
2. Tsugumi finds it hard to leave Hideo even though he hurts her. Why might that be, and how do her friends help?
3. Musashi only gets the Goddess's power when he admits he cannot win alone. Why does that matter?
4. Shiro wants to build a perfect world. Why does his power turn him into a monster?
5. The bushi descendants were made to carry chained swords. What does the story say about blaming children for their ancestors?
