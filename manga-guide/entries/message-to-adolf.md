**Premise.** In 1936 the Japanese reporter Sohei Toge goes to Berlin to cover the Olympics and finds that his younger brother Isao, a student with Communist ties, has been murdered and every record of him erased. Isao had mailed to Japan documents said to prove that Adolf Hitler had Jewish ancestry. In Kobe two boys are best friends: Adolf Kamil, son of a Jewish baker, and Adolf Kaufmann, son of a Nazi consular official and a Japanese mother. Kaufmann is sent to an Adolf Hitler School, forced to shoot Kamil's father as a loyalty test, and becomes a committed SS officer; he loves a Jewish girl, Eliza, and smuggles her to Kamil in Japan. Toge is hunted by the Gestapo and the Japanese military police while the documents pass from hand to hand (a teacher, Ms. Ogi; an army officer's son in the Sorge spy ring). Kaufmann returns to Japan in 1945 to recover them, rapes Eliza, now Kamil's fiancee, and finds the documents only after Hitler is dead. Decades later, Kaufmann fights with the PLO in Lebanon and Kamil serves in the Israeli army; after Kamil's unit kills Kaufmann's wife and daughter, the two duel and Kamil kills him. In the 1980s Toge visits Kamil's family in Israel after Kamil dies in a bombing, and resolves to write a book telling the story of the three Adolfs and where "justice" can lead (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; reviews).

## Life message

**Hatred taught as an ideology can turn a loving child into a killer and friends into enemies, and "justice" pursued as revenge only hands the killing to the next generation; the story mourns the ordinary people crushed between nations and asks the reader to remember.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | In-group and out-group: race science and "blood" (the Hitler ancestry secret, Kaufmann's half-Japanese status) decide who belongs. Fathers shape sons (Wolfgang drives Kaufmann into the Hitler Youth; Col. Honda kills his own son), and revenge for kin drives the final duel. |
| Pragmatic | The documents are power that no one manages to use: they pass through many hands and are found too late (Complete Review calls the lack of urgency odd). The story's practical lesson is about indoctrination: what a regime teaches children decides what they will do as adults. |
| Religious | Religion appears as identity rather than faith: Jewish families in Kobe and Germany, Kaufmann's later Muslim wife, Japanese wartime militarism. No God or prayer resolves anything. Faith readers find a strong witness against racial hatred and the idolatry of nation, and no forgiveness at the end: the cycle of vengeance is broken only by a witness who writes it down. |
| Socio-cultural | Japan's own alliance with Nazi Germany and its secret police are in view; the Kobe Jewish community and refugees through Shanghai; Richard Sorge's spy ring. A scholarly chapter reads it as a critique of exclusion based on fixed ideas of "Japaneseness" (Taylor and Francis abstract). The ending carries the cycle into the Arab-Israeli conflict. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — almost every main character is killed, maimed or broken, and the final duel repeats the hatred of the war, but Toge survives to tell the story; reviewers find it bleak yet leaving "a hope that human beings can change" (Experiments in Manga, Part 2).
Hope source: K — the story's way through is knowing and telling the truth: Toge, deaf and aged, resolves to write the history of the three Adolfs so that readers see where hatred and revenge lead; finds little hope (N) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every tradition welcomes the story's condemnation of racial hatred and war; the Bahá'í and secular humanist frames see their core teaching as the story's thesis. The splits are over three things. First, the hero's rape of Rosa is followed by her suicide but never judged by Toge or the narrative, which lowers most conservative Christian and Latter-day Saint readings to B. Second, Jewish frames welcome the remembrance of persecution but weigh the Hitler-ancestry premise, one stereotyped Jewish character and the coda that pairs an Israeli Jew with a former SS man as fellow avengers. Third, conservative readers note that communist spies are allies while patriotism appears mostly as harm; pacifist and Buddhist readings see the same coda as the story's strongest lesson.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Almost no one wins. Those who serve hatred lose everything (Kaufmann finds the documents only after Hitler is dead and dies in a revenge duel); those who resist suffer heavily but keep their conscience (Toge, Ms. Ogi, Yoshio Honda), and the persecuted friend becomes a killer himself.

## Creator and publisher context

- Osamu Tezuka (1928-1989), born in Toyonaka, Osaka; drafted to work in a war factory in 1944 while in high school; studied medicine at Osaka and earned an M.D. in 1961, though he did not practice; known as the "God of Manga" for Astro Boy, Kimba, Black Jack, Phoenix and Buddha (Wikipedia, Osamu Tezuka).
- Wikipedia notes his gekiga works from 1967 on featured dark, immoral protagonists, violence and erotic scenes, and that he became somewhat milder in the 1980s with Message to Adolf among his later works.
- On-record intent: Tezuka in English says he built the premise on a theory that Hitler had partial Jewish ancestry and included Richard Sorge; it says the work is not entirely bleak and suggests faith in humanity can be rewarded. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How does a child who loves his Jewish best friend grow up to shoot that friend's father? Message to Adolf follows three men named Adolf to ask how hatred is taught, how it spreads through law and loyalty, and whether anyone can step out of it once it has a hold on a family or a nation.

**The answer the story gives.** Hatred is learned, and it is passed on. Kaufmann is not born a Nazi: his father pushes him into the Hitler Youth, his school makes him kill as a test of loyalty (Part 1), and by 1944 he runs a death march (TV Tropes). The story does not stop at Nazism. Japan's own military police torture Toge and Ms. Ogi, and in the coda the persecuted friend, Kamil, kills Kaufmann's family and then Kaufmann himself. The final answer is not forgiveness but witness: Toge resolves to write the book that tells all of it, so readers can see what "justice" turned into (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Kaufmann is the argument that ordinary love can be overwritten by ideology and fear of not belonging; his half-Japanese status makes him try harder to prove himself. Kamil is the argument that the victim is not immune; he begins as the boy who considers himself Japanese and ends as an avenger. Toge is the stubborn truth-teller, and also a man who commits rape against the woman who betrayed his brother, which the story shows but never judges; readers have to decide what to do with that. Col. Honda is duty and honor taken to the point of killing his own son. Ms. Ogi is conscience: a teacher punished for anti-war poems who still protects the documents.

**Symbols and motifs.** The documents: a secret about "blood" that everyone wants and no one can use, found only when it no longer matters. The name Adolf, shared by a dictator and two boys. The graveside in Israel, where the story begins and ends. Tezuka's cartoon style for most of the story, broken by full realism in its worst moments (Complete Review).

**Traditions it echoes.** Holocaust literature and wartime spy thrillers; the Japanese anti-war tradition of postwar manga; the tragic pattern of friends turned enemies. Many readers will hear the Dhammapada's "hatred never ceases by hatred," Paul's "vengeance is mine," or the Bahá'í teaching of the oneness of humanity. The cycle of revenge across generations echoes Greek tragedy.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarianism and nationalism are critiqued from both the German and Japanese sides, and in the coda from both sides of the Arab-Israeli conflict. Pacifism is affirmed by showing what war does rather than by a pacifist hero. Secular humanism carries the ending: truth and memory, not faith, are the hope. Jewish life is shown with sympathy and suffering, though with one stereotype and a contested plot premise about Hitler's ancestry. Bushido-style honor appears only as tragedy.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 (ch. 1-17) is a thriller in Berlin and Kobe: Isao's murder, Toge's flight, the boys' friendship, Kaufmann's schooling and first killing. Part 2 (ch. 18-36) brings the war years, the Sorge ring, Kaufmann's SS career, his return to Japan, the bombing of Kobe and the end of the war, then jumps to Lebanon and Israel (Experiments in Manga; Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. What did Kaufmann's school teach him, and how did it get him to act against his own friend?
2. Toge does something terrible to Rosa. Why do you think the story never makes him face it? Should it have?
3. Col. Honda kills his son for "treason." Was Yoshio a traitor or a hero?
4. Kamil was a victim of hatred. How does he end up doing what was done to him?
5. Why does Toge decide that writing a book is the right response? What would you want readers to learn from it?
