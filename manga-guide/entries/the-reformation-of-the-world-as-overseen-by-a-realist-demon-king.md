**Premise.** A man from our world is reborn as Astaroth (Ashta in some translations), the 72nd and youngest of 72 demon kings, sent by a goddess to unite a chaotic world under his rule (Yen Press). Weakest of the demon kings, he wins by strategy: he attacks heroes while they are still weak, floods an enemy country with counterfeit money to wreck its economy, and answers werewolves with cannons (MangaUpdates blurb). He summons allies with a game-like stat screen (ANN): his maid and adviser Eve, the Shinsengumi swordsman Hijikata Toshizou, and Jeanne d'Arc (Joan of Arc) (Yen Press, v3). After defeating the demon king Sabnac (v1) he rejects slavery and treats all subjects as equals, including Sabnac's former followers, and plans a city where humans and demons live together in peace (Yen Press, v2). He recruits dwarves against a necromancer (v3), defeats the demon kings Eligos (v4) and Decarabia (v5-6), summons "one of the greatest strategists of all time" to run a captured castle (v6), faces Zagan and a hero who is the son of Genghis Khan (v7), and on a trip for coffee beans meets a half-elf girl asking him to save her father, the samurai Sakamoto Ryouma (v8) (Yen Press blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you were the weakest player in a brutal game, would you play fair? Astaroth is the 72nd of 72 demon kings, and the series asks what a clear-eyed person from our world would do with power and no illusions.

**The answer the story gives.** Win by thinking, then rule well. He strikes before enemies grow, attacks economies instead of armies, and uses the weapon that works; once he wins, he ends slavery and treats every subject as an equal (Yen Press v2). The story rewards both halves.

**Characters as arguments.** Astaroth argues for realism. Eve argues for loyal counsel. Hijikata and Jeanne d'Arc bring martial codes into a story that does not share them; how the story uses them is not confirmed in sources opened. Rival demon kings argue for slavery, greed and brute force, and lose. The heroes argue for the old story, and are beaten before it can start.

**Symbols and motifs.** The number 72 (the demon kings echo the 72 spirits of the Lesser Key of Solomon, from which names like Astaroth, Sabnac, Eligos, Decarabia and Zagan come; from general knowledge); stat screens for summons; the planned city of humans and demons.

**Traditions it echoes.** Machiavelli's prince, Sun Tzu and Kautilya's Arthashastra (realist statecraft; my own framing); the isekai kingdom-builder (How a Realist Hero Rebuilt the Kingdom is a frequent comparison; from general knowledge; to verify); game-style summoning.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism and utilitarian reasoning are celebrated; authority is benevolent; tradition is outmaneuvered. Religious readers meet demons as heroes, a goddess as a quest-giver and a Catholic saint as a summon; secular readers meet a ruler who never asks for consent.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-12): the first war and the anti-slavery reforms. Volumes 3-4 (ch. 13-24): new allies, dwarves and the necromancer, Eligos. Volumes 5-7 (ch. 25-41): Decarabia, a great strategist summoned, Zagan and Genghis Khan's son. Volume 8 onward (ch. 42-81): a quieter quest that turns into a rescue of Sakamoto Ryouma. Chapter ranges come from the MangaUpdates volume map.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Astaroth attacks heroes before they get strong. Is that smart, fair, or both?
2. He ends slavery but tricks his enemies. Does one make up for the other?
3. What is the difference between being a realist and being ruthless?
4. Joan of Arc and Hijikata fight for a demon king here. What would the real people have thought?
5. Would you rather live under Astaroth or under a king who fights fair but rules badly?
