**Premise.** Chiyo Koduka, 24, has quit her job as a video game designer, sunk into self-pity and fallen three months (about ¥210,000) behind on rent. Her landlady, Ritsuka Hara, 19, offers to knock ¥10,000 off the debt for each favor, and the first favor named is that they sleep together. Chiyo, drunk, agrees and wakes with little memory of the night (TV Tropes; Seven Seas). The later favors are games, chores and hugs, and by the end of volume 1 Chiyo learns the landlady never meant to demand sex: the request was a joke about an earlier drunken meeting, and a chore list was ready (Okazu vol. 1 comments; TV Tropes). The landlady, a lesbian who lost her family's support after coming out, moves in to make the favors easier and quietly falls for Chiyo while assuming Chiyo is straight (ANN; Okazu vol. 2; TV Tropes). A new tenant, Setouchi, and Chiyo's friend Aoi help Chiyo recognize her own feelings (v2). In volume 3 Chiyo finds work that suits her, starts talking to people again, and the two become a couple at Christmas; the epilogue has Chiyo working as an independent game developer (Okazu vol. 3; TV Tropes).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can something good grow from a bad beginning? Chiyo wakes up hungover next to her landlady with no memory of the night, three months behind on rent and out of work. The original Japanese title says it plainly: even if it was just once, I regret it. The series asks whether regret is the end of the story or the start of one.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, but not by drifting. Okazu calls volume 3 a story of Chiyo's redemption: she gets a job, talks to people again and chooses the landlady with clear eyes. The relationship becomes good only after the misunderstanding is cleared up (end of v1) and both women stop hiding (v2-3).

**Characters as arguments.** Chiyo argues for honesty learned late: she was drunk, idle and hapless, and grows up. The landlady argues for guarded love: rejected by her family for being gay, she offers favors instead of confessing (TV Tropes; Okazu vol. 2). Setouchi is the warning: she once let a love pass from doubt (v2). Aoi, Chiyo's friend, is the voice that says the obvious.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rent ledger, paid down favor by favor; the beer can; the shared apartment; Christmas as the season of the confession (v3).

**Traditions it echoes.** The romantic comedy of misunderstanding, the yuri romance's tradition of quiet inner conflict, and the older story of the prodigal who comes to herself and goes back to work. For faith families, the story's moral arc (from sloth and drink to work and faithfulness) runs alongside a relationship many traditions do not bless.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: being honest about who you love is the happy ending. Hedonism is the problem at the start, not the answer. Feminist or progressive critics' main objection is internal: ANN finds the consent and manipulation of the opening muddied.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 is the uneasy bargain and the reveal that it was a misunderstanding. Volume 2 adds a rival tenant and Chiyo's slow recognition of her feelings. Volume 3 brings work, confession and a Christmas ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Chiyo agreed to something while drunk and regretted it. What does that say about drinking and decisions?
2. The landlady never actually demanded sex. Why did she let Chiyo believe it?
3. What changed when Chiyo started working again?
4. Setouchi let a love pass because of doubt. What kept her from speaking?
5. The landlady's family rejected her. How do families in your tradition handle disagreement about who a child loves?
