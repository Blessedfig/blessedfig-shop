**Premise.** Keita Imaizumi is a high-school boy who lives alone because his family wants him to learn independence. Three gyaru (fashionable "gal") classmates, Reina Hamasaki, Ruri Kurumizaka and Yukina Sasaki, start hanging out at his apartment. In the doujinshi continuity he has sex with all three, and they move in with him (Wikipedia). The spinoff follows the four as they share a home, money troubles and school life while keeping their relationship secret from classmates and Imaizumi's family. Side characters include Imaizumi's womanizing older brother Keiichiro, Reina's hard-drinking older sister Lisa, the basketball rival Gouriki, and Ruri's self-declared "servant" Mari Tsutsumi (Wikipedia; The Outer Haven).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a home? The series asks whether a shy boy who lives alone can find belonging, and answers by filling his apartment with three, then four, then more young women who choose him.

**The answer the story gives.** A home is a place where you are wanted. Imaizumi is passive and unsure of himself; the girls lead, tease, provide money and comfort, and in return he gives kindness and attention. The Outer Haven's reviewer notes that the girls back him up when his confidence fails, telling him their opinion of him is what matters (vol. 3). Sexual availability is part of the bargain, implied rather than shown in the spinoff but assumed throughout, since the series sits inside the continuity of a pornographic doujinshi.

**Characters as arguments.** Imaizumi stands for the reward of gentleness: he never pursues, he is chosen. Reina, Ruri and Yukina stand for confident, self-directed desire, each with her own thread (Reina's modeling and family scandal, Ruri's resourcefulness, Yukina's growing romantic feelings). Lisa pushes appetite further than the group, drinking and trying to monopolize Imaizumi, and is scolded but welcomed (vol. 4). The mother stands for ordinary parental expectation; she is always deceived, and the story asks the reader to cheer the deception.

**Symbols and motifs.** The apartment itself, which must be guarded, moved (ch. 14) and kept secret. Money and the credit card plan (ch. 4-5) as the practical glue of the household. Heat and undress (the broken air conditioner, ch. 10) as the series' recurring excuse for fan service. The "lawful wife" contest (ch. 25) turns the question of marriage into a game.

**Traditions it echoes.** The harem comedy from Urusei Yatsura and Love Hina onward; the "student living alone" setup; the gyaru-romance boom of the 2010s and 2020s (from general knowledge; to verify). It also echoes the doujinshi-to-mainstream path, in which an adult work is toned down for a commercial magazine while keeping its premise.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism is affirmed without cost. Expressive individualism is affirmed: the four live as they choose, against the expectations of family and school. A modest communitarian thread runs through the shared chores, meals and sickbed care (ch. 19-20). Traditional authority appears only as something to hide from. No religious lens is present.

**Shifts across the series.** Reviewers note a drift from situational fan service toward slice-of-life and relationship story: the basketball arc (vol. 2), the photographer and eviction (vol. 3), and Yukina's deepening bond with Imaizumi (vol. 4), which the reviewer calls "surprisingly great". Chapter 35, "Second-Years", marks the cast moving up a school year (vol. 7). Later volumes were not reviewed in sources opened.
