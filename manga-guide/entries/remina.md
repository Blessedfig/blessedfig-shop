**Premise.** In a future Tokyo, Professor Oguro wins the Nobel Prize for finding a wormhole from which a planet has emerged. He names it Remina after his daughter, who was born when it appeared and is now 16 (Anime UK News). Remina becomes a reluctant celebrity, signing with a talent agency and becoming the spokeswoman for Mineishi Construction. Then the planet begins to swallow stars, then Mars, and heads for Earth; an astronomer who says he has looked into its eye goes mad. The public decides the Oguros summoned it. A masked cult hijacks the news to demand their deaths, and mobs hunt Remina as she flees with her agent Mitsumura, fan-club president Goda and Kunihiro, the Mineishi heir. Mitsumura dies shielding her; she and her father are crucified, and he dies asking her forgiveness. Kunihiro sexually assaults her in his family's bomb shelter, and his parents hand her to the mob. A homeless man, Daisuke, protects her through torture, a burning cross and a planet-wide airborne chase. The cult leader is unmasked as Goda. The planet devours the Earth; Remina, Daisuke (revealed as the Mineishis' estranged son) and a few young survivors drift through space in the detached shelter with a year of supplies (Wikipedia; Anime UK News; The Comics Journal).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a frightened crowd do when it cannot fight what threatens it? Remina's answer is grim: it finds a person to blame. The planet is a fact no one can change; the girl who shares its name is a target anyone can reach.

**The answer the story gives.** Scapegoating is useless and monstrous: killing Remina would not have stopped the planet, and the crowd that tries to do it is destroyed anyway. Decency survives only in a few people who refuse to join, and even they cannot save the world.

**Characters as arguments.** Professor Oguro argues for pride in knowledge, and his naming of the planet after his child shows how a parent's vanity can expose a child (The Comics Journal). Mitsumura argues for love that gives itself. Goda shows how adoration can sour into a wish to possess and then to destroy. Kunihiro and his parents show self-interest and the choice to believe the powerful son over the girl. Daisuke, the outcast son who never reached space, is the one who sees the injustice and acts.

**Symbols and motifs.** The planet's eye and tongue: an appetite that watches. The shared name: a girl defined by something she never chose. The cross: an instrument of torture turned on an innocent. The bomb shelter: a refuge built for the rich that becomes the last ark.

**Traditions it echoes.** Witch hunts and lynch mobs; the scapegoat ritual; the Passion (Anime UK News); Lovecraft's cosmic horror, which Ito cites as an influence; disaster films like When Worlds Collide (ICv2); Go Nagai's Devilman (The Comics Journal).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism has the last word on the cosmic scale, while the human story condemns the crowd in clear moral terms. Critical theory readings (class, celebrity, lynching imagery) are offered by reviewers, not stated by the book. Christian imagery is borrowed for horror; the only religion on stage is a murderous cult.

**Shifts across the series.** The volume moves from a bright science-fiction future and a celebrity story (opening chapters) through rising panic and the planet's approach (middle) to the hunt, crucifixions, assault and torture (second half), ending with the Earth swallowed and a few survivors adrift (final chapter). Chapter breaks not confirmed.

**Discussion questions.**
