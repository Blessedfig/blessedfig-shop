**Premise.** Ichiri "Icchi" Sanjou, a skateboarding girl in her last year of middle school, feels a strange pull whenever she sees the full moon and chafes under a strict father, Pedro, who smashes her skateboard when she asks to join a karate club (ch. 1). That night she walks into a convenience-store robbery and sees a ragged old Brazilian man knock the robber out with a capoeira kick. He is Mestre Buscapé, a master of Capoeira Angola, and he begins teaching Icchi and her friends (ch. 1-7). Icchi learns that Pedro and her mother Aruna are former members of Lança Mortal, a Brazilian capoeira mafia, who were sent to hold her hostage as a baby and instead ran away with her rather than cut off her fingers to send to her parents (fan wiki). Her birth father, Isao Sanjou, now heads Lança Mortal and has declared an "Inheritance War": in five years his fortune goes to the strongest of his three children, Aguri, Icchi and Legba, who are expected to fight, even kill, each other. The one-legged gangster B.J. holds Pedro and Aruna hostage and forces Icchi, now 15 (ch. 19), to fight a series of opponents to make her strong enough to win, so that he can take revenge on Isao. Her fights draw her into Japan's underworld: the Ogun Union gang, an underground "Brawlers Festival" with betting (ch. 66-81), and a final war with gang leader Ogun Tetsuma (fan wiki; TV Tropes). Icchi tells Isao she wants nothing from him and only wants to be forgotten (fan wiki).

## Life message

**Real freedom comes from mastering yourself and choosing the people you belong to, not from power, money or bloodline; joy can be a way of fighting back.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | A bloodline contest: Isao wants children only to pass on power to the strongest (fan wiki), a crude picture of selection that the story treats as monstrous. Against it stands adoptive care: Pedro and Aruna risk death for a child not their own. |
| Pragmatic | Capoeira's trickery (malandragem, ch. 29) and adaptability beat brute strength; Icchi wins by learning in the middle of a fight (ch. 75-76). Hostages and threats show how power works in the underworld. |

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame honors Icchi's loyalty to the parents who rescued and raised her and her refusal of a father who would set his children against each other for money. The divide is the fighting itself: traditions with nonresistance or strict media standards (Anabaptist, Seventh-day Adventist, Jehovah's Witness) read a story that celebrates combat as joy as the core concern, while Sikh, Hindu and many secular frames accept fighting to protect others. Afro-Brazilian history splits readers differently: Historically Black Protestant, Mainline and progressive frames welcome its remembrance of slavery and resistance, while Pentecostal and Jehovah's Witness families may be wary of deity names and legends of spiritual power.

## Creator and publisher context

- Toshio Sako, born December 7, 1973 in Shimonoseki; moved to Tokyo after high school and worked as a barber; decided at about 27 to draw manga and trained as an assistant to Takeshi Hinata; debuted in 2005 with Usogui, serialized 2006-2018 in Weekly Young Jump; Batuque 2018-2024; Genikasuri, about a boxer, from 2025 (Japanese Wikipedia). He is described as a professional boxer and a capoeirista (Japanese Wikipedia).
- Research and practice: he travelled to Brazil to learn about capoeira, still trains, and posts capoeira videos; Young Jump published a video of him teaching capoeira (Critical Hits; fan wiki links). Mestre Buscapé is modeled on the real Mestre Cobra Mansa (fan wiki; to verify).
- Public work-related activity: in 2024 a fund in his name was set up to pay for lessons and activities for children living at a children's home (Shin Nihon Gakuen), funded by art exhibition proceeds (Japanese Wikipedia).
- No interview on the series' themes, religion or politics was opened (search cap). **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Young Jump, seinen). English: Shueisha's Manga Million (digital, 2026). The move online after vol. 8 was announced as giving Sako more freedom with the story (Critical Hits; ANN news headline).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to be free? Icchi has a loving but suffocating home, a birth father who wants her only as a contender, and a gangster who owns her time by holding her parents hostage. Capoeira, an art born among enslaved people, becomes her answer.

**The answer the story gives.** Freedom is something you practice: in the ginga, in trickery (malandragem, ch. 29), in joy under pressure. Icchi learns in the middle of fights (ch. 75-76), apologizes to the people she beats, and tells her father she wants nothing from him (fan wiki). Chapter titles such as "Escrava" (Slave, ch. 6) and "Liberdade" (Freedom, ch. 18) state the theme.

**Characters as arguments.** Icchi argues for joy and loyalty. Mestre Buscapé argues for play and the long tradition. Pedro and Aruna show that people with a criminal past can choose mercy. B.J. is the argument that money and revenge can fill a loveless life, and the story shows its emptiness. Isao is the argument that power belongs to the strongest, which the heroine refuses. Tetsuma, abused with drugs as a child and hating drugs as an adult, shows that the abused do not all become the same.

**Symbols and motifs.** The full moon Icchi remembers from the night she was abandoned (ch. 1, 12); the roda (circle) as a stage and a community; the berimbau; Icchi's mask reading "sorry"; Besouro, the legendary capoeirista Isao is said to embody (ch. 17).

**Traditions it echoes.** The seinen fighting manga (Baki, Usogui), the mafia-succession story, and the real history and legends of capoeira (Besouro, Mestre Bimba, Mestre Pastinha, ch. 23-24). For readers of Exodus, the image of an art of the enslaved used to stay free will resonate. My own framing.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and community are affirmed together: Icchi finds herself inside a roda and a family. Might makes right is the villain's creed. Tradition is honored through teachers and history. Spiritual elements are legend and color, not doctrine.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-18): the mestre, the training, the truth about her family. Vols. 3-4 (ch. 19-40): B.J.'s forced fights and capoeira history. Vols. 5-6 (ch. 41-62): B.J.'s past, the batizado, the Ogun Union. Vols. 7-8 (ch. 63-84): the Brawlers Festival. Vols. 9-18 (ch. 85-157): the online run, the war with Tetsuma and the Inheritance War; not confirmed in detail.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Pedro broke Icchi's skateboard. Later we learn why he was so strict. Does that change how you see him?
2. Capoeira began among enslaved Africans in Brazil. Why might people under bondage create a fighting art that looks like a dance?
3. Icchi says sorry to the people she beats. Why?
4. Isao says only the strongest child deserves his fortune. What is wrong with that idea?
5. Who is Icchi's real family?
