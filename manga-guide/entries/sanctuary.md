**Premise.** In 1975, two Japanese boys living in Cambodia, Akira Hojo and Chiaki Asami, survive the Khmer Rouge takeover: their parents are killed and they live through a labor camp, burying the executed and eating rats to stay alive (Thompson, ANN; TV Tropes). Back in Japan they find a country they see as rich, apathetic and run by corrupt old men, and they vow to remake it as their "sanctuary" (MangaUpdates). A game of rock-paper-scissors decides their roles: Hojo enters the yakuza and Asami politics. In their late twenties, Hojo becomes boss of the Sagara Alliance and moves to unite the yakuza and turn it into a legitimate enterprise, secretly funding Asami, while Asami forms a group of young Diet members to break the hold of aging Liberal Democratic Party power brokers, led by the secretary-general Norimoto Isaoka, a former Sugamo prisoner (Wikipedia). Deputy police chief Kyoko Ishihara, 27, sets out to expose their alliance and falls in love with Hojo. Along the way Hojo is shot by the Hong Kong boss Wong and lies in a coma (Viz Part Three, 1994-1995), Asami's group proposes amending Article 9 of the constitution to force a public debate and a referendum (SKJAM; TV Tropes), and they fight the Kobe yakuza, Hong Kong triads and Russian gangsters. By the end, Hojo has united the major syndicates and runs for the Diet, Asami becomes the youngest prime minister, and the two return to Cambodia, where Asami, ill with an incurable cough, is expected to die (Wikipedia says he dies; one MyAnimeList reviewer reads the ending as strongly implying it).

## Life message

**Two loyal friends who refuse to accept a corrupt, stagnant order can remake their country, and the story treats crime, violence and manipulation as acceptable tools for that end.**

| Pragmatic | Ends justify means: blackmail, staged attacks (Asami is stabbed in a staged attempt for sympathy, TV Tropes), bribery and murder are tools. Hojo's line that the yakuza can do in a day what takes others thirty years sums up the method (TV Tropes). |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot in sources opened. Cambodia, the killing fields and the closing return there give the story its moral weight; TV Tropes files the leads as survivors hardened into calm. | Socio-cultural | A political fantasy about early-1990s Japan: gerontocracy, LDP dominance, Article 9, foreign labor and dependence on the United States (Thompson). Thompson calls it a power fantasy of "superheroes in suits"; Spurgeon calls it melodrama, not a serious political statement. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the two friends win everything they set out to win, but many followers die along the way and the story closes on Asami's illness and coming death in Cambodia.
Hope source: F — the way through is the bond between two sworn friends and their loyal followers; Spurgeon found the final scene moving. Effort and ambition (E) are the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Sho Fumimura is the pen name Buronson (born 1947 in Saku, Nagano) used at Ikegami's request for Sanctuary and Odyssey; he joined the Japan Air Self-Defense Force after junior high school, then became a manga writer in 1972, and co-created Fist of the North Star (Wikipedia, Buronson). He received a special Saito Takao Award in 2021 and runs a free manga school in his hometown (Wikipedia).
- Ryoichi Ikegami began in rental comics at 17, assisted Shigeru Mizuki, and drew Crying Freeman and Mai, the Psychic Girl; he became a professor at Osaka University of Arts in 2005 (Wikipedia, Ikegami).
- The pair later made Strain, Heat (2002 Shogakukan Manga Award) and Begin (2016-2020), which Wikipedia lists among Sanctuary's follow-ups.
- No interviews on the series' politics or intent were found in sources opened. Thompson links its rearmament idea to Shintaro Ishihara's 1989 The Japan That Can Say No as a patriotic talking point of the era. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Big Comic Superior, seinen). Viz's English edition (1990s) was labeled Mature Readers and is out of print.
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a corrupt, sleepy democracy be saved by two men willing to do anything? Hojo and Asami saw what happens when a society collapses into cruelty, in Cambodia in 1975. Back in Japan they find comfort, apathy and old men who will not let go. Their question is whether Japan can wake up before it rots.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if the right men take power by any means. The series rewards the plan: Hojo unites the yakuza, Asami becomes prime minister, and enemies are won over or destroyed. The only cost the story dwells on is personal: Asami's illness and the return to Cambodia, where the friendship began.

**Characters as arguments.** Hojo argues that the underworld can do in a day what the law takes thirty years to do. Asami argues for a revolution of the young inside the system. Isaoka, the strongest opponent, argues for the old way of money and seniority, and is the character readers find most real. Tokai is loyalty with no conscience, a man who will die for his boss and harms women without a thought; TV Tropes notes the story later deconstructs him. Kyoko Ishihara stands for the law and gives it up for love.

**Symbols and motifs.** The sanctuary itself: a safe place the boys never had. Rock-paper-scissors: a child's game that decides two destinies. Cambodia, at the start and the end. The tree and the sunset of the last scene, which Spurgeon found moving.

**Traditions it echoes.** The yakuza film, the political thriller and the Japanese "men's drama" of loyalty and tears. Its politics echo a strand of early-1990s Japanese nationalism (Thompson). Its view of the strong man who saves the nation by breaking its laws has a long history in fiction about strongmen and reformers.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarian and pragmatic reasoning win. Patriotism is affirmed and the peace clause is treated as an obstacle. The honor code of the yakuza is celebrated in Hojo's men. Liberal democracy is both the goal and the target: the story wants more participation but shows its institutions as corrupt and its laws as things to get around.

**Shifts across the series.** The early parts set up the alliance, Hojo's rise to don and Asami's first campaign. The middle (Viz Part Three, 1994-1995) has Hojo shot by Wong and Asami's group moving against Isaoka over the Future Land scandal and Article 9. Later arcs bring Hong Kong, Okinawa and Russian gangs and Hojo's attempt to educate and legitimize the yakuza, which rogue factions resist. The final volume brings both men to the top and back to Cambodia.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hojo and Asami say they want a better Japan. Is a goal still good if you reach it by crime?
2. Why do you think surviving the killing fields made them this way? Could it have made them different?
3. Tokai is loyal to Hojo but hurts women and is never punished. What does that say about the story's values?
4. Isaoka is the villain, but many readers find him the most believable character. Why?
5. What do you owe your country, and what should you never do for it?
