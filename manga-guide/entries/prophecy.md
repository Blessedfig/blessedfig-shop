**Premise.** Tokyo's police have set up a new Cyber Crime division, led by Erika Yoshino, a 26-year-old lieutenant known for being tough and outspoken. A man with a newspaper over his head ("Paperboy"; in Japanese, Shinbunshi) begins posting videos from internet cafés announcing punishments of people who have caused online outrage, and then carrying them out: a food company that made light of a food-poisoning outbreak is set on fire; a part-timer who bragged online about frying cockroaches is abducted and made to eat them; a student who mocked the victim of a sex crime is abducted and assaulted as punishment; a recruiter who mocked job applicants is beaten in the face with a plastic bat on video (vol. 1; Japanese Wikipedia; Comics Worth Reading; ANN). Public support grows; an internet-café clerk helps Paperboy escape; a foreign environmental group whose leader called the tsunami a punishment is attacked online; and a Diet member pushing a bill to restrict minors' internet use and anonymous forums is threatened with death (vol. 2). Paperboy is four men, former temp and day laborers: Gates, Kansai, Metabo and Nobita. Their shared past with a fifth friend, Hyoro, a Filipino of Japanese descent who sold a kidney to come to Japan to find his father and died of illness at a labor camp, drives the final volume, a failed attack, a last live broadcast and a sacrifice (vol. 3; Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**When work and society discard the people at the bottom, they may band together to strike back, but online mob justice is fickle and cruel; the deepest loyalty is a friend who keeps a promise to the dead and takes all the blame.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who punishes the people the law does not reach, and what happens when an anonymous crowd cheers? Paperboy punishes people for what they said online, and the public increasingly backs him. Silverman (ANN) calls it the vigilante dilemma set in the present day.

**The answer the story gives.** The crowd is not to be trusted: it turns on its own (the junior-high pirate, vol. 1), wants blood, and is never satisfied (Comics Worth Reading, vol. 3). The vigilantes' cruelty is shown plainly. But the men's reasons are real: they were used and discarded, and their friend died for lack of care. The final answer is personal: a promise kept to the dead, and one man taking the blame for the others.

**Characters as arguments.** Gates argues that the overlooked must make themselves seen, and that a promise to a friend outranks his own life. Yoshino argues for the law, and the reader is not asked to like her (ANN), but she alone sees the truth and honors it. Nobita, who tries to tip off the police and is punished, is the group's conscience (Sequential Ink praises his body language). The politician argues for control of speech while faking support. The net-café clerk, who helps Paperboy because he agrees with one act, shows how sympathy spreads.

**Symbols and motifs.** The newspaper mask: news turned into a weapon and a disguise. The "prophecy" video: crime announced as spectacle. The one-time password token Hyoro carried as a fortune-telling toy, which becomes the key to their crimes (Japanese Wikipedia). Bones carried home.

**Traditions it echoes.** Vigilante fiction from Robin Hood to Death Note and Anonymous-era hacktivism; Japanese proletarian fiction about day laborers; Tsutsui's own concern with censorship (Yūgai Toshi).

**How each lens is portrayed.** The labor critique is affirmed; utilitarian vigilantism is explored and found wanting; duty (to a promise, to the law) is the story's strongest value, held by both hero and detective. Free speech is defended against regulation, but the online crowd that free speech produces is the book's darkest picture.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the punishments and the investigation's start; the most social commentary (Comics Worth Reading). Vol. 2: the environmental group, the net-café clerk, the threat to a lawmaker; the group begins to crack. Vol. 3: the tip-off, the failed attack, the final broadcast and the reveal of Hyoro's story; Sequential Ink and Comics Worth Reading note the turn from social issues to personal loyalty.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Paperboy punishes people for things they said online. Is that ever justice?
2. The online crowd cheers Paperboy, then wants more. Why are crowds like that?
3. What did society owe Hyoro, and who failed him?
4. Gates dies so the others can go free. Is that loyalty, or a loss that did not need to happen?
5. Yoshino keeps the law even when the public is against her. Is she right?
