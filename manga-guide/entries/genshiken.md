**Premise.** Kanji Sasahara, a shy freshman at Shiiou University, has always loved anime, manga and games but been ashamed of it. On club day he joins Genshiken, a scruffy club of otaku: the opinionated Madarame, the cosplay-loving Ohno, the model builder Tanaka and the handsome, unbothered gamer Kousaka. Kousaka's girlfriend, Saki Kasukabe, a fashionable non-otaku, spends the first volumes trying to drag him out of the club and ends up its reluctant friend and defender (Wikipedia). Over four years Sasahara learns to buy his first erotic doujinshi without shame (vol. 1), becomes club president and leads the club in making its own fan comic for Comifes (vols. 4-5). In vol. 4 Chika Ogiue joins, loudly declaring she hates otaku while secretly drawing boys'-love comics; her shame comes from a middle-school incident with a boyfriend (told in vol. 8). The series ends with Sasahara and Ogiue together, Madarame unable to tell Saki how he feels, and the older members graduating (vol. 9; Ogiue Maniax).

## Life message

**Accepting yourself and what you love without shame, and finding friends who accept you, is how you grow up; growing up also means moving on.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you love something the world thinks is embarrassing, and still grow up? Sasahara has hidden his tastes all his life; Genshiken asks what happens when he stops.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if you accept yourself and find people who accept you. Sasahara learns to buy what he likes without hiding (vol. 1), leads the club (vols. 4-5) and graduates into a job connected to what he loves (vol. 9). Ogiue's harder road shows that self-acceptance can also mean forgiving yourself for real harm done.

**Characters as arguments.** Sasahara argues that shame is optional. Saki argues for the mainstream, and the story respects her even as she softens. Ogiue is shame itself, a fan who hates fans because she hates something she did. Madarame is the joy and the trap of fandom: proud, funny and unable to say what he feels. Kousaka shows unashamed enjoyment as something almost enviable.

**Symbols and motifs.** The clubroom; Comifes as a pilgrimage; the club's own fan comic; cosplay as a way of trying on a self; Ogiue's sketchbook; graduation.

**Traditions it echoes.** The college coming-of-age story, Japanese club manga and the otaku self-portrait (Otaku no Video, which IGN recommends alongside it; Wikipedia). Faith readers may hear themes of shame and grace in Ogiue's story; secular readers, self-acceptance.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins. Consumerism is loved and teased. Community is the means of growth. Hedonism is simply present: erotic material and drinking are never debated, which is the point most families will want to discuss.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-18): Sasahara and Saki enter the club; buying doujinshi; Comifes. Vols. 4-5 (ch. 19-30): Ogiue arrives; the club makes its own fan comic. Vols. 6-7 (ch. 31-43): cosplay, Madarame's graduation, new members. Vols. 8-9 (ch. 44-55): the Karuizawa trip, Ogiue's backstory, the confession and graduation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why was Sasahara ashamed of his hobbies at first? What changed?
2. Saki never becomes an otaku. What does she teach the club, and what does the club teach her?
3. Ogiue hates otaku but is one. Why might someone hate the thing they love?
4. The club treats erotic comics as a normal hobby. Do you think that is right? What might it cost?
5. Madarame never tells Saki how he feels. What would you have done?
