**Premise.** Okuto Nakamura, 16, is a shy, octopus-loving high school boy who has fallen for his classmate Aiki Hirose without ever having spoken to him. Each short chapter follows his comic attempts to get close: imagined smooth conversations, a school festival octopus-dumpling stall (ch. 2), a class play in which Hirose plays a girl (ch. 6), a recruitment attempt by the occult club (ch. 7), a cockroach mishap (ch. 9) and a class trip to an aquarium in Yokohama. Most attempts fail spectacularly. The homeroom teacher, Otogiri, turns out to be a former delinquent who stands up for his students (ch. 10). By ch. 11 Nakamura stops Hirose from being dragged along by another classmate, and the two become friends; the romance stays unspoken (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; Manga Bookshelf; Anime Feminist).

## Life message

**Small, brave steps toward someone you care about are worth the embarrassment, and a real friendship is a happy ending in itself.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you take the first step toward someone you like, when you are sure you will mess it up? Nakamura has never spoken to Hirose and is terrified that he will ruin everything before it starts.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep trying, keep it simple, and be kind. His grand plans fail, but every failure makes him a little braver, and the honest moment at the end (ch. 11) wins a real friendship. The story treats friendship as a happy ending, not a consolation.

**Characters as arguments.** Nakamura argues that sincerity counts more than smoothness. Hirose, oblivious and good-natured, shows that the other person is usually kinder than our fears. Kawamura, the artist classmate, and Oomori, Hirose's childhood friend, show a class that is friendly rather than cruel. Otogiri, the ex-delinquent teacher, shows that people can change and protect others. The delinquents are the only meanness, and it is quickly defeated.

**Symbols and motifs.** The octopus: Nakamura's pet and his oddball passion, everywhere from festival dumplings to his daydreams. The aquarium on the class trip, where the two finally talk. The school play's costume swap, a comic play on roles.

**Traditions it echoes.** 1980s-90s Japanese romantic comedy (reviewers name Ranma 1/2 and Kimagure Orange Road) and the newer BL that focuses on identity rather than sex (Anime Feminist). For religious readers it raises questions about friendship and courage, with a premise many traditions would discuss.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed lightly: Nakamura's feelings are valid and normal. Virtue is shown in practice: courage, kindness and honesty win. No lens is argued; the story simply assumes acceptance.

**Shifts across the series.** The volume is one arc: ch. 1-5 set up the crush and the class; ch. 6-10 widen to the play, the occult club and the teacher; ch. 11 brings the friendship. The sequel (separate entry) continues the story.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nakamura's plans keep going wrong. Why does the simple honest moment work when the big plans did not?
2. Hirose never guesses what Nakamura feels. Is it okay for Nakamura to keep it to himself?
3. Nakamura stands up to the delinquents even though he is scared. What makes someone brave?
4. Is becoming friends a happy ending? Why or why not?
5. How do you treat a classmate who is shy or different?
