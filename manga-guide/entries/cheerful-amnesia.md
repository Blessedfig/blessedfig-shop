**Premise.** Arisa Suzuki, in her twenties, wakes in the hospital having lost three years of memory, including everything about Mari, the quiet, older woman she lives with as a couple. When Mari tells her they are lovers, Arisa falls for her all over again on sight and sets out to relearn their life together, above all the physical side she cannot remember (vol. 1). Arisa takes a factory job, meets old friends and coworkers who know things she has forgotten, and hides the relationship from Mari's visiting mother (vol. 2). Mari proposes at a summer festival and Arisa faints; a flashback shows the amnesia began when Arisa, overexcited by an earlier proposal, went missing for three days (vol. 3; TV Tropes). They plan a wedding ceremony, practice kissing, and face Mari's mother, who objects that women should not marry women (vol. 4). At the ceremony Arisa's memories return: flashbacks show her meeting Mari at a fast-food counter as a student, the start of their relationship and moving in together (vol. 5). The last volume is newlywed life, Mari's younger sister and their delayed wedding night (vol. 6) (Yen Press; Wikipedia; The Outer Haven; Okazu).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you forgot the person you love, would you still love them? Arisa wakes with no memory of Mari and decides, instantly, that she loves her anyway. The quieter question belongs to Mari: can you keep loving someone who no longer remembers the life you built?

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, love can be chosen again. Arisa falls in love a second time; Mari stays patient and proposes (vol. 3); memory returns at the wedding (vol. 5). The comedy is that Arisa cares most about the sexual side of the life she forgot; the heart is Mari's steady love.

**Characters as arguments.** Arisa argues for joy without shame. Mari argues for patience and quiet faithfulness, and reviewers find her the most sympathetic (Outer Haven; Friedman). Mari's mother argues the traditional view and is pushed aside rather than answered. Nanami shows the cost of unspoken love (vol. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The rocks Arisa gives Mari as wishes she promises to grant (vol. 3); fast-food fries ordered as an excuse to see Mari (vol. 5); cockroaches as the unlikely start of their trust (vol. 5); Mari's welding, joining what was apart. The open mouth Okazu calls the artist's trademark.

**Traditions it echoes.** The amnesia romance (falling in love again), yuri comedy about adult couples (Okazu compares Fu-Fu), and the four-panel gag strip.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: the couple is right to marry. Hedonism is the joke and is tempered by devotion. Traditionalism appears only in the mother and loses.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 are amnesia comedy and relearning intimacy. Vol. 3 adds the proposal and the cause of the amnesia. Vol. 4 is wedding planning and the mother. Vol. 5 is memory's return and the backstory. Vol. 6 is newlywed life and the wedding night.

**Discussion questions.**
1. If you lost your memory, what would you want the people who love you to do?
2. Mari keeps loving Arisa even though Arisa has changed. What does that say about love?
3. Arisa tells Mari's coworkers about their relationship without asking. Was that okay?
4. How does the story handle Mari's mother? Does it answer her, or just overrule her?
5. In the backstory the two met when Arisa was a student and Mari an adult. How should we think about age gaps like that?
