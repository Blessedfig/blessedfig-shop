**Premise.** Hana, a college student in Tokyo, falls in love with a quiet man who turns out to be a wolf man, the last of the Japanese wolves living in human form. They have two children, Yuki ("snow") and Ame ("rain"), who switch between human and wolf shape. Soon after Ame's birth the father dies in an accident while hunting for food, and his body is taken away by city workers. Raising two shape-shifting children alone, with noise complaints and a visit from child services about missed vaccinations, Hana moves to a run-down farmhouse in the mountains. With help from a stern old neighbor, Nirasaki, she learns to farm and becomes part of the village. Wild Yuki chooses school and human life; timid Ame is drawn to the forest and an old fox who teaches him. In a storm, Yuki tells her friend Souhei the truth and Ame leaves to live in the mountains as a wolf; Hana, alone at the end, is proud of the children she raised (Wikipedia; Plugged In; Common Sense Media).

## Life message

**Loving your children means giving everything to raise them and then letting them choose who they will be, even when that means letting them go.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Mamoru Hosoda (born September 19, 1967, Kamiichi, Toyama; Kanazawa College of Art) worked at Toei Animation from 1991 to 2005 and founded Studio Chizu in 2011. - On-record intent (Wikipedia, citing Anime! ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a parent owe a child, and when does the parent have to let go? Hana's children are literally of two natures; she cannot decide for them which one to live by.

**The answer the story gives.** Give everything, then step back. Hana studies, farms, hides, moves and sacrifices, and at the end lets Ame run into the mountains and Yuki leave for school. She weeps that she has not been able to do anything for him, and the story answers that she already has (Wrong Every Time; Plugged In).

**Characters as arguments.** Hana argues for unconditional love. Yuki argues for belonging: she gives up her wild habits to fit in, then is accepted wild self and all by Souhei. Ame argues for being what you are: from fearing the villain wolf of fables (Plugged In) to embracing the forest. Nirasaki argues that hard knowledge comes from tradition and work, not books. The dead father argues, in a vision, that Hana did well.

**Symbols and motifs.** Rain and snow (the children's names); Hana's forced smile (Wrong Every Time); the restored farmhouse with gilded leaves under the grime; the snow run; the bear in the forest as the wild Hana cannot enter; the howl at the end.

**Traditions it echoes.** The animal-bride and shapeshifter folktale, Call of the Wild and Jane Eyre (Japan Times via Wikipedia), Miyazaki's nature films, and the mother's sacrifice in many faiths. Christian reviewers read it as a parable of parental love (Plugged In; Greydanus).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and environmentalism are celebrated; expressive individualism is honored through the children's choices; stoic endurance is Hana's strength. Feminist critics welcome a mother at the center but note how little the story lets her want for herself (Pixel Jack).

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-4): college romance, the births, the father's death and life in Tokyo. Volume 2 (ch. 5-9): the move, the farm, Nirasaki, Ame's near-drowning and school. Volume 3 (ch. 10-16): Souhei, the siblings' fight, the storm and the partings, with a manga-only prologue and epilogue (MangaDex map; ComicsAlliance).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hana promises to let her children choose. Was that hard for her? Why did she keep the promise?
2. Yuki hides part of herself to fit in. When is that wise, and when does it hurt?
3. What did the village give Hana that she could not give herself?
4. Ame leaves home very young. How did Hana know he was ready, or did she?
5. Talk about a time someone in our family gave something up for someone else.
