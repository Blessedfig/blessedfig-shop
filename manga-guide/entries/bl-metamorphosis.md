**Premise.** Yuki Ichinoi, 75, has lived alone since her husband died (two years before the story in Japanese Wikipedia; three in AniList) and teaches calligraphy at home. Looking for somewhere to cool off, she walks into a bookstore, buys a manga because the art is lovely, and finds it is a boys' love (BL) romance about two high-school boys; she declines the paper cover the shy clerk offers (Publishers Weekly). The clerk is Urara Sayama, 17, a high-school girl who lives with her divorced mother, loves BL and has no friends who share it (Japanese Wikipedia). Ichinoi comes back for the next volume, and the two become friends across 58 years, talking manga over coffee. Urara takes her to an event where the series' author, Komeda Yu, signs books (v1-2, PRH blurbs; Comics Beat). With Ichinoi's push, Urara starts drawing her own manga (v3-4) and sells it at Comitia, a self-publishing fair, where Ichinoi overheats in the crowd and a visitor from the publishing world stops at their table (v5, PRH blurb). Side threads follow Urara's childhood friend Tsumugu and his girlfriend Eri (MyAnimeList reviews; Manga Bookshelf) and Ichinoi's daughter, who lives abroad. By the end, Ichinoi moves away and the friendship goes on at a distance (Goodreads reader reviews of vol. 5; to verify).

## Life message

**It is never too late or too odd to love what you love, and a shared passion can turn two lonely people of very different ages into true friends who help each other grow.**

| Pragmatic | Small practical acts carry the story: buying the next volume, meeting at a café, traveling to an event, learning to draw, printing and selling a first manga (v3-5). Ichinoi's lack of embarrassment is useful; it frees Urara to act. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the friendship changes both women for good and Urara finishes and sells her first manga, but Ichinoi's age and mortality are named often and she moves away at the end, which readers describe as quiet and bittersweet (Goodreads reader reviews of vol. 5).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is anyone ever too old, or too odd, to love something new, and can a hobby be the doorway to a real friendship? Ichinoi, 75 and alone, and Urara, 17 and friendless, are both outsiders: one has outlived her circle, the other has never found hers.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, and the friendship matters more than the hobby. Ichinoi's open delight gives Urara permission to stop hiding; Urara's knowledge opens a new world to Ichinoi. By the end Urara has made and sold a manga of her own (v5), which neither would have done alone. SOLRAD calls it "an earnest and heartfelt examination of intergenerational relationships."

**Characters as arguments.** Ichinoi argues that age is no reason to stop being curious, and that there is nothing to be embarrassed about. Urara argues, at first, for hiding what you love to avoid being judged, then learns to create. Komeda Yu, the author they admire, shows that the people who make stories are ordinary and grateful for readers. Tsumugu and Eri stand for the ordinary teen world Urara feels shut out of.

**Symbols and motifs.** The engawa, the veranda between house and garden, where the two meet and talk: a space between generations and between private and shared life. The paper cover Ichinoi declines (v1): a small act of not hiding. The building her husband meant to take her to (v1, Manga Bookshelf): grief turned into a new outing. Urara's first manga: passive fandom becoming creation.

**Traditions it echoes.** The intergenerational friendship story (Princess Jellyfish is PW's comparison), the Japanese iyashikei or "healing" slice of life, and the mentor tale, with the twist that the elder is also the learner. Religious readers may hear echoes of Titus 2 or of honoring the aged; Buddhist readers, impermanence accepted with grace.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is affirmed in its gentlest form: not rebellion, just freedom to like what you like. Community is affirmed through friendship and fan gatherings. Secular humanism is implicit; no religion appears. Readers whose traditions do not approve of same-sex romance will notice that the story treats BL warmly but shows only brief, non-graphic excerpts.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1: the meeting and the first outing. Volume 2: the signing event and meeting Komeda Yu. Volumes 3-4: Urara decides to draw and works on her first manga with Ichinoi's encouragement. Volume 5: Comitia, Ichinoi's exhaustion, the publishing visitor and the parting (PRH blurbs; Goodreads).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Urara hide her hobby, and why doesn't Ichinoi?
2. What does each woman give the other that she could not get from people her own age?
3. Is there something you enjoy but keep quiet about? Why?
4. Ichinoi starts something new at 75. What would you like to learn when you are old?
5. The friendship continues after Ichinoi moves. What keeps a friendship alive at a distance?
