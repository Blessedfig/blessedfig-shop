**Premise.** Rishe Irmgard Weitzner, a duke's daughter, has her engagement to Crown Prince Dietrich broken off at a ball for the seventh time. Each time she wakes on that night at 15 and lives five more years: as a merchant, an apothecary (herbalist), a scholar, a lady's maid, a hunter and a knight. Each life ends at 20 in a war begun by Arnold Hein, crown prince (later emperor) of the Galkhein Empire; in her sixth life, as a knight disguised as a man, Arnold killed her himself (ch. 1). This time she wants an easy life, but Arnold, impressed by her swordsmanship, proposes. She accepts on conditions (no royal duties, no touching, freedom to do as she likes) and goes to Galkhein as a political hostage-bride, where she keeps working: training bullied maids, starting a nail-polish business that employs slum dwellers with the merchant Kaine Tully, curing Kaine's sister, reconciling Arnold with his younger brother Theodore, sneaking into knight training in disguise, and steering Arnold toward a treaty instead of conquest with the small kingdom of Coyolles, while turning her old teacher Michel away from selling gunpowder for war (ch. 1-31, v1-6; Wikipedia episode summaries). Later light novels open Arnold's past: a cruel emperor father, the killing of newborns he was forced to watch, the Church he will one day try to destroy, and his own mother's death (Gaffney, LN vols. 3 and 6); how far the manga has reached in this material is not confirmed.

## Life message

**Every kind of honest work makes a person more capable, and a capable, kind person can change a future that looks fixed, even the heart of an enemy; peace is built by trade, medicine, trust and patience rather than by conquest.**

Hope source: E — the way through is effort and growing stronger: the skills Rishe earned in six hard lives are what let her change the future; love between Rishe and Arnold (L) is the close second.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** No popularity poll was found in sources opened. Reviewers praise Rishe above all: Silverman (ANN) calls her a no-nonsense heroine; Gaffney ranks the couple among the best in villainess stories (LN vol. 5). Fans and story agree on Rishe. The split is over Arnold: Silverman reads his violence as a man pushed to a breaking point, "terminally awkward rather than simmering evil in his soul," while the story keeps his worst acts in reserve, so readers' sympathy runs ahead of what the manga has explained.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you had lived six lives and died the same way every time, what would you do with the seventh? Rishe has been a merchant, an apothecary, a scholar, a maid, a hunter and a knight, and every life ended in the same war. The story asks whether the future can be changed, and whether a man who will become a tyrant can be turned before he does.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, by work, patience and mercy rather than force. Rishe does not kill Arnold or flee; she marries him and changes his world one small act at a time: an antidote, a nail-polish business that hires the poor, a reconciled brother, a treaty instead of an invasion, a bomb turned into fireworks. Caitlin Moore puts the moral in one line: every kind of work has dignity.

**Characters as arguments.** Rishe argues that skill joined to kindness is the strongest power there is. Arnold argues, by his past and future acts, that cruelty can be the price of order; Kyle even respects him for it, which gives the view a fair hearing. Michel argues that knowledge is for destruction, and is turned. Theodore argues for love that hides itself and nearly dies proving it. Dietrich is vanity without substance. Diana shows that a bully can become a teacher.

**Symbols and motifs.** The seven loops; the ring in Arnold's icy blue (v6); the pocket watch Arnold gives Rishe so she will not be late, and the clockwork that becomes a reason for peace; fireworks made from gunpowder; fireflies that remind Arnold of torches on battlefields (likely ch. 28); Arnold's scarred shoulder.

**Traditions it echoes.** The time-loop story (Groundhog Day), the Japanese villainess web novel, Beauty and the Beast (a woman who sees the person inside the monster), and the court-romance of a hostage bride. For Jewish and Christian readers, Esther and Joseph (a young outsider in a foreign court who saves a people) are close parallels; for Hindu and Buddhist readers, the loops echo learning carried across lives, though the story never names karma.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism and virtue ethics are celebrated. Pacifism wins the main arc without the story rejecting the sword outright. Utilitarianism is voiced by Arnold and Kyle and set against Rishe's insistence on a better way, and duty ethics appears in her refusal of human experiments. Feminism is present in her competence but not argued. Religion stays at the edge in the manga so far.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-16) are arrival, the maids, Kaine's business and the brothers' reconciliation. Volumes 4-6 (ch. 17-31) are the knights' course in disguise, Coyolles, Michel's gunpowder and the ring, ending where the anime ends. Volumes 7-8 (ch. 32 onward) continue into new material; the novels' next arc (the Church, Arnold's childhood, poisoning) is likely there but not confirmed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rishe has learned six trades. Which one helps her most in Galkhein, and why?
2. Why doesn't Rishe hate Arnold for killing her? Would you?
3. Arnold kisses Rishe after she asked him not to touch her. How does the story treat that, and how should it?
4. Kyle respects Arnold for doing cruel things to end wars quickly. Is he right?
5. Theodore risks his life to make his brother live. What did each brother not understand about the other?
