**Premise.** Shuichi Nitori, a fifth grader who has just changed schools, secretly wants to be a girl; her new friend Yoshino Takatsuki, a tall classmate, wants to be a boy. The story follows them and their friends from elementary school (vols. 1-4) through junior high (vols. 5-12) to the end of high school (vols. 12-15). Classmate Saori pushes Shuichi to wear dresses; the two go out dressed as the other sex and befriend Yuki, an adult trans woman, and her boyfriend Shiina (vol. 2). Puberty brings periods, breasts, a changing voice and growing distress. Shuichi's sister Maho becomes a teen model and drags Shuichi along; the model Anna Suehiro first mocks Shuichi, then dates her (vol. 7). In second year of junior high Shuichi comes to school in a girl's uniform, is laughed at and stops attending for a time (vols. 8-9); a former bully, Doi, apologizes and helps her return (vol. 10). In high school Yoshino comes to live as a girl by choice and confesses her feelings for Shuichi (vol. 15). Shuichi tells Anna she wants to transition medically, Anna stays with her, and after graduation Shuichi leaves for college and keeps writing a semi-autobiographical novel called *The Boy Who's a Girl* (Wikipedia; chapter list).

## Life message

**Growing up means finding out, slowly and with friends, who you really are and having the courage to live it even when people laugh; kindness and honest friendship matter more than fitting in.**

| Evolutionary biology | Puberty is the real antagonist: Yoshino's first period (vol. 1) and breasts (vol. 5), Shuichi's pimples (vol. 7) and changing voice (vol. 11) push their bodies away from how they see themselves. First crushes, jealousy and rivalry (Saori and Yoshino, vol. 4) drive the friend group apart and back together. |
| Pragmatic | Small steps work better than leaps: a haircut, a wig bought with a grandmother's gift (vol. 2), a tie in place of a ribbon, a chest binder (vols. 7-8). Shuichi's all-at-once attempt to come to school in a girl's uniform (vol. 8) ends with her mother taking her home and weeks of missed school (vol. 9). Practical friends (Doi, Makoto) help her back. |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot, but Saori starts attending a Christian church (vol. 4), where she meets Fumiya; Shuichi shelters in the church while skipping school (vol. 9). Wikipedia says Saori converts partly to seek forgiveness for how she has treated others. The Japanese title plays on the parable of the prodigal son. Faith readers weigh the story's acceptance of gender transition against its gentleness, family love and condemnation of bullying. |
| Socio-cultural | A Japanese school world of sex-specific uniforms, pronouns and honorifics (Yoshino is called "Takatsuki-kun"). SOLRAD sets it against Japan's 2003 law on legal gender change. Adult mentors (Yuki, who runs a gay bar) show a life that worked out, at a cost: Yuki is estranged from her parents (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). |

Outlook: Hopeful — the children are hurt by bullying and by their own changing bodies, but they are loved, they apologize and forgive, and the series ends with Shuichi heading to college, still with Anna and still writing, and Yoshino at peace with herself (Wikipedia; Lost in Anime).
Hope source: S — the story's way through is being true to yourself: Shuichi keeps choosing to live as she is, step by step, and the ending leaves her future open but hers (Lost in Anime); friendship is the close second (F).

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the story's kindness, its condemnation of bullying and its reconciliations. The divide is the message about gender: progressive Christian, Reform and Conservative Jewish, Unitarian Universalist, secular, atheist and agnostic readers see the affirmation of two transgender children as the story's heart (A or B), while Catholic, Orthodox, Baptist, Evangelical, Pentecostal, Latter-day Saint, Muslim, Orthodox Jewish and historically Black church readers weigh it as a message their teaching on God-given sex does not share (D). The ch. 12 incident and the frank puberty scenes raise depiction concerns across nearly all frames, including progressive ones.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Kindness and honesty win in small ways; cruelty loses or changes. No one is punished for being trans, but the cost of being seen (ridicule, lost school days, estranged parents) is real.

## Creator and publisher context

- Takako Shimura, born October 23, 1973, Kanagawa Prefecture; known for manga on lesbian and transgender themes: Shikii no Jūnin (1997-2002), Wandering Son (2002-2013), Sweet Blue Flowers (2004-2013), Scenes from Awajima (2011-2024), Even Though We're Adults (2019-2023); character concepts for the anime Aldnoah.Zero (Wikipedia, Takako Shimura).
- On-record intent: she first planned a high-school girl who wants to be a boy, then switched to a boy who wants to be a girl before puberty because growing up would bring him many worries; she said the theme continues the second half of Shikii no Jūnin (August 2003 interview, via Wikipedia). In a 2026 exchange with SOLRAD she said she drew on a period of gender unease in her own adolescence, that real transgender and lesbian people she knew influenced the characters, and "I do not consider myself a pioneer or anything of the sort." Thompson notes she addresses readers' worries about drawing young characters in her author's notes (not read).
- Translator Rachel Thorn (formerly credited as Matt Thorn) compared it to Anne of Green Gables and The Rose of Versailles and called it "sweet, thought-provoking, funny, and moving" (via Wikipedia). Publisher Gary Groth licensed it because "it's not typical subject matter for comics in general" (via Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Enterbrain (Comic Beam, a seinen magazine; now part of Kadokawa). English: Fantagraphics, now its Takumigraphics imprint for East Asian comics (Comics Beat, October 30, 2025).
- No controversies involving the creator were found. Okazu reports discomfort among some reviewers with how her stories handle trauma for queer characters. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (no full interview opened).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do when the person you are inside does not match the body that is growing up around you? Shuichi and Yoshino meet in fifth grade and recognize the same secret in each other. The series asks how two children can find their way, and who will walk with them.

**The answer the story gives.** Slowly, honestly and with friends. Shimura gives no speech and no single turning point: there are haircuts, a wig, a class play, a uniform worn once and regretted, a binder, a novel. Shuichi ends the series with her future open but her own (Lost in Anime); Yoshino, who wanted to be a boy, finds she no longer does (vol. 15). Ash Brown calls it "an authentically meaningful series."

**Characters as arguments.** Shuichi argues for gentle persistence. Yoshino argues that identity can change as one grows. Saori argues for blunt honesty and shows how it wounds; her church-going is her search for forgiveness. Anna and Doi are the case for changing one's mind: both begin with contempt and end as allies. Yuki is the voice of experience, a life that worked out at a cost, and also the series' most criticized figure because of ch. 12.

**Symbols and motifs.** The exchange diary (vol. 2) and the novel (vol. 15): writing as the way to be known. Class plays with reversed roles (The Rose of Versailles, Romeo and Juliet, The Boy Who's a Girl) where the children can safely be themselves. The voice changing (vol. 11). The title's pun on the prodigal son, the child who leaves and is still loved.

**Traditions it echoes.** The quiet shōjo coming-of-age story (Rachel Thorn compares it to Anne of Green Gables and The Rose of Versailles), the Japanese school drama, and Shimura's own earlier stories in Boku wa, Onna no Ko. Readers of faith may hear the prodigal-son parable in the title and in Saori's search for forgiveness.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism carries the story, tempered by the cost of being seen. Traditional gender norms are questioned through their effect on children, not argued against. Religion appears briefly and respectfully. Progressive readers praise the trans representation while criticizing Yoshino's arc and Yuki (Anime Feminist panel; Okazu).

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (elementary school) are the gentlest and most hopeful. Volumes 5-12 (junior high, the part the anime adapts) bring dating, the uniform day and its aftermath (vols. 8-10). Volumes 12-15 (high school) are more adult: jobs, a bar, modeling, a kiss and the final night together, and Shuichi's decision about transition. English readers have only had vols. 1-8 until the new edition.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think Shuichi and Yoshino trusted each other so quickly?
2. Doi bullied Shuichi and later apologized. What changed him, and is an apology enough?
3. Shuichi went to school in a girl's uniform all at once. What might she have done differently, and what should her classmates have done?
4. Yoshino ends up choosing to live as a girl. Does that make her earlier feelings wrong?
5. How should a child stay safe while getting to know adults outside the family?
