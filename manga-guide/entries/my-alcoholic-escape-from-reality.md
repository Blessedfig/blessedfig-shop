**Premise.** Nagata Kabi, an essay-manga artist in her early thirties, has given up autobiographical comics because her earlier books made her parents cry, and is trying and failing to write fiction (Japanese Wikipedia; Sam Quixote). Drinking to escape escalates to all day, every day. After severe stomach pain she is diagnosed with acute alcoholic pancreatitis and fatty liver and is hospitalized from October 7 to 26, 2018 (Real Sound; Anime UK News). Most of the book is the hospital stay: IV lines, painkillers, bland porridge, sleepless pain, an outing for her ADHD medication, and arguments with herself about work (ANN; Ani-Gamers). After discharge she lies to doctors, drinks again, breaks her diet, and even thinks about drinking enough to be readmitted (Lesbrary; Ani-Gamers; SAPE blog). With her parents, psychologist and friends, and after reading a writer's memoir, she decides to make essay manga again, more carefully, and draws this book (Japanese Wikipedia; Ani-Gamers). The ending is hopeful but unfinished (Lesbrary).

## Life message

**Running from pain into drink breaks the body as well as the mind; recovery is slow and untidy, but honesty, the care of others and work you believe in can lead back to life.**

| Religious | No religion appears in sources opened. Faith readers will find a strong cautionary tale about drunkenness and a story about honoring parents, without any spiritual answer. |
| Socio-cultural | Drinking is normal adult life in Japan, which kept her from seeing a problem; her psychologist sums up that attitude (SAPE blog). The hospital treats the pancreas, not the addiction (SAPE blog). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — she survives, leaves the hospital and returns to her work, but depression is unresolved and she still drinks a little; Lesbrary calls it a narrative in progress with a small amount of hope.
Hope source: E — effort and growing stronger: she climbs back by deciding to make manga responsibly and look after her health; family and friends (F) are the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Nagata Kabi (born 1987) left an art university after half a year, went through an eating disorder and self-harm, won a manga prize at 25 and published one-shots in Harta under her real name before her 2016 breakthrough (Japanese Wikipedia). My Lesbian Experience with Loneliness won the 2018 Harvey Award for Best Manga (English Wikipedia).
- On record (Japanese Wikipedia, citing the book): in September 2018 she stopped essay manga, feeling she had made her parents cry, and turned to fiction; after the October 2018 hospitalization and reading writer Nonoka Sasaki's memoir, she returned to essay manga and drew this book.
- Later books continue the story: My Wandering Warrior Existence (2020), My Pancreas Broke, but My Life Got Better (2022) and others (English Wikipedia).
- Publisher: East Press in Japan; Seven Seas in English, rated Older Teen. No controversies found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when the thing you use to escape reality becomes the thing that breaks you? And can you tell your own story without hurting the people in it?

**The answer the story gives.** Escape fails; only facing reality works, slowly and imperfectly. She learns to eat what she can, take her medication, accept help, and draw her life with more care for her family (Anime UK News; Ani-Gamers).

**Characters as arguments.** Nagata is both the argument and its critic: she explains why she drinks and shows why it does not work. Her parents stand for the cost of honesty to others. Her doctor's softened rule ("take it easy") becomes a test she fails, then learns from (Ani-Gamers).

**Symbols and motifs.** Rice porridge; the IV line; non-alcoholic beer; the blank storyboard; the book itself as proof she chose to draw again.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese essay manga and the diary comic (Real Sound compares Azuma Hideo's Disappearance Diary); recovery narratives; for faith readers, the wisdom literature on drink and the call to honor parents.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism as escape is critiqued by its results; expressive individualism survives but is tempered by care for others; recovery is secular and practical.

**Shifts across the series.** Opening: drinking and diagnosis; middle: the hospital stay; late chapters: discharge, relapse and the decision to make this book; one extra chapter (AniList).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Nagata drink? What else might have helped her cope?
2. She lied to her doctors. Why do people hide problems from those trying to help?
3. Is it fair to write about your family? How did she try to protect hers?
4. The book does not end with her fully recovered. Is that more honest, or less hopeful?
5. What does your family believe about alcohol, and why?
