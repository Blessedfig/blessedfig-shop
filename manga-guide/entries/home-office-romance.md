**Premise.** Nokoru Mitsuhashi, a systems engineer at an overworking "black company" with a 75-minute commute, is sent to work from home when the COVID-19 lockdown begins. With time to sleep, he rediscovers fantasy manga and games, rearranges his apartment and turns his balcony into a garden. The garden catches the eye of his neighbor Natsu Izumi, an archaeology graduate student whose fieldwork has stopped. They talk from their balconies, share snacks and book recommendations and chat online; he misreads her relationship with her professor and fears he has misjudged her interest. The narrator tells the story as how he met his wife, so readers know from the start that they marry (ANN; Anime UK News; Walt's Comic Shop; Laura Neuzeth). Chapter titles include "Dried Burdock," "Bugs," "Thank-You Gifts," "Beyond the Border" and a bonus, "Up All Night" (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What would you do with your life if work stopped eating it? Nokoru's 75-minute commute felt fine when he took the job (Silverman, ANN); only when the pandemic sends him home does he see how little of his life was left over.

**The answer the story gives.** Rest, make things, pay attention. Nokoru sleeps, reads, games and gardens, and the garden draws his neighbor. The romance grows from small, ordinary acts: snacks, books, messages, thank-you gifts. The story's frame (a husband telling how he met his wife) tells the reader from the first page that this patience pays off.

**Characters as arguments.** Nokoru argues that a person is more than his job; he is gloomy, analytical and kind. Natsu argues for a life built around a calling: she loves her field and keeps going on long digs, and Nokoru supports her rather than resenting it (Neuzeth). The office never speaks; it is simply what he leaves behind.

**Symbols and motifs.** The balcony: a small, divided space where two strangers can meet at a safe distance. The garden as a life tended slowly. Archaeology, patient digging for what was buried. Seasonal food in the chapter titles (dried burdock, dumplings in bamboo leaves, chicken boiled in a pot).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese office-romance manga (the creator's own Sweat and Soap), the slice-of-life genre's attention to daily rituals, and pandemic stories about the "new normal" (Otaku USA). Religious readers may hear echoes of Sabbath rest and of loving the neighbor next door.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and pragmatism are gently affirmed: Nokoru chooses a life that suits him. Neighborliness and marriage complete the picture, so the individualism is not lonely. The labor critique is mild and personal.

**Shifts across the series.** One volume: chapters 1-10 set up telework and the balcony friendship; chapters 11-20 move through misunderstanding and waiting to the couple's commitment ("Beyond the Border," ch. 19); a bonus chapter, "Up All Night," closes the book.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nokoru only noticed how tired he was once he stopped commuting. What would you notice if your routine changed?
2. The romance starts with small things: snacks, books, thank-you gifts. Why do small kindnesses matter?
3. Natsu loves her work and Nokoru supports it. What does it take to support someone else's calling?
4. Is the problem in this story work itself, or one bad company?
5. The narrator tells us they marry. Does knowing the ending make the story better or worse?
