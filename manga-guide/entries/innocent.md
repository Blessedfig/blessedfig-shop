**Premise.** In 18th-century Paris, Charles-Henri Sanson is heir to the fourth generation of the Sanson family, the royal executioners known as "Monsieur de Paris". Kind and sensitive, he vomits at his first dissection and recoils from killing, which he believes is against God's will (Japanese Wikipedia; The Comics Journal). His father, Charles-Jean-Baptiste, and his grandmother, Anne-Marthe, force him into the role; his father tortures him to break his will (TV Tropes; Manga Bookshelf). Charles accepts the office to keep the family's place, but vows to be the last Sanson to spill blood (Dark Horse blurb). His first execution is his friend Jean de Chartois, framed by his own father (Japanese Wikipedia). He befriends the poor farmer Robert-François Damiens, who later attempts to kill Louis XV and is drawn and quartered with Charles in charge (vols. 4-6, Dark Horse blurb; Japanese Wikipedia). His half-sister Marie-Joseph, who has studied dissection and execution on her own since childhood, intervenes at the Damiens execution at age 6, is branded by her grandmother for it, and grows into a rebellious executioner who defies the rule that women must obey (Japanese Wikipedia; Wikipedia). The last volumes (7-9) open the "Crimson Versailles" arc: Marie Antoinette arrives in France and is drawn to Marie-Joseph, and Charles meets the young prince Louis-Auguste (Dark Horse blurb; Japanese Wikipedia). The Revolution, the guillotine and the King's execution belong to *Innocent Rouge*.

## Life message

**A good life is to keep mercy and conscience alive inside a cruel duty you cannot escape: to see the condemned as human beings, to refuse cruelty for its own sake, and to hope for a justice that no longer kills.**

Outlook: Bleak — within these nine volumes friends are tortured and executed, a child is abused, cruelty is rewarded and Charles's dream of abolishing executions stays unrealized; it comes true only far beyond this title (TV Tropes; Japanese Wikipedia).
Hope source: S — the only light the story holds out is characters keeping faith with their own conscience against an inherited role, above all Charles's vow to be the last executioner (Dark Horse blurb) and Marie-Joseph's refusal to obey (Wikipedia).

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame agrees that the story condemns torture, child abuse and aristocratic impunity and honors mercy. They split on two points. First, the death penalty: frames that now oppose it (Catholic teaching since 2018, Anabaptist, Reform Jewish, secular) read Charles's vow as the story's moral center, while frames that accept lawful capital punishment read it as a story about cruelty and abuse of the office rather than the office itself. Second, the depiction: every frame weighs the sexual abuse of a child as Significant; religious frames also weigh the eroticized torture, sex outside marriage and same-sex encounters, while secular readers focus on whether the style aestheticizes suffering.

## Creator and publisher context

- Shin'ichi Sakamoto was born July 19, 1972, in Osaka Prefecture; self-taught; debuted in 1990 at 18. He calls *The Climber* (2007-2012) his turning point, the first work where he put his "self, lifestyle, and thought process" into the work. He works fully digitally, photographing assistants in costumes he sometimes sews himself. Later work: *#DRCL midnight children* (2021-) (Wikipedia, Shin-ichi Sakamoto, citing NHK's *Urasawa Naoki no Manben Neo*, 2020).
- On *Innocent*: after visiting Paris, Versailles and the Louvre in 2011 he decided to set a manga in France; reading Masakatsu Adachi's book on Sanson made him choose the executioner. The title comes from Adachi's report that Sanson exclaimed "I am innocent" before his critics; Sakamoto said "I found the phrase as simple as it is strong." He said he does not intend a historical study, though he tries to respect the important facts, and that some relationships, such as Charles-Henri and Jean, are pure invention (French Wikipedia, citing a manga-news.com interview and BoDoï; not opened). Marie-Joseph is a real name in the family tree, but the female executioner is almost wholly fiction (Japanese Wikipedia).
- No on-record statements on his religion or politics were found. No widely reported controversy involving Sakamoto was found.
- Publishers: Shueisha (*Weekly Young Jump*, then *Grand Jump*); English: Dark Horse Manga, rating every Innocent and Innocent Rouge omnibus 18+.
- **Status: Needs more info on the creators.** (Interviews on themes beyond the title and invented relationships were not opened.)

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person stay innocent while doing the killing a society demands? Charles-Henri Sanson is born into an office he did not choose: the royal executioner, honored by the King and despised by the crowd. The title, from the words "I am innocent" that Adachi's book attributes to Sanson (French Wikipedia), poses the question directly.

**The answer the story gives.** Within these nine volumes, the answer is painful. Charles cannot refuse; his father tortures him into the role (Manga Bookshelf). What he can do is kill without cruelty and keep alive the hope that killing will end. He vows to be the last Sanson to spill blood (Dark Horse blurb). The story treats this as a moral victory inside a defeat: the hope is real, but it is not realized here.

**Characters as arguments.** Charles argues for mercy and conscience. His father and grandmother argue for duty to the family name, though the father is later shown keeping a chapel of atonement, a sign that duty and guilt lived together in him (Japanese Wikipedia). Marie-Joseph argues for freedom from God, King and the rule that women obey, and her joy in killing makes that freedom unsettling. The nobles (Chartois, de Luxe, Griffin) stand for privilege used against the weak. Jean, Damiens and Alain stand for the innocent who die under that system.

**Symbols and motifs.** The executioner's sword, given by the King and meant only for the guilty, which Charles refuses to use on an innocent lion (Japanese Wikipedia). The crowd of staring faces (Manga Bookshelf). Martyr poses (St. Sebastian, the Crucifixion) for the tortured Charles (The Comics Journal; Manga Bookshelf). The brand on Marie-Joseph's breast. Reviewers note that the story ties sex and killing together explicitly (The Comics Journal), and critics disagree on what that pairing means.

**Traditions it echoes.** Catholic martyr art; the Japanese romance with Versailles (*The Rose of Versailles*); Mishima's linking of beauty, pain and desire (The Comics Journal); Sade's *Justine* (Manga Bookshelf); abolitionist arguments from the Enlightenment that the story's ending looks toward.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Humanism and progressivism are the moral horizon: mercy, medicine, equality. Christianity is the hero's personal faith and the story's visual vocabulary, sincere but not institutional. Authoritarian monarchy and inherited tradition are critiqued as cruel. Feminism is explored through Marie-Joseph, whose rebellion the story admires and fears at once. Nihilism appears in her pleasure in killing, which the story does not endorse.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: Charles's training, torture and first execution (Jean). Vols. 4-6: Damiens, the botched and hideous quartering, Marie-Joseph's first intervention and branding, a time skip. Vols. 7-9: Charles as head of the family and briefly a playboy, Marie-Joseph as an executioner at Versailles, Marie Antoinette's arrival (Dark Horse blurbs; Japanese Wikipedia). Reviewers say the second half loses Charles's development and turns toward Marie-Joseph (AniList review). Volume ranges are approximate.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Charles believes killing is against God's will but carries out executions anyway. Is he responsible for them? What does your tradition teach about acting under orders?
2. Is a "merciful" execution possible, or is mercy in that setting a contradiction?
3. Marie-Joseph is the most loved character but enjoys killing. Why might readers prefer her to Charles?
4. The art makes suffering beautiful, which some reviewers praise and others criticize. Does beauty make cruelty easier or harder to judge?
5. The story's hope (an end to executions) is not realized within it. Can a story be hopeful if its hope comes true only centuries later?
