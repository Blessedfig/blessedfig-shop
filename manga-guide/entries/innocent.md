**Premise.** In 1753 Paris, Charles-Henri Sanson, 14, is heir to the fourth generation of the Sanson family, the royal executioners known as "Monsieur de Paris" (Shueisha vol. 2 blurb). Kind and sensitive, he vomits at his first dissection and recoils from killing, which he believes is against God's will (Japanese Wikipedia; The Comics Journal). His father, Charles-Jean-Baptiste, and his grandmother, Anne-Marthe, force him into the role; his father tortures him to break his will (TV Tropes; Manga Bookshelf). He takes his father's place on the scaffold for the first time, vowing to fight the family's "law of blood" (vol. 2 blurb); his first victim is his friend Jean de Chartois, framed by his own father (Japanese Wikipedia; fan wiki). He befriends the poor farmer Robert-François Damiens, who later attacks Louis XV and is sentenced to be drawn and quartered, the first such execution in 147 years (vols. 3-4 blurbs). His half-sister Marie-Joseph, who has studied dissection on her own since early childhood, gives him the advice that ends the botched quartering and is branded with the family crest by her grandmother for climbing the scaffold; Charles frees her and cries that "the new era belongs to us" (vol. 5 blurb). In 1762 Marie-Joseph debuts as an executioner with the beheading of an army marshal and then performs a "standing decapitation" thought impossible (vol. 6 blurb). Charles, now a formal head of the house, meets the young future Louis XVI (vol. 7), argues in a public trial at Versailles (vol. 8), and backs Madame du Barry while Marie-Joseph guards and guides the 14-year-old Marie Antoinette; the siblings fight a duel over the court's future (vol. 9 blurb). The Revolution, the guillotine and the King's execution belong to *Innocent Rouge*.

## Life message

**A good life is to keep mercy and conscience alive inside a cruel duty you cannot escape: to see the condemned as human beings, to refuse cruelty for its own sake, and to hope for a justice that no longer kills.**

| Religious | Charles believes killing "contradicts the will of God" (The Comics Journal). His tutor was a priest, Father Grisel (ch. 51, TV Tropes); his uncle Nicolas becomes a monk after the Damiens execution; his father's forbidden study with crosses (TV Tropes) is a "chapel of atonement" (Japanese Wikipedia). | Socio-cultural | A society of fixed orders where the executioner is royally appointed yet despised as "Death" (Japanese Wikipedia). Nobles are cruel or decadent (TV Tropes "Aristocrats Are Evil"); the poor starve (Damiens's son). Marie-Joseph challenges a patriarchal order that reserves the office to men (Wikipedia; Eroica Fenice). Outlook: Bleak — within these nine volumes friends are tortured and executed, children are abused and burned, the reformer Alain is killed and cruelty goes unpunished, and Charles's dream of abolishing executions is realized only far beyond this title (TV Tropes; Japanese Wikipedia; fan wiki).
Hope source: S — the only light the story holds out is characters keeping faith with their own conscience against an inherited role, above all Charles's vow to fight the family's "law of blood" (Shueisha vol. 2 blurb) and Marie-Joseph's refusal to obey (Wikipedia).

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame agrees that the story condemns torture, child abuse and aristocratic impunity and honors mercy. They split on two points. First, the death penalty: frames that now oppose it (Catholic teaching since 2018, Anabaptist, Reform Jewish, secular) read Charles's vow as the story's moral center, while frames that accept lawful capital punishment read it as a story about cruelty and abuse of the office rather than the office itself. Second, the depiction: every frame weighs the sexual abuse of children as Significant; religious frames also weigh the eroticized torture, sex outside marriage and same-sex relationships, while secular readers focus on whether the style aestheticizes suffering, a question Sakamoto answers himself by saying he draws cruelty "with an awareness of beauty" (ANN interview).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Within this title cruelty and rank mostly win, the innocent are executed, and the story's sympathy stays with mercy and with those who defy their assigned place.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** No official popularity poll was found. On AniList, Marie-Joseph has 1,116 favorites against Charles-Henri's 451 (AniList, as viewed), so readers favor the transgressive sister who enjoys executing over the merciful hero, and she becomes the protagonist of *Innocent Rouge* (Japanese Wikipedia). ## Creator and publisher context

- Shin'ichi Sakamoto was born July 19, 1972, in Osaka Prefecture; self-taught; debuted in 1990 at 18. He calls *The Climber* (2007-2012) his turning point. He works fully digitally, photographing assistants in costumes he sometimes sews himself; for *Innocent* a fashion designer handmade an 18th-century robe à la française (Wikipedia, citing NHK; ANN interview, September 2024). Later work: *#DRCL midnight children* (2021-).
- On *Innocent* (Manga-News interview, November 30, 2015, opened): after a 2011 visit to Paris, Versailles and the Louvre, reading Masakatsu Adachi's study made him choose Sanson; he wanted to highlight the man "rather than his era" and to "break the image" of the executioner by showing a sensitive one. The title comes from Sanson's reported words "I am innocent", a phrase "as simple as it is powerful". He takes liberties to express feeling but tries to respect the important facts; Jean and his relationship with Charles are "a pure invention"; the work is "a synthesis", his personal vision. He calls the sex scenes "fairly daring", an attempt "to break the codes of the era and address certain social taboos". He wants readers to remember that the world they take for granted can "tip over overnight" and to move attitudes forward on discrimination and hasty judgment. On the Revolution: "I don't have the right to make a mistake on such a period."
- On depicting cruelty (ANN interview, 2024, mainly about *#DRCL*): "What I have decided to do with the visuals is to avoid depicting grotesque scenes as they are"; "No matter how cruel the scene may be, I draw it with an awareness of beauty." On love: "It is easy to assume that love is born when there is a man and a woman, but the reality is more complicated."
- Marie-Joseph is a real name in the family tree, but the female executioner is almost wholly fiction (Japanese Wikipedia).
- No on-record statements on his own religion or party politics were found. No widely reported controversy involving Sakamoto was found.
- Publishers: Shueisha (*Weekly Young Jump*, then *Grand Jump*); English: Dark Horse Manga, rating every *Innocent* and *Innocent Rouge* omnibus 18+.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person stay innocent while doing the killing a society demands? Charles-Henri Sanson is born into an office he did not choose: the royal executioner, honored by the King and despised by the crowd. At 14 he takes his father's place on the scaffold (Shueisha vol. 2 blurb). The title, from the words "I am innocent" that Adachi's book attributes to Sanson, a phrase Sakamoto found "as simple as it is powerful" (Manga-News), poses the question directly.

**The answer the story gives.** Within these nine volumes, the answer is painful. Charles cannot refuse; his father tortures him into the role (Manga Bookshelf). What he can do is kill without cruelty and keep alive the hope that killing will end. He vows to fight the family's "law of blood" and, after freeing his branded sister, cries that "the new era belongs to us" (vols. 2 and 5 blurbs). The story treats this as a moral victory inside a defeat: the hope is real, but it is not realized here. By the last volumes he has become a formal, rule-bound head of the house who sides with order at court (vols. 7 and 9 blurbs).

**Characters as arguments.** Charles argues for mercy and conscience. His father and grandmother argue for duty to the family name, though the father keeps a chapel of atonement, a sign that duty and guilt lived together in him (Japanese Wikipedia). Marie-Joseph argues for freedom from God, King and the rule that women obey; her joy in killing, which one Italian critic reads as a response to the abuse she suffered (Eroica Fenice), makes that freedom unsettling. The nobles (Chartois, de Luxe, Griffin) stand for privilege used against the weak. Jean, Damiens and Alain stand for the innocent who die under that system.

**Symbols and motifs.** The executioner's sword, given by the King and meant only for the guilty, which Charles refuses to use on an innocent lion (Japanese Wikipedia). The crowd of staring faces (Manga Bookshelf). Martyr poses (St. Sebastian, the Crucifixion) for the tortured Charles (The Comics Journal; Manga Bookshelf). The family crest burned onto Marie-Joseph's chest. The comet of ch. 45, "A Comet Appears" (TV Tropes). Reviewers note that the story ties sex and killing together explicitly (The Comics Journal), and Sakamoto says he means the "daring" scenes to address social taboos (Manga-News); critics disagree on what that pairing achieves.

**Traditions it echoes.** Catholic martyr art; the Japanese romance with Versailles (*The Rose of Versailles*); Mishima's linking of beauty, pain and desire (The Comics Journal); Sade's *Justine* (Manga Bookshelf); abolitionist arguments from the Enlightenment that the story's ending looks toward.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Humanism and progressivism are the moral horizon: mercy, medicine, equality. Christianity is the hero's personal faith and the story's visual vocabulary, sincere but not institutional. Authoritarian monarchy and inherited tradition are critiqued as cruel. Feminism is explored through Marie-Joseph, whose rebellion the story admires and fears at once. Nihilism appears in her pleasure in killing, which the story does not endorse.

**Shifts across the series.** Shueisha's volume blurbs give the outline. Vols. 1-2 (1753): Charles at 14, his torture, his friendship with Jean and Jean's execution. Vols. 3-5 (1757): Damiens's sentence, the stalled quartering, Marie-Joseph's intervention and branding; the "Crimson Versailles" arc begins at the end of vol. 5 (Japanese Wikipedia dates the arc from vol. 7). Vol. 6 (1762): Marie-Joseph's debut with Griffin's execution and the "standing decapitation". Vols. 7-9: Charles as formal head of the house, the young Louis-Auguste, the public trial at Versailles, Marie Antoinette's arrival, the sibling duel and Alain's death. Reviewers say the second half loses Charles's development and turns toward Marie-Joseph (AniList review).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Charles believes killing is against God's will but carries out executions anyway, starting at 14. Is he responsible for them? What does your tradition teach about acting under orders?
2. Is a "merciful" execution possible, or is mercy in that setting a contradiction?
3. Marie-Joseph is the most loved character but enjoys killing. Why might readers prefer her to Charles?
4. The author says he draws even cruel scenes "with an awareness of beauty". Does beauty make cruelty easier or harder to judge?
5. The story's hope (an end to executions) is not realized within it. Can a story be hopeful if its hope comes true only centuries later?
