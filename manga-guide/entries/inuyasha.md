**Premise.** In 1496 the priestess Kikyo guards the Shikon Jewel, which can grant any wish, and falls in love with the half-demon Inuyasha; tricked into betraying each other, she pins him to a sacred tree with an arrow and dies, and the Jewel is burned with her. Five hundred years later Kagome Higurashi, a 15-year-old girl who lives at her family's Shinto shrine in Tokyo, is pulled through the shrine's Bone-Eater's Well into the Sengoku era of 1546. The Jewel comes out of her body (she is Kikyo's reincarnation), she frees Inuyasha, and she accidentally shatters the Jewel into shards that scatter across Japan. With Inuyasha (kept in check by a bead necklace that makes him fall at her command), the fox-demon child Shippo, the lecherous Buddhist monk Miroku (whose hand carries a curse, a Wind Tunnel, that will one day swallow him), the demon slayer Sango and her cat Kirara, she hunts the shards and the half-demon Naraku, a bandit who gave his body to demons out of desire for Kikyo and who engineered the original betrayal. Kikyo is raised as a clay-and-bone revenant (ch. 45-48) and walks between life and death; Sango's little brother Kohaku is killed, revived and used by Naraku (from ch. 86); Inuyasha's cold half-brother Sesshomaru slowly learns compassion. Kikyo dies for the last time in Inuyasha's arms (ch. 465, vol. 47). At the end Naraku traps himself and Kagome inside the completed Jewel, which wants her to make a selfish wish; with Inuyasha beside her she wishes the Jewel gone forever. The well closes for three years; Kagome finishes high school, returns, marries Inuyasha and trains as a village priestess, while Miroku and Sango marry and have three children (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Hatred, envy and the craving for power feed on one another without end, but loyal love, forgiveness and a found family can break the cycle when someone is willing to give up a selfish wish.**

Bonds of kin (Sango and Kohaku) drive the strongest emotions. |
| Pragmatic | Shards are power; each new weapon form of Tessaiga answers a new threat (Wikipedia). Kikyo reasons coldly about purifying the Jewel; Sesshomaru helps his brother perfect his sword when it serves the shared enemy. |
| Religious | Saturated with Japanese religion as story material: a Shinto shrine family, shrine maidens (miko) who purify, Buddhist monks with sutras and staffs, a Buddhist saint who cannot let go of life (Hakushin, Mount Hakurei arc), an underworld (meido) and reincarnation of a soul across 500 years. Beneath the Tangles reads Kikyo's mercy to Hakushin as Christlike love for an enemy. Faith readers weigh real goodness (sacrifice, forgiveness, marriage) against demons as friends, necromancy and priestly magic presented as good. |
| Socio-cultural | Takahashi wanted a darker story than her comedies and set it in the warring Sengoku period to soften its violence (Wikipedia). Readers debate its gender dynamics: But Why Tho reads Inuyasha's growth as a story of a hardened boy learning tenderness, while Miroku's groping is now seen as dated; ANN's 2025 feature defends Kikyo against fans who hated her as the rival. |

Outlook: Hopeful — Naraku is destroyed, the Jewel is wished away, Kagome returns to marry Inuyasha and the friends build families, though Kikyo, Kagura and many others die along the way.
Hope source: L — love between two people is the story's way through: Kagome's love for Inuyasha keeps her from the Jewel's selfish wish and brings her back across time; the found family of friends (F) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the story's moral arc: craving for power is evil, friends become family, enemies can repent, and the climax is a selfless wish. The divide is spiritual: Shinto, Buddhist, Hindu, Wiccan and "nothing in particular" frames find the shrine, the priestesses, the monks and reincarnation natural or even edifying (A), while Jehovah's Witness, Pentecostal, Seventh-day Adventist, Historically Black Protestant and Orthodox Jewish frames see necromancy and pagan spiritual power presented as good (C or D). Progressive readers welcome the strong women but object to Miroku's harassment played as comedy and to the stereotyped Jakotsu.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who give themselves for others (Kagome, Kikyo, Kohaku, Sango, Miroku) win or are redeemed, while those who crave the Jewel's power (Naraku and the demons who take shards) are destroyed; the cost is high, and Kikyo and Kagura die.

## Creator and publisher context

- Rumiko Takahashi, born October 10, 1957 in Niigata, studied in Kazuo Koike's manga school and assisted horror artist Kazuo Umezu before debuting in 1978; her works have over 230 million copies in circulation (Wikipedia, Rumiko Takahashi).
- On-record intent (via Wikipedia): after Ranma ½ she wanted a darker story, closer to her Mermaid Saga, and set it in the Sengoku period to portray violence more softly; she did no special research on samurai and castles; as of June 2001 she did not yet know how the Inuyasha-Kagome relationship would end, and she says she finds her endings during serialization.
- No statement by Takahashi on religion, on Miroku's lechery or on the nude scenes was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Weekly Shōnen Sunday). VIZ Media publishes it in English under its Shonen Sunday imprint, rated Teen Plus; VIZ is owned by Shogakukan and Shueisha (from general knowledge; to verify).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do people do with a power that will grant any wish? Every villain in InuYasha wants a shard, and every shard makes them crueler. The Jewel was born from a priestess's endless battle with demons (from general knowledge; to verify), and it feeds on desire.

**The answer the story gives.** Give it up. In the final volume the Jewel, revealed as aware, waits for Kagome to make a selfish wish so the struggle can go on forever; she wishes it gone (Wikipedia). The answer is personal too: Inuyasha's anger, Sesshomaru's pride and Miroku's lechery all have to be outgrown.

**Characters as arguments.** Kagome argues for kindness that does not keep score. Inuyasha argues that an outsider can be faithful. Kikyo argues for duty, and shows its cost; she also shows mercy, which Beneath the Tangles connects to love of enemies. Naraku argues for desire twisted by envy into hate; at the end he admits he still wanted Kikyo. Kohaku shows that a child used for evil can be redeemed. Sesshomaru shows that power is not enough.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Jewel and its shards; the sacred tree; the Bone-Eater's Well that joins and separates two worlds; the bead necklace; Miroku's Wind Tunnel, a curse that swallows everything; Tessaiga and Tenseiga, the swords that kill and heal; Kikyo's soul collectors.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese folklore and Hakkenden; Buddhist teaching that craving binds and letting go frees; the Shinto priestess who purifies; the Western fairy tale of the girl who goes through a door into another world. Christian readers find echoes of mercy to enemies and of the cost of greed (Mark 8:36).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Buddhist ideas and communitarianism carry the ending. Pagan and occult powers are the world's physics, good in the hands of the faithful and evil in the hands of the greedy. Might makes right is the villains' creed and is defeated.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-10: the quest begins, the companions gather, Kikyo returns (ch. 45-48) and Sango's family is destroyed (ch. 86-93). Volumes 11-30: Kohaku, Kagura, Mount Hakurei and the Band of Seven (ch. 235-276). Volumes 31-46: Naraku's heart, the Infant and new enemies. Volumes 47-56: Kikyo's death (ch. 465), the final battle, the wish and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Naraku and many demons want the Jewel. What do they think it will give them, and what does it actually do to them?
2. Why was Kagome's final wish the hardest and best choice?
3. Kikyo shows mercy to people who did not deserve it. When is mercy hard for you?
4. Miroku's groping is played as a joke. How should we think about jokes like that?
5. Kohaku did terrible things while controlled. Is he guilty? How does the story treat him?
