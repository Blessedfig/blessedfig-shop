**Premise.** One night, small worm-like creatures fall from the sky and burrow into human heads, taking over the brain and turning the host into a shape-shifting predator that eats people. One fails: it enters high-school student Shinichi Izumi through his hand while he sleeps, and Shinichi stops it at the arm. It takes over his right hand, names itself Migi ("right"), and the two must share one body (ch. 1-2). Other parasites see Shinichi as a threat. He loses his mother to a parasite that takes her body (ch. 11-12), is stabbed through the heart and saved by Migi, whose cells spread through him and make him stronger and colder (ch. 12-17). The parasites organize: a teacher-parasite, Ryoko Tamiya (later Reiko Tamura), studies humans and gives birth to a human baby (ch. 6, 27); a parasite student massacres Shinichi's school (ch. 23-24); a human mayor, Hirokawa, shelters the parasites, and a five-parasite fighter, Gotou, hunts Shinichi (ch. 28-41). Police and the army attack City Hall (ch. 52-56), and Hirokawa, revealed as human, calls humanity the true parasite of the Earth (ch. 55). Shinichi loses Migi to Gotou, recovers with an old woman in a village, and kills Gotou with Migi's help (ch. 58-62). A year later Migi goes to sleep for good, and a human serial killer, Uragami, takes Satomi hostage in the final chapter (ch. 63-64) (fan wiki; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life means holding on to human feeling (grief, tears, mercy and care for others) while facing honestly that humans are one predatory species among many, with no special right over the Earth; love and mercy, not reason alone, are what make us human.**

Outlook: Bittersweet — Shinichi survives, regains his tears and his love for Satomi, and makes peace with what humans are, but his mother, Kana, Reiko and most of his school are dead and Migi is gone into permanent sleep.
Hope source: F — what pulls Shinichi back to his humanity is a chain of bonds: Migi's friendship ("glad they became friends," ch. 58), Reiko Tamura entrusting her baby to him (ch. 48), the old woman Mitsuyo's care and advice (ch. 59-60), and Satomi, whom Migi wakes to save in the last pages (ch. 64).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hitoshi Iwaaki (born July 28, 1960) began as an assistant to Kazuo Kamimura (1984), won the Tetsuya Chiba Award (1985), and has worked mainly for Kodansha's Afternoon; Historie won the Japan Media Arts Festival grand prize (2010) and the Tezuka Osamu Cultural Prize (2012) (Wikipedia).
- **On-record statements.** In Kodansha USA's 2016 creator interview he said the idea came from childhood documentaries about food chains: "I remember wanting to write about the 'egotism of the human race over this planet,'" but "I didn't want to look down on humans. I just wanted to tell the story from an ordinary person's point of view." He said that at the time he "thought 'environmental protection' was the most important theme," and that "dealing with the unknown, or someone who you have a hard time connecting to" may matter more today. He designed parasites as efficient, simple creatures, inspired by folk-tale monsters and Tezuka (Kodansha USA interview, opened).
- Per Japanese Wikipedia (summarizing his afterwords and magazine answers): he first imagined the story as a romantic comedy about a hand that moves on its own; at the start he had no intent to carry a strong message, only to "introduce a new creature with disagreeable logic," but by the end he was linking everything to the theme. The planned ending was "nature's warning to foolish humanity," but as such themes became fashionable and stale, he gave that view to Hirokawa and changed course. He called Gotou "beautiful wildness" and "a representative of great nature"; Gotou was to escape into nature, but he rewrote it so Shinichi first spares Gotou, then kills him while apologizing. In the final-volume afterword he tells how, after finishing the last chapter, he cut off the tip of his left thumb while drunkenly playing with the real knife he had bought as a model for Uragami's; it grew back in two months, "just like Migi," and he felt again how the human body is ruled and kept alive by nature. English Wikipedia adds that he chose a high-school setting because of the scene in which Migi turns into a penis in front of Satomi. Monthly Afternoon printed his answers to reader letters (1993) (Wikipedia, characters list). Nothing found on religion or politics.
- Publisher: Kodansha (Japan); Kodansha USA (English, 16+). Earlier English editions: Tokyopop/Mixx and Del Rey (Wikipedia).
- Controversies: banned in mainland China (2015) with 37 other titles (Wikipedia). No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a human being human? The series asks this from both sides. A predator species arrives that has no feelings at first and eats people; one of them, Migi, ends up sharing a body with a teenager. The title holds the twist: "Parasyte," "parasitic beast," turns out in ch. 55 to name not the creatures but humanity, the species that feeds on the whole Earth (Japanese Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** Not reason, which the parasites have in plenty, and not strength, which Gotou has most of all. The story's answer is feeling: grief, tears, love and mercy. Shinichi loses the ability to cry after his mother's death (ch. 13) and grows cold as Migi's cells spread through him (ch. 17-18). He regains his tears holding the orphaned baby of the parasite who once threatened him (ch. 48). At the end he spares Gotou, thinking of mercy as "the final treasure of mankind," then turns back and kills him in tears (ch. 62). A year later he accepts that humans protect nature mostly for their own sake, and that parasites were "never really that different from the rest of Earth's inhabitants" (ch. 63).

**Characters as arguments.** Migi is the case for pure self-interest, and he slowly learns friendship; before he is absorbed he is glad he did not take Shinichi's brain (ch. 58). Reiko Tamura is the case for curiosity: she studies humans, has a child to test a theory, attends a lecture on altruism (ch. 36), and dies protecting the child, saying she only found more questions (ch. 48). Hirokawa, a human, is the case against humanity: humans are "poison" and parasites the cure (ch. 20); he dies calling soldiers "parasites" (ch. 55). Gotou is nature's raw strength, which Iwaaki called "beautiful wildness." Uragami, a serial killer, claims murder is human nature and that humans are the real parasites (ch. 64); Satomi answers that he is "more of a monster than the parasites." Mitsuyo, an old woman, argues for plain human care: there is no shame in running for your life, keep thinking, don't give up (ch. 60).

**Symbols and motifs.** Hands: Migi in the right hand; Shinichi must cut with his left (ch. 61). The mother's burn scar (ch. 9), and the parasite wearing her face (ch. 12, 48). The dead puppy Shinichi throws away (ch. 18) and, as he reveals in the last pages, later buried (ch. 64). Tears and their absence. Toxic waste: the thing that saves Shinichi is human pollution (ch. 62). Holes in the chest, for Shinichi, Hideo and Uragami.

**Traditions it echoes.** Body-horror science fiction (Japanese Wikipedia notes the possible influence of The Thing, 1982); Tezuka's humanism, which Iwaaki read in high school; the environmental manga of its era. Japanese Wikipedia compares its structure to Miyazaki's Nausicaa manga: both start by criticizing humanity from the side of another ecosystem, then reject that view and end with humans living on, carrying their guilt.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Naturalism is the ground of the story. Environmentalism is taken seriously but its extreme, anti-human form is given to the villain. Secular humanism wins the argument in the end. Christian readers find a sharp echo of human sinfulness, as Twwk does, but no God; traditions that hold humans apart from animals by divine image will meet a story that says otherwise.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3: the arrival, Migi, the mother's death and Shinichi's change. Volumes 4-6: Hideo's school massacre, Kana's death, Shinichi at his coldest. Volumes 7-8: Gotou, Reiko's arc and death, Uragami. Volumes 9-10: the City Hall raid, Hirokawa's speech, Migi's loss, the village and the final fight, then the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Migi says humans are closer to demons than parasites because they eat so many species (ch. 3). Is he right? What would you answer?
2. Why does Shinichi lose his tears, and why does he get them back holding Reiko's baby (ch. 48)?
3. Hirokawa says humans are the Earth's poison. Uragami says murder is human nature. How are their views alike, and how does the story answer them?
4. Shinichi first spares Gotou, then kills him (ch. 62). Which choice was right, and why?
5. In ch. 57 two high-school students sleep together on the night Shinichi fears he may die. How does your family think about that choice?
