**Premise.** Yoshifumi Nitta is a young, rising member of the Ashikawa-gumi yakuza who loves expensive vases. A metal pod falls on his head and out steps Hina, a girl of about 12-13 from a future organization that raised psychics as weapons. She threatens his vase collection until he lets her stay, and he slowly becomes her father: he cooks, enrolls her in middle school, and eventually tells his mother and sister she is his daughter (ch. 12). A second psychic, Anzu, sent to kill Hina, is stranded (ch. 6), shoplifts to survive, and is taken in by homeless men in a riverside camp who teach her to earn an honest living (ch. 7); when the camp is cleared she is adopted by the Hayashis, who run a Chinese restaurant. Hina's classmate Hitomi, who cannot say no, is pressed into bartending at Utako's bar Little Song (ch. 4, 11) and keeps being mistaken for an adult until she is running companies. A third psychic, Mao, is marooned on an island for 153 days and ends up at a Chinese martial-arts school. After a three-year time skip (ch. 47) the girls are in high school, Nitta and Hina move to a suburban house, a male psychic named Haru explains the future war that created them (ch. 76), and the cast tries to prevent a bad future that Hitomi is partly linked to. Hitomi stages a music festival where Hina, Anzu and Mao perform (vol. 19). At the end Hina and Nitta say a heartfelt goodbye before she returns to her own time, followed by a comic aftermath (TV Tropes; the exact ending is to verify) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; TV Tropes; library synopsis).

## Life message

**Family is something you choose and keep choosing: gratitude, honest work and taking responsibility for someone else make a home, even for a gangster and a girl from nowhere.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | An unrelated man provisions and protects a child who is not his, and is rewarded with attachment; Anzu survives by joining a cooperative band of homeless men who share food and labor (ch. 7). Status rivalry inside the yakuza and Hitomi's rise through the ranks play out as comedy. |
| Pragmatic | Money, food and work are the series' constant measures: Nitta teaches Hina that a good job earns a reward (ch. 2); Anzu learns the value of cans collected and yen earned (ch. 7, 13-14); Hitomi's competence keeps getting her promoted. What works is honored more than what is proper. |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot. Nitta visits his late father's grave with family (ch. 12; anime ep. 6), and the title puns on the Dolls' Festival. Faith readers will weigh the warm adoption story, care for the poor and Anzu's change of heart against a gangster hero, heavy drinking and a child serving alcohol played for laughs. |
| Socio-cultural | A deadpan satire of Japanese adult life: yakuza bureaucracy (ch. 15), salaryman careers, hostess clubs and bars, government eviction of a homeless camp (anime ep. 6). ANN's Rebecca Silverman notes that the child-labor jokes around Hitomi come closest to cruel humor, while the homeless arc is played with real warmth. |

Outlook: Hopeful — almost everyone ends up better off than they started: Hina gains a father, Anzu a family and a trade, Hitomi wealth (if not peace), and the bad future the cast fears is pushed back by friendship and comic luck.
Hope source: F — family and community: the story's way through is people taking each other in (Nitta and Hina, the homeless camp and Anzu, the Hayashis); love and gratitude matter more than powers. Honest effort (E) is a close second in Anzu's arc.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the adoption story, Anzu's gratitude and the humane picture of homeless men. The divide is the bar and the gang: traditions with firm teaching on alcohol (Muslim, Bahá'í, Adventist, Pentecostal) or on separation from worldly media (Anabaptist, Jehovah's Witness) read a child bartender and a never-repentant yakuza hero as an endorsed normal (C), while most others treat these as comic exaggeration to discuss (B). Secular frames rate it highest but share the progressive worry that child labor is played as a joke.

## Creator and publisher context

- Masao Ohtake (大武政夫) wrote and drew the series from 2010 to 2020 in Enterbrain's Fellows!/Harta; Wikipedia lists Joshikōsei Joreishi Akane! and JM as his other manga. No birth date or biography was found in sources opened.
- On-record intent (One Peace Books interview): he says swapping the power roles of a tough yakuza and a little girl is a common comic device he did not overthink; he did no research on the yakuza and drew on the shared public image from films, manga and news, because the story is a comedy; he was glad the humor crossed borders.
- No statements on religion, politics or the bar and child-labor jokes were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Enterbrain (a Kadokawa brand; Wikipedia category) in Japan; One Peace Books in English. The anime was produced by Feel.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a family: blood, law, or showing up every day? A gangster who wanted only money, women and vases is handed a child who cannot cook, clean or study, and the series watches him become a father almost against his will.

**The answer the story gives.** Family is chosen and then kept by work. Nitta learns to cook, worry and forgive (ch. 9); Hina learns, slowly, to help (ch. 5, 8). Anzu's arc says the same thing more sharply: a girl sent to kill is taught by homeless men to collect cans, say thank you and pay back what she stole (ch. 7), and that gratitude earns her a family (anime ep. 6).

**Characters as arguments.** Hina argues for appetite and ease and is never punished for it, only outgrown. Anzu argues for gratitude and effort and is the story's moral center. Hitomi is the cautionary tale of a good girl who cannot say no: her competence makes her rich and miserable. Utako is self-interest with a hidden heart. Yassan and the camp argue that dignity does not depend on money.

**Symbols and motifs.** Nitta's fragile vases (his old life, broken by Hina); salmon roe as reward and love; the empty cans Anzu collects; the bar as a stage where children act as adults; the pod and travel bead, the link to a future of children used as weapons.

**Traditions it echoes.** The reluctant-guardian comedy (Kikujiro, Paper Moon) and the found-family story; for Christian readers, the care of the fatherless and the stranger; for Buddhist and Confucian readers, gratitude and filial duty. The future war and child soldiers echo dystopian science fiction, kept mostly offstage.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism wins: no one in the story flourishes alone. Virtue is shown in Anzu and teased into Hina. Pragmatic money talk is everywhere but softened by generosity. The satire of child labor is real but light, and the story never condemns Nitta's trade.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-21 (vols. 1-4) set up the households, the camp and the bar. Ch. 22-46 deepen Anzu's adoption and Hitomi's career. After the time skip (ch. 47 on) the girls are in high school, Mao and Haru arrive, and the comedy grows broader while a future-war plot runs underneath to the finale (vol. 19). Reviewers on MyAnimeList call the ending rushed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nitta never planned to be a father. What changed him?
2. Why does Anzu pay back the shopkeepers when she could have kept the money?
3. Hitomi is good at everything and happy at nothing. Why can't she say no, and what would you tell her?
4. The homeless men give Anzu up so she can have a better home. Was that the loving choice?
5. The story makes jokes about a kid working in a bar. Where is the line between funny and harmful?
