**Premise.** Naoya Mukai, a blunt, honest high schooler, has finally won his childhood friend Saki Saki after confessing to her every month. Then classmate Nagisa Minase confesses to him. Unable to lie and unwilling to cheat in secret, he begs Saki's permission to date both, and with his parents living away for work, the three move in together (vols. 1-2). Rika Hoshizaki, a popular video blogger known as Milika, decides she wants him too; after he defends her from her strict father she falls for him and pursues him relentlessly, stalking the house and using drastic schemes (from v2). Saki's best friend Shino Kiryū, the top student, sets out to break up the arrangement but is secretly in love with Naoya; after a trip to Okinawa she becomes his third girlfriend (vols. 9-11). Naoya gives Rika five months to win him over (v7-8); he studies to first in his class to prove he can make the relationship work (v13); Saki doubts and then recommits to sharing (vols. 14-15). On December 31, the end of the deadline, Rika becomes his fourth girlfriend, and the last chapter shows all five together (v16, ch. 144) (Wikipedia; fan wiki volume synopses from Kodansha).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hiroyuki (story and art), born April 23, 1982, from Ishikawa Prefecture; known for the four-panel comedies Dōjin Work, The Comic Artist and His Assistants and Aho-Girl, all adapted to anime; his sister Kōji Megumi is also a manga artist (Wikipedia, Hiroyuki).
- The series grew from his own short "Seiseidōdō, Futamata-suru Hanashi," posted on Twitter and self-published (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent (Comic Space, 2021, Japanese): most romantic comedies stop at one heroine, and he wanted to try making several heroines happy at the same time; the central difficulty is whether Naoya can be forgiven for two-timing, so Naoya must feel guilt or readers will turn on him; he wanted the heroines' appeal to be more visual than in his earlier comedies and enjoys drawing characters' embarrassed reactions (translated paraphrase).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Shonen Magazine). Kodansha USA rates the English edition 16+ and first released it digital-only.
- No statements on religion or politics and no controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a boy love more than one girl at once and still be a good person? Hiroyuki names this as the question readers keep judging (Comic Space, 2021).

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if he is honest, fair and everyone agrees. Naoya's refusal to lie is his whole defense, and the ending gives every girl a place. The story never seriously tests the arrangement against adults or the wider world.

**Characters as arguments.** Naoya argues that honesty can make an unusual love right. Saki argues for jealousy and the first claim, and finally chooses to share. Nagisa argues for devotion and self-improvement. Shino argues for monogamy and propriety, then joins. Rika argues for possessive desire and will not take no for an answer; the story treats her pursuit as comedy and rewards it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The shared house without parents; the monthly confessions; the five-month deadline ending on New Year's Eve; Rika's alter ego Milika; the no-sex rule as the line Naoya keeps.

**Traditions it echoes.** The harem romantic comedy it parodies (from general knowledge: Love Hina, The Quintessential Quintuplets) and, for some readers, debates about polygamy and consent. Traditions that teach exclusive faithfulness will read it as a counter-message.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism win. Traditionalism, voiced by Shino and Rika's father, loses. Duty ethics appears in Naoya's rules, which the story treats as his saving virtue, while the rules about consent that bind Rika are not enforced.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-27): the setup and Rika's arrival. Vols. 4-7 (ch. 28-63): hot springs, Shino and the camping trap. Vols. 8-11 (ch. 64-99): living five together and Okinawa; Shino joins. Vols. 12-16 (ch. 100-144): exams, solo dates, Saki's crisis and Rika's deadline, ending with four girlfriends.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Naoya says being honest makes his two relationships fair. Do you agree? What else would matter?
2. Rika keeps pushing after Naoya says no. If a boy acted like that toward a girl, how would the story feel?
3. Shino thinks the arrangement is wrong, then joins it. What changed her mind?
4. What do you think parents should say about teens living together without adults?
5. What does it mean to be faithful to someone?
