**Premise.** In a near future of full-dive virtual reality, Rakuro Hizutome, a high school second-year, is a "trash game" hunter: he loves beating broken, glitch-filled games. Burned out, he buys the best-selling, flawless VR game Shangri-La Frontier and enters as Sunraku, a bird-masked, nearly naked speed fighter (ch. 1). He is cursed by the unique monster Lycagon, befriends Emul, a talking vorpal bunny, and her family in the hidden village of Rabituza, and teams with two online friends from trash games, the player killer Arthur Pencilgon and the pro gamer Oikatzo, to defeat Wethermon the Tombguard, an undead samurai guarding his wife's grave (ch. 9-45). Later arcs bring the Lycagon rematch with his shy classmate Rei Saiga, who has a crush on him and plays as Psyger-0 (ch. 76-95); an undersea city and the kraken-like Ctarnidd (ch. 101-125); a real-world esports tournament against an American champion (about ch. 126-156); and clan wars and further unique monsters (ch. 160-288; chapter titles) (Wikipedia episode and chapter lists).

## Life message

**Play is worth doing well: mastering a hard challenge for the joy of it, alongside friends and rivals, is its own reward, and an opponent's story deserves respect as much as your victory does.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Katarina (original story) began writing after reading The Irregular at Magic High School on Shosetsuka ni Naro while living overseas, built the world before the characters, and planned the major arcs and ending early (Wikipedia, Production). He named the game after Shangri-La in James Hilton's Lost Horizon, a lamasery holding the world's wisdom, because players uncover a lost civilization's knowledge. Influences he names: Is It Wrong to Try to Pick Up Girls in a Dungeon?, Deltora Quest, Final Fantasy XI, Monster Hunter, Dark Souls and Xenoblade.
- On intent: Katarina says a life-threatening stake is unnecessary for tension, likening Sunraku to real gamers who retry hard sections for fun; unlike Sword Art Online, the hero can log out freely. The banter among Sunraku, Arthur and Oikatzo was modeled on Hollywood buddy films (Wikipedia).
- Ryosuke Fuji (art), a gamer, accepted without having read the novel; he drew on the European Middle Ages and Horizon Zero Dawn and watches streamer videos to stage Sunraku's moves. His barefoot design for Sunraku was approved by Katarina (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Shonen Magazine). English: Kodansha USA, rated 16+; anime by C2C, licensed by Crunchyroll.
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why do you play? The series opens with that question (ch. 1) and returns to it when Sunraku's friends give their own answers (ch. 97-98). Rakuro Hizutome loves broken games because beating them takes skill no one else bothers to learn. Shangri-La Frontier asks what happens when that skill meets a game that is not broken at all.

**The answer the story gives.** Play is worth taking seriously. Sunraku refuses to take the easy way out of a training fight (ch. 15-16), learns that he must parry an attack the game calls unavoidable to beat Wethermon (ch. 39-42), and keeps adapting when the game makers patch his tricks (ch. 66-70). The joy is in mastery, and it is better shared: the lone trash-game hunter becomes part of a party, a rabbit family and a team.

**Characters as arguments.** Sunraku argues for joy and mastery. Arthur Pencilgon argues for cunning and the thrill of beating people, player-killing included; the story enjoys her and also makes her pay penalties (anime ep. 41). Oikatzo argues for play as a profession. Rei, shy in life and fearless in the game, argues for quiet devotion. Ashura Kai, who exploit a boss for safe experience without fighting it, are the case against gaming the system without honor (anime ep. 9).

**Symbols and motifs.** The bird mask and near-naked avatar: speed over armor. Lycagon's curse that forbids armor, then makes it decay (ch. 3, 94-95). The moon, which powers both bosses and weapons. Wethermon at his wife's grave, and Setsuna's ghost, who is only a copy left to help him rest (ch. 43-45). The rabbits of Rabituza and their "Vorpal Soul."

**Traditions it echoes.** The litRPG and VR-game story (Sword Art Online, which it deliberately departs from by keeping death temporary), the samurai tale of a loyal retainer who cannot rest, Japanese stories of restless spirits laid to rest, and the sports manga of practice and rivalry. Faith readers may hear echoes of the virtue of good recreation and of perseverance, and may also ask about time and attention.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism and virtue through practice are celebrated. Expressive individualism shows in each player's chosen style. The honor code is admired in Wethermon's story. Consumerism and transhumanism are the setting, enjoyed rather than examined. Religion is only game lore.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-45): first steps, Lycagon's curse and the Wethermon raid. Volumes 6-10 (ch. 46-95): crafting, alliances, the crystal scorpions and the Lycagon rematch. Volumes 11-13 (ch. 96-125): the friends' answers to "why do you game?" and the undersea city. Volumes 14-17 (ch. 126-165): a real-world esports tournament. Volumes 18-28 (ch. 166-288): a long raid ("Holding the Heavens Aloft," ch. 173-206), clan "Wolf Wars" (ch. 229-232), a duel arc (ch. 257-272) and a new continent (ch. 278 onward); these later arcs are known from chapter titles only.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sunraku loves games other people call trash. What do you enjoy that others don't understand?
2. Why did Sunraku refuse to die in the training match to escape it?
3. Arthur likes beating other players by trickery. Is that fair play?
4. Wethermon guarded his wife's grave for centuries. Why was defeating him a kindness?
5. How much time is the right amount for a game you love, and how do you know?
