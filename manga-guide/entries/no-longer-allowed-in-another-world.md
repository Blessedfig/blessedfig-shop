**Premise.** June 1948: a gloomy, famous novelist known only as "Sensei" (an obvious stand-in for Osamu Dazai) stands at a swollen river with his lover Sacchan, about to carry out a lovers' suicide pact, when a truck hits them both. Sensei wakes in a fantasy world, summoned as a hero by the elf priestess Annette. He has no combat stats and wants only to die, so he wanders off, sleeps in a coffin and chews sleeping pills like snacks. Along the way he picks up companions (Annette, the catgirl princess Tama, the orphan swordsman Nir and the shape-shifting familiar Melos) and learns Sacchan was summoned too. His one power, "Storyteller," is to write the life story of other summoned people: when he has heard and written it, the troubled "Otherworlder" is healed or sent back home to Earth. Seven powerful Otherworlders, named the Fallen Angels after the seven deadly sins, have seized the dead Dark Lord's lands, and Sacchan is one of them. When they finally meet (ch. 29; anime ep. 12), she refuses to die with him because he has found something better than death; later he in turn breaks free of her and leaves to pursue his own goals, "not death" (fan wiki; Wikipedia; Seven Seas).

## Life message

**Played as dark comedy: a man who says he wants only to die keeps helping others to live, and listening to each person's story heals them; slowly the story gives him reasons to go on, though it jokes about suicide throughout.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A man who resists every instinct to survive keeps surviving (his own poison kills what attacks him); women compete for his attention in a harem setup (AniList Female Harem tag). A comic inversion of the survival-and-mating isekai fantasy. |
| Pragmatic | Sensei solves problems not with force but by listening: each villain is undone when he hears their past and writes it (Wikipedia episodes 4, 7, 9, 11; Real Sound). A well-meaning hero who drives out villains without understanding the town makes things worse (the World Tree arc, ep. 8). |
| Religious | The fantasy world's Church summons heroes from Earth and its Pope pursues his own plans; the villains are "Fallen Angels" named for the deadly sins, a borrowing from Christian imagery (fan wiki). A nature spirit (the World Tree) and a fairy queen reward Sensei's kindness. Faith readers will weigh a protagonist whose death wish is the running joke against a plot that steadily moves him, and those he helps, toward life. |
| Socio-cultural | A parody of isekai power fantasies: other summoned people abuse their "gifts" to get revenge or rule (Suzuki, Kaibara), while the hero has no powers. It draws on the legend of Dazai and Japan's literary tradition of lovers' suicide (shinjū) (Wikipedia). English reviewers were sharply divided over turning a real author's suicide into a gag (ANN; Anime Feminist). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the tone is comic and the companions are loyal, and the plot moves Sensei from wanting death toward living for his writing and his friends, but his lover becomes his enemy and turns violent (ch. 85-86, fan wiki), and the ongoing final arc has not resolved his death wish.
Hope source: F — the hope comes from friends: the companions who keep pulling Sensei back, and whose stories he writes; Sacchan herself says he now has light in his eyes and has found something better than death (ch. 29; Wikipedia ep. 12). Healing others by hearing their stories is a close second (K).

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the merciful core: enemies are healed and sent home rather than killed, and a man who wants to die is gradually pulled back toward life by friends. The divide is over the jokes. Most Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint and conservative readings weigh suicide and pill-chewing played for laughs as a serious problem (C or D), and so do the progressive critics at Anime Feminist; secular humanist, agnostic, atheist, Unitarian Universalist and Satanist readings put more weight on the arc toward living and on the story's distrust of a manipulative Church (B). Families with anyone struggling with depression should read the reviews first (Anime Feminist; ANN).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who listen and show mercy win: Sensei's party heals or sends home one tormented Otherworlder after another, while those who use power to dominate (Suzuki, Kaibara) are stopped. Sensei's death wish loses ground; his love for Sacchan becomes a conflict between them rather than a pact.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hiroshi Noda (story) and Takahiro Wakamatsu (art) work as a team; their other series include Love After World Domination (Seven Seas) and Ningyohime no Gomen ne Gohan (Wikipedia). No interview about this series was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- The model for Sensei is the novelist Osamu Dazai (1909-1948), author of No Longer Human and Run, Melos!, who died with his lover Tomie Yamazaki in the Tamagawa canal in June 1948 (Wikipedia; fan wiki says Sacchan is based on Yamazaki). The anime never names Dazai (ANN, Caitlin Moore). The familiar Melos shares a name with Dazai's story (from general knowledge; to verify).
- A Real Sound column (Taniguchi Ryuichi, 2024) reads the series as saying that life and death are one's own choice and that a writer's observation and words can change others' choices.
- Publishers: Shogakukan (Yawaraka Spirits web magazine; seinen); Seven Seas Entertainment, rated Older Teen 17+.
- No controversies involving the creators were found; the controversy is about the premise itself (see How readers took it).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is life worth living? The question belongs to Dazai, whose work and death made despair famous, and the series drops him into the most wish-fulfilling genre there is. The joke is that the one man who wants nothing from a fantasy paradise keeps getting saved.

**The answer the story gives.** Slowly, yes, though never by lecture. Sensei listens to other broken people (a bullied boy turned tyrant, a coward who once abandoned a kind student, a caregiver whose life was stolen) and, by writing their stories, frees them (ep. 4, 9, 11). In helping them he acquires reasons to live, and Sacchan sees it first: he has found something better than death (ch. 29). The open question, still unresolved in the final arc, is whether he can be freed from her, and she from herself.

**Characters as arguments.** Sensei is despair that keeps choosing other people. Sacchan is love that wants to die together, and later love that wants to own; the fan wiki describes her as deeply unwell and self-sabotaging (ch. 65-88). Suzuki is victimhood turned into tyranny. Yamada is earnest justice that does not stop to understand and makes things worse (ep. 8). Annette and Tama are loyalty and simple goodness, and their jealous comedy keeps the tone light.

**Symbols and motifs.** The coffin he sleeps in and his companions drag (ep. 1-2). The red string tying him to Sacchan, later turned into her puppet thread (fan wiki). The pills, which poison those who try to drain him. Writing itself: in this world, to tell a person's story truly is to set them free.

**Traditions it echoes.** Dazai's No Longer Human and the shinjū tradition. Isekai parody in the line of works that mock power fantasies. For Christian readers, Elijah asking to die under the broom tree (1 Kings 19) and being fed and sent on; for Buddhists, craving for non-existence. The seven Fallen Angels borrow the seven deadly sins.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is the starting point and is steadily undercut. Existentialism and absurdism carry the comedy: meaning must be chosen in an absurd world. Might-makes-right is critiqued through the summoned tyrants. The fantasy Church is a power to be suspicious of, but no real religion is attacked.

**Shifts across the series.** Early chapters (the anime's 12 episodes, through ch. 29) are episodic: one troubled Otherworlder per arc, ending in Sacchan's rejection. Middle chapters bring the Church's politics, an arc in the Kingdom of Blau where Otherworlders are arrested (Epicstream summary of the manga, 2024) and Sacchan's turn to villainy (ch. 51-65). Recent chapters (85-88) turn darker and more personal, and the final arc began in October 2025.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sensei says he wants to die, but he keeps helping people live. Which do you believe, his words or his actions?
2. Why does hearing someone's whole story change how Sensei treats them?
3. Sacchan refuses the pact because Sensei now seems alive in a way he was not before. What changed him?
4. Is it ever okay to make jokes about suicide? When does it become harmful?
5. If a friend told you they wanted to die, what would you do? (Talk about who to tell and how to get help.)
