**Premise.** Inside an adult human body pictured as a city, trillions of cells work as people. A rookie red blood cell, AE3803, keeps getting lost on her oxygen deliveries; a soft-spoken but ruthless white blood cell (neutrophil) U-1146 keeps saving her from invading germs (Wikipedia). Each chapter is a real condition (pneumonia bacteria, cedar pollen allergy, influenza, a scraped knee, food poisoning, heat stroke, cancer, acne, blood loss, H. pylori, psoriasis and, in the final chapter, the novel coronavirus) told as an action comedy, with boxed explanations of the real biology (chapter list; ANN). Supporting cast: brash Killer T Cells, a commanding Helper T Cell, maid-dressed Macrophages, tiny Platelets drawn as children, a jittery Memory Cell and a sympathetic Cancer Cell who wants to stop being hunted (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Every cell has a job, and life goes on because ordinary workers do theirs faithfully and protect one another; no part is too small to matter.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | The book is biology: immune defense, inflammation, cell division gone wrong (cancer), blood loss and recovery. Its 'society' is a body organized by function, which the BMJ reviewer reads as role-bound and collective. |
| Pragmatic | Explains how fevers, allergies, wounds and infections work so readers understand their own bodies (ANN; Common Sense Media). The allergy chapter shows an immune overreaction doing harm (ch. 2), a useful lesson in trade-offs. |
| Religious | No religion. Faith readers often see the body of many members working as one (1 Corinthians 12) and the body as a gift to care for; one chapter shows a blood transfusion saving the body (vol. 4, BMJ), which matters to some families. |
| Socio-cultural | The body as a Japanese workplace: diligent workers, seniors and juniors, uniforms and duty, and no life outside the job (BMJ). Common Sense Media flags the Killer T Cells' equation of strength with aggression and their bullying of a rookie. |

Outlook: Hopeful — the body survives every crisis, from cancer to blood loss to the coronavirus, because its cells keep working together; individual cells die, and the cancer cell's grievance is heard but not answered.
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting others: immune cells and platelets risk everything to keep the body and each other alive; diligence (E) is a close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What keeps us alive, moment to moment, without our noticing? The series turns the body into a city and asks the reader to see the countless workers inside.

**The answer the story gives.** Ordinary work, done faithfully and together. A clumsy red blood cell matters because she keeps delivering oxygen; the neutrophil matters because he never stops protecting; platelets, macrophages and T cells each have a part. When a crisis comes (blood loss, ch. 17-18; the coronavirus, ch. 30) the body lives because everyone does their job.

**Characters as arguments.** AE3803 argues for perseverance over talent. U-1146 argues that a gentle person can do a violent duty without becoming cruel. The Killer T Cell argues for strength and aggression and is shown as effective but arrogant. The Helper T Cell argues for planning and command. The Cancer Cell argues the one real dissent: why must some cells die so the body can live? He is given sympathy and defeated (ch. 8-9; BMJ).

**Symbols and motifs.** Red and white uniforms; the oxygen delivery box; the city map of blood vessels; the sneeze as missile (ch. 11); platelets as children building barricades (ch. 4); the outside world as invisible weather (medicines arriving from beyond).

**Traditions it echoes.** Edutainment in the line of Once Upon a Time... Life and Osmosis Jones (Wikipedia); the Japanese workplace drama; for faith readers, Paul's image of the church as one body with many members (1 Corinthians 12).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Naturalism wins: life is explained by biology. Communitarianism and duty ethics are celebrated through the workers. The collectivist order is explored rather than argued; the BMJ reviewer reads it as authoritarian, and the cancer cell's protest is the only voice for the individual.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-2 (ch. 1-9): introductions, the first cancer story. Vol. 3-4 (ch. 10-19): the immune system's training and the blood-loss crisis. Vol. 5 (ch. 20-25): mutation and the cancer cell's return. Vol. 6 (ch. 26-30, 2018-2021): head injury, iPS cells, psoriasis and the novel coronavirus.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Which cell would you want to be, and why?
2. The red blood cell is not the strongest. Why is she the hero?
3. The cancer cell asks why he has to die. How would you answer him?
4. The Killer T Cells are tough on the rookie. Is that good training or bullying?
5. What did you learn about how your body fights a cold?
