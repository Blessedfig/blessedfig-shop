**Premise.** Alita, a cyborg martial artist, wakes in the floating city of Tiphares, resurrected by her old enemy, the mad scientist Desty Nova, just as Nova reveals that the city's adults have had their brains replaced by bio-chips. Tiphares collapses into violence: Nola's parents kill themselves, the young genius Jim Roscoe turns a war robot on the adults, and Alita searches for the brain of her friend Lou (v1-3). Following it to the space city Ketheres, whose peace rests on mind-controlling implants, Alita and the hacker Ping Wu are drawn into the politics of a colonized solar system run from behind the scenes by Aga Mbadi ("Trinidad"). To get to Lou she enters the Zenith of Things Tournament (Z.O.T.T.), a fighting contest held every ten years, with Alita replicas Sechs, Elf and Zwölf as the "Space Angels" (v4-17). Flashbacks reveal Alita's past as Yoko, a Martian-born Panzer Kunst fighter (v6-7), and the post-impact-winter history of the vampire Caerula Sanguis, whose sacrifice helped humanity return to space (v8-9). The tournament ends in Mbadi's downfall (v17); the last two volumes, "Alita Quest," follow Alita through a final death game set by Nova and her choice about her own flesh-and-blood brain (v18-19) (Wikipedia; ANN vol. 17 and 19 reviews).

## Life message

**Being human lies not in flesh, memory or power but in the choices you make and the people you fight for; technology and immortality without conscience turn people into tools or monsters.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Pragmatic | Skill, preparation and hacking win: Alita studies her opponents; Ping's hacking repeatedly saves her; Arthur Farrell's ruthless planning brings humanity back to space (v9). Power without a plan (Anomaly, Whophon) is destroyed. |
| Religious | Biblical and Kabbalistic names frame the world: Melchizedek (the controlling computer), Tiphares and Ketheres (named, in English, after sephirot of the Kabbalah; Japanese Zalem and Jeru echo Salem and Jerusalem) (from general knowledge; to verify). The vampire Victor Byron, a crusader-era knight, reads his fate through his Christian faith and dies believing God chose humanity (v9). Cults (Springfoot Jack, Whophon) are monstrous (v10). |
| Socio-cultural | A cyberpunk critique of control: a utopia built on replaced brains and mind-controlling implants, a ruler who decides what is real, and an immortal elite who pay to fight wars with child soldiers (Leviathan I). Outlook: Bittersweet — tyranny falls and Tiphares begins to rebuild, but many allies die, Lou's rescue drags and the final volume's resolution is quiet and open-ended (ANN vol. 19), with Alita's story continuing on Mars.
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting others: Alita fights to save her friend Lou, kills a man who sends children to die for entertainment (Colonel Payne), and joins those who protect children (the Guntroll); friendship and loyalty (F) come close behind.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the series' defense of human dignity and freedom against mind control and hidden rule, and its protection of children. The divide is over violence and over what makes a person: secular, Satanist and humanist frames read its questions about mind, body and self warmly, while Christian, Jewish and Muslim frames weigh its constant gore and its transhuman setting, and some (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness) also its vampires and hypnotic cults. Pacifist traditions (Anabaptist) object most, because force is the story's only answer.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yukito Kishiro, born 1967 in Tokyo and raised in Chiba; influenced as a teenager by Armored Trooper Votoms, Mobile Suit Gundam (Yoshikazu Yasuhiko's designs) and Rumiko Takahashi; debut at 17 with Space Oddity in Weekly Shonen Sunday (Wikipedia, Yukito Kishiro).
- On-record intent: Kishiro has said he was forced to end the original series early with an ending he was not satisfied with, and returned to Alita's story in 2000; Last Order was first planned to follow the PlayStation game Gunnm: Martian Memory but uses it only sparingly (Wikipedia, citing Kishiro's FAQ on Yukitopia).
- Publication dispute: the series went on hiatus after its 100th installment when Kishiro objected to Shueisha revising three passages of dialogue (including the word "psycho") in a reprint of the original series without his consent; he moved Last Order to Kodansha's Evening in 2011 (Wikipedia, citing ANN news, 2010-2011). This is a public, work-related dispute.
- Several characters were built from readers' design submissions (Wikipedia, character list).
- No statements on religion or politics were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publishers: Shueisha (Ultra Jump), then Kodansha (Evening); English by VIZ Media, then Kodansha USA (16+).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a person a person? Alita has a cyborg body and, it turns out, a real human brain; Tiphares's adults have human bodies and chips for brains; Sechs is a copy of Alita who wants to be more than a copy; Jovians have given up bodies altogether. Last Order keeps asking whether identity lives in flesh, memory, mind or choice.

**The answer the story gives.** Choice and loyalty. Alita becomes herself by fighting for Lou and for others, not by recovering every memory: she is both Yoko and Alita (Phases 38-39). Those who give up their minds to a system (Ketheres) or their consciences to power (Mbadi, Nova's experiments, the immortal elite) become tools or monsters. ANN's reviewer notes that the final volume leaves its biggest moral questions to the reader (v19).

**Characters as arguments.** Alita argues for freedom and friendship. Mbadi argues for order by control, the voice of every benevolent tyrant. Desty Nova argues for knowledge without limits, and is both ally and threat. Jim Roscoe shows despair becoming hatred (v3). Caerula, a vampire who chooses humanity, argues that sacrifice builds the future (v9); Victor, who sees humans as sheep, argues the opposite and dies trusting that God chose them. Springfoot Jack and Whophon argue that life is madness and power is everything, and are destroyed (v10). The Mars Kingdom argues that mortality gives life its worth.

**Symbols and motifs.** Brains and chips. Cities named after the sephirot of the Kabbalah and a computer named for a biblical priest-king (from general knowledge; to verify). The orbital elevator joining a slum to a heaven. The tournament as a world in miniature. Colonel Payne, killed by Alita, lingering in her mind as a Jungian shadow (Wikipedia, character list).

**Traditions it echoes.** Cyberpunk (Neuromancer, Ghost in the Shell), tournament manga (Dragon Ball), and the vampire tradition. For religious readers, Tower of Babel and the search for immortality without God; for Buddhist readers, the question of non-self; for secular readers, debates on human enhancement.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is the world itself, explored rather than simply praised or condemned. Authoritarianism is the clearest target. Existentialism is the heroine's arc. Bushido-like honor and the drive for strength are celebrated in the fights, but strength used to rule is defeated. Christian faith appears through Victor, sympathetically but darkly.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (Phases 1-18): Tiphares's collapse and the trip to space, the sharpest social critique. Volumes 4-17 (Phases 19-105): Leviathan I and the long Z.O.T.T., with flashbacks to Alita's past (v6-7) and Caerula's (v8-9); reviewers see the tournament as overlong. Volumes 18-19 (Phases 106-124): "Alita Quest," a final death game and Alita's choice, setting up Mars Chronicle.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tiphares's adults had their brains replaced and did not know. Were they still themselves?
2. Alita is both Yoko and Alita. Which is the real her, and does it matter?
3. Mbadi says reality should not change without his consent. What is wrong with a ruler who keeps the peace by controlling minds?
4. The Mars Kingdom refuses to make people immortal because life means more when it ends. Do you agree?
5. Alita uses violence to protect people. When is fighting right, and when does it become the thing you fight against?
