**Premise.** Hiroshi Nakahara, a 48-year-old Tokyo salaryman who drinks heavily and is rarely home, boards the wrong train after a business trip and ends up in his boyhood town, Kurayoshi. At his mother's grave at a temple he is carried 34 years into the past: it is 1963 and he is 14 again, with all his adult memories. He relives school, his friendship with Daisuke Shimada, and a first romance with Tomoko Nagase, a classmate he was too shy to approach the first time. He knows that this summer his father, Yoshio, a tailor, will leave the family without explanation, and that his mother will die at 48. He learns his parents' story: his mother's first husband was killed in the Second World War, and Yoshio, his comrade, stayed to care for her and married her, but always felt his life had been chosen for him. Hiroshi confronts his father at the station on the night he leaves; Yoshio admits he is being selfish but says he must find his real self. Hiroshi sees that he is repeating his father's escape from responsibility, and he returns to the present, to his wife Yuko and daughters Akiko and Ayako, changed (EBSCO; Slings & Arrows; Soliloquy in Blue; MyAnimeList user reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could live your teenage years again, knowing everything you know now, what would you change? Hiroshi goes back to the summer his father vanished. The book asks whether the past can be changed, and what a man owes the family he has.

**The answer the story gives.** The past holds: the father still leaves and the mother still dies young (MyAnimeList reviewers). What changes is Hiroshi. Seeing his father's reasons, and his mother's hidden burden, he recognizes his own drift away from his wife and daughters, and goes home to them (EBSCO). Plowright calls it a tale of redemption and awakening.

**Characters as arguments.** Yoshio argues for the right to find one's real self after a life others chose for him (EBSCO). Kazue argues, by her life, for endurance and the cost it carries. Hiroshi, older than his own father was, becomes the one who can understand both, and who must not repeat the pattern. Tomoko and Daisuke are the roads of youth: first love and a writer's dream.

**Symbols and motifs.** The wrong train; the butterfly before each passage (EBSCO: metamorphosis and the souls of the dead); the full moon; the grave; the friend's book titled A Distant Neighborhood at the end (EBSCO).

**Traditions it echoes.** Do-over time travel stories (Wikipedia lists 17 Again, ReLIFE and Erased as related); Taniguchi's own quiet walking stories (The Walking Man, Page 45 and Soliloquy in Blue compare them); the Japanese sense of mono no aware and mujo (EBSCO).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential self-seeking is given a fair hearing through Yoshio and is shown to cost others. Confucian duty and family loyalty are affirmed but not idealized: the mother's sacrifice is painful. Buddhist impermanence colors the whole, in the sense that what is lost cannot be held.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the passage back, school, friendship and the romance, ending as Hiroshi begins to look into why his father will leave, after a talk with his grandmother (Plowright). Vol. 2: the parents' history, the night at the station, the return to the present and two epilogue cameos (Plowright; EBSCO).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hiroshi cannot stop his father from leaving. What can he change?
2. Yoshio says he must find his real self. Is that ever a good reason to leave a family?
3. What did Hiroshi's mother carry that no one saw?
4. Hiroshi was becoming like his father without noticing. What habits do families pass on?
5. If you could talk to one of your parents or grandparents at your age, what would you ask?
