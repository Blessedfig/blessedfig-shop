**Premise.** Karin Maaka, 16 (AniList), belongs to a family of European vampires who settled in Japan two centuries ago, but she is backwards: she makes too much blood instead of drinking it, must bite people to give it away, and otherwise suffers huge nosebleeds. She lives by day, goes to high school and cooks for the family. Each vampire is drawn to a feeling; Karin's blood surges near unhappiness, and it surges most near the poor, hard-working transfer student Kenta Usui, whose depressed young mother Fumio keeps losing jobs (vol. 1). Kenta learns the secret and the family makes him an ally rather than wiping his memory (vol. 2). The two fall slowly in love, confess (vol. 9) and kiss (ch. 50, vol. 12), while Karin's grandmother Elda, a half-vampire coworker named Yuriya and a council of vampire clans complicate things. Karin turns out to be the "Spring of Psyche," whose blood can restore fertility to a dying vampire race; Yuriya's uncle Glark and the Brownlick clan kidnap her to drain her and to force a pregnancy that would produce a successor (vols. 13-14). Kenta and the family rescue her. Her vampire nature leaves her, and her family erases all her memories of them so she can live as an ordinary human; she marries Kenta and has a daughter, Kanon, while her family secretly watches over her (vol. 14) (Wikipedia; VIZ).

**Giving of yourself rather than taking, honesty and faithful love can heal people and bridge any difference; sometimes loving your family means letting them go.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if a vampire had to give instead of take? Chibi Vampire turns the vampire story inside out: its heroine is a blood-giver, drawn to unhappy people and able to make them feel better about themselves. It asks what love looks like when it costs you something, and whether people who are different can share a life.

**The answer the story gives.** Love gives. Karin's blood restores Fumio's confidence (vol. 1) and helps Kenta face his father (vol. 7); Kenta gives his time, his neck and finally his promise to keep her secret for life. The villains are the takers, who would drain Karin to save their kind. The final answer is bittersweet: Karin's family lets her go, erasing her memories so she can live as a human, and watches over her from a distance (vol. 14).

**Characters as arguments.** Karin argues for humility and self-giving. Kenta argues for honest work and faithfulness; his own birth was a source of shame to his grandmother, and he forgives her (vol. 14). Ren argues for vampire superiority and self-indulgence and is treated as comic, though he too loves Karin. Elda argues for pure-blood pride and fear of hybrids, and softens. Yuriya, the sterile half-vampire, argues that mixed love ends in sorrow, then repents. Glark and Bridget argue that one girl's life is a fair price for a species; the story calls that monstrous.

**Symbols and motifs.** Blood as life, given rather than taken. The nosebleed as embarrassment and overflowing love. The sun, which burns the family and lets Karin live by day. Memory: Anju erases classmates' memories (vol. 11), and the family erases Karin's (vol. 14). Sophia, the first Psyche, reborn as Karin's daughter.

**Traditions it echoes.** The vampire romance, inverted; the shonen family comedy; the European folk vampire. For Christian readers, the blood that gives life rather than takes it may suggest sacrificial love; for Buddhist readers, compassion set against greed; for Jewish and Muslim readers, the story's blood play meets real prohibitions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian family loyalty is celebrated. The utilitarian logic of sacrificing one for the many is condemned, as is might-makes-right. The occult furniture is affectionate fantasy. Feminist issues (harassment, teen pregnancy, a forced-pregnancy plot) appear but are not examined in depth.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-18) are school comedy and Kenta's family troubles; volumes 5-7 (ch. 19-30) add the grandmother and Kenta's past; volumes 8-12 (ch. 31-50) bring Yuriya, the romance and Anju's coming of age; volumes 13-14 (ch. 51-58) are the kidnapping, the battle and the time-skip ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Karin helps people by giving away something of her own. Who does that in real life?
2. Why do the villains think it is fair to sacrifice Karin? Why is it wrong?
3. Was it right for Karin's family to erase her memories without asking her?
4. Kenta's grandmother rejected him. Why does he go to see her anyway?
5. Ren and Kenta treat women very differently. What does the story think of each?
