**Premise.** Mina Tepes, princess and ruler of all vampires, has paid off Japan's national debt in exchange for a special district, the Bund, an artificial island in Tokyo Bay, as a refuge for vampires. On a live television debate she kills a famous actor who turns out to be a rampaging vampire and announces to the world that vampires exist and want to live beside humans (ch. 1-3). Her protector is Akira Kaburagi Regendorf, a 17-year-old werewolf of the Beowulf clan who promised as a boy to stay by her side. The two attend a Catholic high school Mina founds, where their classmate Yuki becomes Mina's friend. Fearful humans, terrorists (Telomere), a schemer named Hysterica and the three surviving male "true blood" lords, who want to marry Mina and seize her line, attack the Bund; Mina also shelters the Fangless, vampires who pulled out their own fangs to keep their human values. Later arcs bring a werewolf traitor (vol. 7-8), a clone of Mina who takes her place while she is captured (vol. 9-13) and her return as queen (vol. 14) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Peace between peoples who fear each other needs someone strong enough to shelter the outcasts and to fight the extremists on every side, by ruthless means if necessary; love and a promise kept are what keep that power pointed toward good.**

| Pragmatic | Mina buys a homeland with money, wins public opinion on live TV and uses blackmail, including threatening a politician's young grandson (TV Tropes), to protect her people. The story mostly rewards her hard-headed methods. |
| Religious | Mina's school is Catholic, with nuns and a chapel; crosses harm vampires; students barricade themselves in the chapel during a vampire attack, and Akira is impaled on a cross (TV Tropes; Wikipedia). Vampires are a species with a "true form", not damned souls. Faith readers will weigh sympathy for a persecuted minority and a kept promise against undead heroes, ruthless means and the sexualized depiction of a child-bodied heroine. |
| Socio-cultural | Wikipedia sums up the plot as how fear on both sides changes the status quo. The Bund reads as a story about an outsider minority asking a wary nation for a home; the persecuted Fangless, hated by humans and vampires alike, are its clearest victims. A "special district" bought by paying a nation's debt is the series' political hook. Reading it as an allegory of immigration to Japan is this guide's own reading (to verify). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Mina returns as queen and the Bund survives (ch. 80-82), but friends are maimed or killed, the lords' war goes on into the sequels, and coexistence is still unfinished.
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting others: Mina shelters the Fangless and her people, and Akira keeps his childhood promise to protect her, even becoming a werewolf-vampire hybrid to save her (Wikipedia); love between the two (L) is the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Nozomu Tamaki (story and art), born October 7, 1966, in Setagaya, Tokyo. Dance in the Vampire Bund is his best-known work; he has continued it in four sequel series, the latest (Age of Scarlet Order, TO Books, 2018-) ongoing, and has drawn other series published in English by Seven Seas (Wikipedia, Nozomu Tamaki).
- Wikipedia lists doujinshi and works for adult manga publishers among his earlier work (Wikipedia, Nozomu Tamaki).
- On record: Tamaki gave the anime studio freedom to adapt the story as it saw fit (TV Tropes). No interview about the series' themes or its depiction of Mina was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Media Factory (Monthly Comic Flapper, a seinen magazine), now part of Kadokawa; TO Books for the reprints. Seven Seas publishes it in English, rated Older Teen (16+).
- Controversies: Funimation's planned edits of the anime over nudity involving Mina, reversed after fan pressure (Wikipedia); China's 2015 blacklist of the anime (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a feared people win a place in the world, and what will it cost? Mina buys a home for vampires and steps into the light, betting that honesty will defeat fear. The series asks whether coexistence can be built when both sides are full of extremists.

**The answer the story gives.** Only with strength, loyalty and a willingness to get one's hands dirty. Mina kills a rampaging vampire on live TV to prove she polices her own (ch. 1-3), shelters the Fangless, blackmails the powerful and fights wars. Coexistence survives because someone strong protects it, not because fear goes away.

**Characters as arguments.** Mina argues for protective, unapologetic power. Akira argues for the faithful protector, who keeps his word even when he resents it. Yuki, a frightened human who becomes Mina's friend and later risks her life to expose an impostor (vol. 10-13), argues that fear can turn to loyalty. The lords argue for blood purity and possession; Telomere for human terror; Hikosaka, a bullied boy who becomes a vampire and then a bomber, shows how hate recruits the hurt.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Bund, an artificial island linked to the mainland by one tunnel. Fangs pulled out by choice. Blood as bond, command and pleasure. Mina's two bodies, a child's and a winged adult "true form" she hides because it would let the lords force her to bear their heir (Wikipedia). Crosses and a chapel, used only as weapons and shelter.

**Traditions it echoes.** Dracula and Vlad the Impaler, the "vampires come out" story (True Blood), and the political thriller. Some readers see an allegory of minorities and immigration in Japan (this guide's reading; to verify).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Duty ethics is celebrated in Akira. Authoritarian and strength-rules politics are explored rather than condemned: Mina is an absolute ruler the story wants us to cheer, while the lords' version is villainous. Liberal coexistence is the goal, but force secures it. Hedonism is present in the vampires' nature and in the art itself.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-2 (ch. 1-12): the reveal, the Bund, the school and the chapel attack. Vol. 3-5 (ch. 13-31): Nanami and Yuzuru, the lords, Hysterica's plot and the underground. Vol. 6-8 (ch. 32-49): Akira's werewolf clan and its traitor. Vol. 9-13 (ch. 50-75): the clone Mina, the Bund invaded, the war in the lords' lands. Vol. 14 (ch. 76-82): the queen returns.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mina thinks telling the world the truth about vampires will make peace. Was she right?
2. The Fangless pull out their own fangs. What are they giving up, and why are both sides suspicious of them?
3. Mina uses threats and blackmail to protect her people. When is a leader's ruthlessness wrong even for a good cause?
4. Akira made a promise as a child. Should a promise made that young still bind him?
5. Many reviewers objected to how the series draws Mina. Why does how a story draws a character matter, even when the character is "really" centuries old?
