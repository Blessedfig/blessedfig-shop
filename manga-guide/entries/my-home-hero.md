**Premise.** Tetsuo Tosu, 47, a toy-company salesman who writes mystery novels as a hobby, finds a bruise on his college-student daughter Reika's face. Her boyfriend Nobuto beats her, has killed girlfriends before and plans to drain her grandparents' money and then kill her. Overhearing this from a closet, Tetsuo beats Nobuto to death (ch. 1-3). His wife Kasen arrives and becomes his partner in hiding the body, which Tetsuo dissolves and buries in their garden (ch. 6-7). Nobuto's father is Yoshitatsu Matori, a powerful yakuza earner, and his lieutenant Kubo and the surveillance expert Majima hunt the family (Part 1, ch. 1-48). In Part 2 Tetsuo confesses to police but first takes Reika to Kasen's home village, run by a cult under Kasen's father, where a war with Kubo ends in a massacre (v10-17). In Part 3, seven years later, Reika is a police investigator, Tetsuo is framed for murder, and the fugitives Kubo and Shino return for a final reckoning (v18-26) (Wikipedia; Kodansha; Apple Books).

## Life message

**A father's love can drive an ordinary man to terrible acts; protecting your family is right, but each crime breeds more crimes, more guilt and more danger, and the story keeps asking whether a good dad who has killed can ever be forgiven.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What would you do to save your child, and what would it cost you? Tetsuo's first act is to stop a man who planned to kill his daughter; every chapter after asks whether that act, and the lies needed to hide it, can be lived with.

**The answer the story gives.** Love is right, but crime does not stay small. The killing of Nobuto draws the yakuza to the family's door, the cover-up demands more deception, and in Part 2 Tetsuo's past sets off a massacre he feels guilty for (Apple Books vol. 17). The finale returns to a father risking everything for his family (Apple Books vol. 26) under a banner of crime and punishment (Oricon).

**Characters as arguments.** Tetsuo argues that a father's love justifies anything, and spends the series paying for it. Kasen argues for partnership and nerve; she grew up in a cult and knows how to deceive (Wikipedia). Matori, a yakuza father avenging his son, is Tetsuo's mirror ("Two Fathers," ch. 20). Kubo argues for vengeance and becomes a serial killer. Reika, once the victim, becomes the law (Part 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The garden where the body is buried ("Return to the Earth," ch. 6; "Soil," ch. 23); the home as fortress; the mystery-novel notebook; wiretaps and surveillance; the cult's scripture; the seven-year gap.

**Traditions it echoes.** Crime and Punishment and the inverted detective story (the reader knows the killer); Breaking Bad (IGN France via Wikipedia). For Jewish readers, the law of the rodef fits the first killing; for Christians, Romans 12:19 and the question of forgiveness; for Buddhists, karma returning in a cycle of revenge (ch. 206).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Family loyalty is affirmed; utilitarian trade-offs are explored and shown to multiply harm; authoritarian cults and gangs are condemned; the law (duty ethics) returns in Reika and in Tetsuo's confession.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 (ch. 1-48, v1-6): the killing, the cover-up and the duel with Matori's gang. Part 2 (ch. 49-150, v6-17): new suspicion, confession, the cult village and the massacre. Part 3 (ch. 151-225, v18-26): seven years later, Reika as an investigator, Tetsuo framed, and a final reckoning with Kubo and Shino.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tetsuo kills to save Reika. Was the killing right? Was hiding it right?
2. How does each lie Tetsuo tells lead to the next danger?
3. Matori also loves his son. How is he like Tetsuo, and how is he different?
4. Why do you think Reika becomes a police officer?
5. Can a person who has done something terrible for a good reason be forgiven? By whom?
