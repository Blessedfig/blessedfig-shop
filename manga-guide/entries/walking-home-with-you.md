**Premise.** Shun Satō, a tall first-year at Wasabiyama High, was a middle-school soccer star until an injury ended his hopes of playing professionally; now every sports club wants him for his height, and he drifts. When he mutters that he just wants to go home, second-year Naoki Satō, nicknamed "Chokki-chan" ("go-straight-home girl") for that same catchphrase, drafts him into her one-member, unofficial "Hyper-Epic Head-Home Club" (Wikipedia; Natalie). Despite the name, Naoki loves detours: each chapter is a small after-school mission (a convenience-store run, keeping in touch without smartphones, a river, fireflies, a planetarium, a school trip), logged in a club diary. Naoki calls her small personal enthusiasms her "romance"; Shun, reluctant at first, comes to look forward to the walks and falls for her (Mangapedia; MangaUpdates; Omoi blurb). The old quick read's question is settled: "Chokki-chan" is Naoki's nickname, so Shun's crush is on Naoki.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do after the thing you lived for is gone? Shun was a soccer star; an injury ended it, and everyone around him still sees only a tall athlete to recruit. The series asks whether a life can be rebuilt out of small things.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, and with company. Naoki's club has no trophies, only detours, snacks, fireflies and a diary. Each outing trains Shun to notice, and noticing gives him back a reason to look forward to tomorrow. The romance grows out of that attention.

**Characters as arguments.** Naoki argues that delight is a skill: she calls her small interests her "romance" and treats an ordinary walk as an expedition. Shun argues, at first, for giving up; his slow change is the story. The recruiters stand for a world that values people for what they can do for a team.

**Symbols and motifs.** The walk home itself, a space between school duty and home duty. The club diary. The detour, which the club's name pretends to forbid. Seasonal markers (fireflies, fireworks, first shrine visit) that measure a year of healing.

**Traditions it echoes.** The iyashikei (healing) slice of life; Japanese school-life comedy; for faith readers, gratitude for daily bread and "consider the lilies"; for Buddhist readers, mindfulness; for secular readers, the idea that meaning is made in ordinary time.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism appears in its gentlest form: doing what you love, kindly and with a friend. Existential loss is real but handled softly. No ideology is argued against.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-26) set up the club and its seasons; volumes 4-6 (ch. 27-53) add newer students, festivals and a camp; volume 7 (ch. 54-62) and the latest chapters (ch. 63-66) turn toward the future, career choices, a school trip and, by title, a more open romance (to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shun lost his dream. How did the walks help him, and what helps you when something you love ends?
2. Naoki calls her small interests her "romance." What is yours?
3. Was Shun right to turn down the sports clubs? What is the difference between commitment and pressure?
4. Which outing would you most want to join, and why?
5. How can you tell a friend is drifting, and what could you invite them to do?
