**Premise.** Rikuo Uozumi has finished university in Tokyo and drifts through a convenience-store job, taking photographs but refusing to make it a career. A strange, black-clad girl, Haru Nonaka, who keeps a crow named Kansuke with a lame leg, starts dropping by: she fell for him years ago when she handed back the exam ticket he dropped on the day of his university entrance exam. Haru left high school after being caught working at a bar, lives alone in her late grandfather's old house and works at the Milk Hall café, which turns into a bar at night (Japanese Wikipedia; AniList). Rikuo still loves his college friend Shinako Morinome, now a chemistry teacher, who turned him down because she cannot let go of her childhood love Yū Hayakawa, who died young. Yū's younger brother Rō, about seven years younger than Shinako and a student at the school where she teaches, follows her to Tokyo and loves her too (Japanese Wikipedia; TV Tropes). Over eleven volumes Rikuo takes a job at a photo studio (vol. 5) and quits the store (vol. 6); Shinako and Rikuo try dating and part as friends; Haru dates a cram-school teacher, Amamiya, who leaves to care for a childhood companion; Rō lives with a model, Rio, follows her to Italy and comes back for Shinako. In the end Rikuo and Haru become a couple, and the last chapter leaves open whether Shinako and Rō will (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**You cannot live in yesterday: grief and old loves should be honored and then let go, and a vocation and a love are found by small, awkward steps forward rather than by waiting for life to start.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Kei Toume, born April 13 in Zama, Kanagawa; studied at Tama Art University; debuted with Rokujō gekijō (1992); known for Lament of the Lamb and Kurogane, both published in English (Wikipedia, Kei Toume).
- On-record intent (Japanese Wikipedia, summarizing interviews in Febri and the EX collection): after dark works like Lament of the Lamb and Kurogane, she wanted a bright story with bright characters; never having worked in a company, she made the Business Jump series about a freeter who does no business; she expected about three volumes but enjoyed it and the story grew; male characters have water-related names and female characters plant-related ones; she avoided markers of the period, which is why the leads rarely use mobile phones; on finishing she thanked readers who waited patiently through the long gaps.
- The setting is a small town on a private railway near Shinjuku, which Toume identified as Setagaya; Yokohama and Kanazawa also appear (Japanese Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Business Jump, then Grand Jump, both seinen magazines). English publisher and rating not confirmed.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How long can you stay in yesterday? Rikuo lives in a pause after university; Shinako lives beside a grave; Rō lives in his dead brother's shadow; Haru lives on a single kind moment years ago. The series asks what it takes for each of them to step into tomorrow.

**The answer the story gives.** Small steps, and other people. Rikuo starts by taking one job seriously (vol. 5). Shinako tries loving someone living, even though it does not last. Rō tests another life with Rio and comes back knowing what he wants. Haru's constancy finally meets Rikuo's choice. Toume, who expected a three-volume story, let it grow for eighteen years, and the slowness is part of the answer: growing up takes time.

**Characters as arguments.** Rikuo argues for freedom and finds it empty until it is used. Shinako argues for faithfulness to the dead and learns its cost. Haru argues for open-hearted persistence; readers disagree whether it is strength or self-neglect. Rō argues to be seen as himself. Fukuda, with his planned life, is the foil who shows the path Rikuo did not take.

**Symbols and motifs.** Kansuke the crow with the lame leg; Rikuo's camera and his rule against photographing people; Haru's strange rice balls filled with chocolate or bean paste; water names for the men and plant names for the women; the RC Succession song of the title; Shinako looking at the sky on the last page.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese seinen slice-of-life drama, the long-form love polygon (Maison Ikkoku is a common comparison; from general knowledge; to verify), the coming-of-age story of the late twenties, and in its theme of releasing the dead, ideas found in many traditions (Ecclesiastes' time to mourn; Buddhist letting go), though it quotes none of them.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential choice and self-expression are affirmed but only when joined to commitment; communitarian friendship is the safety net; traditional paths (a company job, family duty) are neither scorned nor idealized.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes (1-3) set up the love triangle and the freeter life; middle volumes (4-6) bring Rikuo's job change and new rivals (Minato, Rō's friends); late volumes (7-11) drop the chapter-arc structure, pair Haru with Amamiya and Rikuo with Shinako, and resolve the quadrangle; the 2020 epilogue shows Haru and Rikuo's future (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shinako cannot let go of Yū. When does remembering someone help, and when does it hold you back?
2. Haru waits years for Rikuo. Is that admirable, and what would you tell a friend in her place?
3. Rikuo refuses to make photography his job at first. Why might someone be afraid to try at what they love?
4. Rō is a student and Shinako is a teacher. Why does she keep him at a distance?
5. What small step could you take toward something you keep putting off?
