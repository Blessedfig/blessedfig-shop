**Premise.** Present-day Tokyo. A manga artist named Usamaru Furuya finds the photographs and notebooks of Yozo Oba online and tells his story. Yozo, the son of a wealthy, distant politician from Japan's far north, has never understood other people and fears them; since childhood he has hidden behind clowning to win approval. In Tokyo he falls under the influence of Horiki, a fellow art student, and drifts into drinking and affairs, living off a series of women who shelter him. He survives a double-suicide pact in which the woman drowns; tries to make a home with a single mother and her little daughter, then leaves them; marries Yoshiko, a trusting young woman who helps him stop drinking; and falls apart again after she is sexually assaulted. A second suicide attempt, morphine addiction and confinement in a mental hospital follow, and the story ends with Yozo living in isolation, feeling neither happy nor unhappy (Wikipedia, novel plot; Dacey on the manga's changes; Kodansha USA blurb via AniList).

## Life message

**None is held up as a lesson: the story shows a man destroyed by fear of others, a false face, drink and the people who use him and whom he uses, and it asks what it means to be fully human, leaving almost no path out.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Usamaru Furuya, born January 25, 1968, in Tokyo, took the Osamu Tezuka manga correspondence course as a child, studied oil painting at Tama Art University and debuted in the alternative magazine Garo in 1994. He works across underground and mainstream magazines in styles from photorealistic to cute; Christianity is a recurring theme in his work, often in young people's struggle to grow up (Wikipedia, citing Sean Patrick Webb).
- On-record intent: none found in sources opened (search budget used up). His choices, as reviewers describe them: a present-day setting, himself as narrator, and the childhood section reduced to brief flashbacks (Dacey).
- Osamu Dazai (Shūji Tsushima, 1909-1948), son of a wealthy landowner and politician from Aomori, wrote No Longer Human as his last book; Wikipedia calls it semi-autobiographical. Like Yozo, Dazai attempted suicide several times with partners and died by suicide in 1948 (Wikipedia). This is part of the book's history, which parents may want to know.
- Publisher: Shinchosha (Weekly Comic Bunch, a seinen magazine). Vertical, a publisher of literary and adult manga now part of Kodansha USA, released the English edition (from general knowledge; to verify).
- No controversies involving the adaptation were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to be human with other people? Yozo has never understood others and is terrified of them; he survives by playing the fool. The title, "disqualified as a human," is his verdict on himself.

**The answer the story gives.** Almost none. The story shows what happens when a person cannot be honest with anyone: every relationship becomes a performance or a refuge, and refuge turns into ruin. It offers no rescue, which is why it reads as a warning to some and a mirror to others.

**Characters as arguments.** Yozo argues, by his life, that a false face destroys the one who wears it. Horiki is the friend who makes it easy to fall. Yoshiko is innocence, and what happens to her becomes Yozo's bitter question of whether trusting others is a sin (novel). The women who shelter him show care that he cannot return. Furuya the narrator stands for the reader, trying to understand rather than condemn.

**Symbols and motifs.** Three photographs at three ages, each emptier than the last (novel frame). The marionette, the drowning man, the burning man (Dacey). Blurred faces. In the novel, a self-portrait so dark he shows it only to one friend.

**Traditions it echoes.** Dostoevsky (Yozo recalls Crime and Punishment in the novel) and the Japanese confessional novel; for Christian readers, Augustine's restless heart and Romans 7, with the difference that grace never comes. Buddhist readers see craving and a false self at the root of suffering.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is the frame: the search for what makes a person human. Nihilism and hedonism are shown from inside and found empty. Expressive individualism is complicated: the problem is not that Yozo cannot express himself but that he has no self he can trust. The Christian lens appears as a God of punishment Yozo cannot escape and a love he cannot believe in.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 introduces Yozo through the narrator, his school years and his first fall with Horiki (likely including the double-suicide pact). Volumes 2-3 follow the women who shelter him, the marriage to Yoshiko, her assault, the second attempt and the hospital (placement to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yozo hides behind jokes. Why do people wear masks like that, and what does it cost?
2. Horiki keeps pulling Yozo back. How do you recognize a friend who is bad for you?
3. Yozo believes in God's punishment but not in God's love. What might have changed that?
4. The story offers no hope. Does a story have to offer hope to be worth reading?
5. If a friend talked the way Yozo writes, who could they turn to? (Point to a trusted adult or a crisis line such as 988 in the US.)
