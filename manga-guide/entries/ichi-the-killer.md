**Premise.** In Shinjuku's Kabukichō red-light district, a man called the Old Geezer (Jijii) uses Ichi, a timid, tearful young man (22, per TV Tropes) with martial-arts training and a blade in his boot, to kill the head of the Anjo yakuza family and steal his money (v1). Anjo's enforcer Kakihara, a sadomasochist who lives for pain, hunts the killer, torturing suspects along the way (v2-4). Jijii manipulates Ichi through his memories of being bullied and of a girl who was raped after defending him in high school, memories that trigger his rages and his sexual arousal (v1-9). Ichi kills Kakihara's men one by one; Kaneko, a frightened hitman who befriended Ichi, is killed by him (v9). In the final fight Kakihara falls to his death (v10). The "masochistic girl" on the phone who fed Ichi's fantasies turns out to be Karen, Jijii's partner, and Ichi kills her. Three years later Ichi has recovered and lives under his real name, Shiroishi, while Jijii talks about training a new killing machine: Kaneko's young son Takeshi, who is seen covered in blood with a knife; Jijii is also revealed, on a phone line, as possibly an old man with Alzheimer's (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Violence is a sickness made out of bullying, humiliation and manipulation: the "killing machine" is built, not born, those who build him use him and throw him away, and the cycle passes to the next hurt child.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Dominance and submission in raw form: yakuza ranks enforced by pain, a bullied boy's rage turned predatory, and sex fused with violence in almost every major character (Kakihara, Ichi, the twins). Kin bonds exist only at the margins: Kaneko and his son Takeshi. |
| Pragmatic | Jijii treats people as tools: he steals Anjo's money, takes 300 million yen to destroy the Anjo family (v3), and builds a killer from trauma and suggestion; the epilogue has him describe Ichi's "right mix of imagination and reality" (v10). |
| Religious | No religion appears. Faith readers will see bondage to wrath and lust without grace: cruelty is never redeemed, and the ending implies the cycle continues with a child. Some readers may find a bleak moral in that; many traditions will find the depiction itself the main issue. |
| Socio-cultural | Machiyama (Pulp, via Wikipedia) read it as existentialist commentary on how people affirm that they are alive inside a peaceful consumer society. Bullying, the sex trade and the yakuza world of Kabukichō are its setting. Beveridge (AnimeOnDVD, on the OVA) calls it an exploration of how people respond to violence, pressure and sexuality. |

Outlook: Bleak — almost every character dies, the one who recovers (Ichi) does so only after a massacre, and the last pages hand the role of killing machine to a child.
Hope source: N — finds little hope: Ichi's recovery after leaving Shinjuku is the only light, and the final panel suggests his rage may return (TV Tropes).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Where does violence come from? Ichi is a weeping, childlike man who kills like a machine, and Kakihara is a man who can only feel alive in pain. The series asks what made them and who profits from them.

**The answer the story gives.** Violence is manufactured. Ichi's rages are built from bullying, humiliation and a memory of sexual violence that Jijii feeds and shapes (v1-9); Jijii explains at the end that the killer came from the right mix of imagination and reality, and that leaving Shinjuku cured him (v10). The answer offers almost no hope: the maker survives and has already found the next child.

**Characters as arguments.** Ichi argues that the bullied can become monsters. Kakihara argues that pain is the only real thing, and dies the moment he feels it. Jijii argues that people are tools; the story half-undercuts him by suggesting he may be a confused old man. Kaneko, afraid and loving, is the one sympathetic adult, and he is killed.

**Symbols and motifs.** Tears before killing. The number 1 written in blood (v1). Kakihara's slit cheeks and piercings. The telephone voice that feeds fantasies. The blade in the boot. A child with a bloody knife (v10).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese ero-guro (erotic grotesque) art, yakuza crime fiction, and the psychological horror of the serial-killer story; critics compared the OVA to Cronenberg and De Palma (Toole, via Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism and might-makes-right are the world of the story; existentialism is its question. Hedonism in the form of sadomasochism is followed to its end and found empty. No religious or communal view is offered as an answer.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 set up the hunt: Anjo's murder, Kakihara's search, Ichi's first rages. Volumes 5-8 are the massacre of Kakihara's men and the twins' cruelty. Volumes 9-10 bring the confrontation, Kaneko's death, the final fight and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ichi was bullied as a child. How much of what he became is his fault?
2. Jijii used Ichi's worst memories against him. Why is that a kind of violence too?
3. Kakihara wanted to feel pain more than anything. What do you think he was really looking for?
4. The story ends with Takeshi. What does that ending say about violence?
5. Some people call this series art and others call it exploitation. What would you need to know to decide?
