**Premise.** Minato Asano is a tall first-year who stoops to look smaller and chose her high school because it had no concert band (ANN). She is a gifted trombonist, but being shut out by her bandmates in junior high has left her afraid of standing out or giving offense, so she uses her sharp ear to blend in (Manga Bookshelf, vol. 1). Second-year Hibiki Sugawa, who is trying to revive a school band, hears her and will not take no for an answer; Okazu notes that Hibiki's pushiness can mean she does not listen to what Minato wants, but her intentions are good. The band (Hibiki, Minato, Chika, Sakiko and Emma) sneaks out for late-night practice, at one point running from police with their gear (ANN). Minato notices that Hibiki is hiding a wrist injury and makes her stop (ANN; Manga Bookshelf), and Hibiki's family history has left her resentful of competitive music (ANN). In volume 2 Minato performs at the band's first concert, faces former classmates in the audience and wins the crowd; she sees Chika and Sakiko kissing and learns how they became a couple, and she blurts out that she likes Hibiki (Okazu). In volume 3 Hibiki is graduating; Minato turns down a backup spot in an adult local band out of fear, lashes out at Hibiki, and the two finally say what they need to say; the story ends a year later with the two meeting on a spring day (Okazu, Japanese vol. 3). One reviewer notes the main couple is never shown kissing (Drunken Anime Blog).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to come out of hiding? Minato has a rare gift and has built her life around not being noticed: she stoops, blends her playing into everyone else's, and picked a school with no band (ANN). The series asks whether a person hurt for standing out can risk it again.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if someone wants you as you are and stays when you falter. Hibiki's pushing is the first step, the band's support at the concert is the second (Okazu, vol. 2), and Minato's own choice to speak her feelings is the last (vol. 2-3). Gaffney credits the final volume for showing that being pushed out of one's comfort zone can be good.

**Characters as arguments.** Minato argues that safety is not the same as living. Hibiki argues for joy in music without competition, and is corrected: she cannot play through pain, and she is more like her father than she thinks (Gaffney, vol. 3). Chika and Sakiko show a later stage of love, steadier and a little bitter (Drunken Anime Blog). The junior-high bandmates are the silent case for conformity; they lose the room when Minato plays.

**Symbols and motifs.** The trombone, a loud instrument for a girl who wants to be quiet. Minato's height and posture. The hidden wrist injury, which only Minato can hear. The concert stage. A spring day a year later.

**Traditions it echoes.** The school-band story and the Japanese girls'-love genre, here without the tragic note common in older yuri (Drunken Anime Blog). Many religious and secular readers alike will recognize the shape of healing through community; traditions differ on the romance itself.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: Minato learns to stand tall. Communitarianism is the means: the band heals. Progressivism is assumed rather than argued, since no character questions the romances.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 is recruitment, night practice and Hibiki's injury; volume 2 is the concert, the side couple and Minato's confession; volume 3 is graduation, conflict and the reunion a year later (Okazu; ANN; Manga Bookshelf).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Minato try so hard to blend in? What would you have said to her?
2. Hibiki would not take no for an answer. When is pushing a friend kind, and when does it ignore what they want?
3. How did Minato know Hibiki was hurt? What does it mean to really pay attention to someone?
4. The people who shut Minato out never apologize. How does she move on anyway?
5. Talk about what your family believes about dating and romance, and how this story's couples fit or differ.
