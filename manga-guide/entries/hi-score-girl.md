**Premise.** In 1991 Haruo Yaguchi, a sixth grader with bad grades, no friends and a big ego, lives for the arcade. Then Akira Ono, a rich, silent, top-of-the-class classmate, beats him 30 times in a row at Street Fighter II. Their rivalry turns into a friendship carried on almost entirely through games, since Akira never speaks and communicates by gestures, looks and the occasional punch. Akira escapes a crushing heir's regimen run by her family's instructor, Moemi Gouda, and is sent to Los Angeles at 12 (vol. 1). In middle school Haruo grows close to Koharu Hidaka, a lonely, studious girl whose family shop installs arcade machines (vol. 2), and a love triangle forms when Akira returns. The story follows the three from about age 12 to 17 through the 1990s arcade boom, with real games and hardware drawn in detail. In the last volume, after losing a tournament to Akira, Haruo races to the airport as she is sent back to America for good, confesses, and promises to follow her and marry her; she accepts, and Koharu steps aside (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; ANN vols. 9-10).

**A shared passion can grow into friendship and love, and growing up means learning to care for someone else more than for your own wins.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Rensuke Oshikiri, born 1979 in Tokyo, grew up in Kawasaki from about age three (Japanese Wikipedia); much of the series' arcade scene is set around Kawasaki (Felicia Nikotama's Mizonokuchi team, Wikipedia). He debuted in 1998 in Weekly Young Magazine with horror-comedy, had his first long run with Dero Dero (2003-2009), and wrote the bleak, gag-free horror Misumisou (2007-2009) before starting Hi Score Girl in 2010 (Japanese Wikipedia).
- On-record influences: he credits one game, Yuyami Doori Tankentai, with turning him from gag manga toward human drama (Japanese Wikipedia, citing an Ohta Books interview). No interview about Hi Score Girl's themes was opened (search cap). **Status: Needs more info on the creator's statements.**
- Copyright dispute (public record, work-related): in 2014 SNK Playmore complained that the manga used its game characters without permission; police searched Square Enix's offices, printed volumes were recalled and sales paused; the case was settled in 2015 and the manga resumed and was reissued (English and Japanese Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Square Enix (Monthly Big Gangan, seinen). The English edition is published by Square Enix Manga & Books, rated Teen.
- No statements by the creator on religion or politics were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can two people who cannot say what they feel still come to know each other? Haruo has no friends and too much pride; Akira has every advantage and no voice at all, literally, since she never speaks. Games become their shared language.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly, by showing up again and again. Haruo starts out a "snarky brat with an ego" (Wikipedia) and grows by losing to Akira, by caring for Koharu without leading her on for long, and finally by saying out loud what he feels (vol. 10). ANN notes the characters express feelings through matches instead of words, and that Haruo finally sees he has been imitating Guile and becomes his own person.

**Characters as arguments.** Haruo argues for passion that turns into care. Akira argues for a self that survives a regimen designed to erase it. Gouda is the case for duty without joy, and she bends when she sees what it costs Akira (TV Tropes). Haruo's mother is the opposite model: loud, loving, strict about grades and generous about play. Koharu is the honest rival in love who loses graciously. Makoto, who refused the heir's role, shows the price someone else paid for her freedom.

**Symbols and motifs.** The arcade cabinet as meeting place; 30 straight losses; Guile as a nagging inner voice; the toy ring Haruo gave Akira, which ends a fistfight when he sees she still wears it (TV Tropes); the airport, where Akira leaves at 12 and again at 17.

**Traditions it echoes.** The childhood-sweetheart romance and the Japanese coming-of-age story, and the gamer memoir. For some Christian and Jewish readers it echoes the question of how a gift (play) becomes an idol; for Buddhist readers, the move from craving to care. My own framing.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: the children follow what they love. Traditionalism and duty to the family name are critiqued but not mocked; Gouda is softened, not destroyed. Consumerism is present only as nostalgia for real products.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-9): sixth grade, the rivalry, Akira leaves for Los Angeles. Vols. 2-4 (ch. 10-27): middle school, Koharu, the triangle, the blowup at the end of vol. 4. Vols. 5-8 (ch. 28-51): high school, tournaments, Akira's family pressure, Makoto's role grows (ANN). Vols. 9-10 (ch. 52-63): the tournament, Koharu's match with Akira, the airport confession.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Haruo lost to Akira 30 times. Why did he keep coming back?
2. Akira never speaks. How do the other characters know what she feels, and do they always get it right?
3. Gouda changes her mind about games. What made her see things differently?
4. Haruo's mother is strict about grades but lets him play. Is that a good balance?
5. Koharu steps aside. Was that brave, sad, or both?
