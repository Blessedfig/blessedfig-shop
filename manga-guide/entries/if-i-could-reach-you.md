**Premise.** Uta, a hardworking high school girl, realizes at her older brother Reiichi's wedding that she is in love with the bride, Kaoru, a childhood friend who is now her sister-in-law. A year later she lives with the couple and tries to let her feelings go. Kaoru, warm and lonely, treats Uta as a little sister and confides in her as cracks show in the marriage (vol. 1). Uta's friends Kuro, Miyabi and the free-spirited Konatsu have their own love stories (vol. 2). Kaoru finds Reiichi with another woman at the end of vol. 2, falls and breaks her ankle, and loses some memory for a time (vol. 3); a trip gives Uta and Kaoru a heart-to-heart (vol. 3). Uta and Reiichi's mother, whose harsh words left Uta feeling like a burden, returns from abroad and Uta moves out (vol. 4); Kaoru seeks an honest talk with Uta (vol. 5); Kaoru and Reiichi face what is unspoken in their marriage (vol. 6); Kaoru sends Uta an SOS, finally says what she has been holding back, and decides to take the next step (vol. 7) (Penguin Random House blurbs; Anime UK News; Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with a love you cannot act on? Uta realizes she loves Kaoru on the very day Kaoru marries her brother. She cannot unfeel it, and she cannot leave the house she shares with them. The series asks whether silence is kindness or a slow harm to everyone.

**The answer the story gives.** Silence protects for a while, then fails. Uta's restraint is shown as admirable, but the household's unspoken things (Reiichi's secret, Kaoru's loneliness, Uta's love) are what break the marriage. The way forward is honesty: Uta admits her feelings to herself (vol. 2), Kaoru seeks a heart-to-heart (vol. 5) and finally says what she held back (vol. 7).

**Characters as arguments.** Uta argues for love that respects limits. Kaoru argues for keeping the peace by smiling, and learns its cost. Reiichi is kept at a distance so the reader shares Kaoru's doubts (Anime UK News, vol. 3). Konatsu separates romance from sex and lives frankly. Kuro and Miyabi show a younger, uneven love that has to be worked out. Uta's mother is the source of Uta's feeling that she is a burden.

**Symbols and motifs.** The thread of the Japanese title: a thread that does not reach, echoing the red thread of fate. The blue-tinted opening at the wedding (Okazu, Luce). Light and dark pages for happy and dark moments (Anime UK News). The SOS on a phone screen (vol. 7).

**Traditions it echoes.** Yuri magazine drama about unrequited love and "too late" love; Luce compares its mood to Inio Asano's Solanin. It is close in setup to Kuzushiro's "I'm Living with My Brother's Wife" (Okazu), with the difference that the brother is alive.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins in the end, but slowly and at a price. Traditionalism is not mocked: Uta honors the marriage while it lasts, and the story treats the marriage's failure as sad, not as a victory. Progressive readers find a gentle queer coming-of-age; religious readers find restraint and honesty they share, wrapped around a romance many of them do not.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: Uta's secret love and her friends' romances. Vol. 3: Kaoru's crisis and the trip. Vols. 4-5: distance, the mother's return, Uta moving out. Vols. 6-7: Kaoru and Reiichi's marriage faces the truth and Kaoru reaches for Uta. The Japanese-only epilogue (not in the English edition) shows what becomes of Uta and Kaoru.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Uta did not choose her feelings. What choices does she still have?
2. Is keeping a secret to protect someone ever the right thing? When does it stop being kind?
3. Why do you think the author shows us so little of Reiichi's thoughts?
4. Uta's mother made her feel like a burden. How do words from parents stay with us?
5. Different families believe different things about same-sex relationships. What does yours believe, and how would you treat a friend like Uta?
