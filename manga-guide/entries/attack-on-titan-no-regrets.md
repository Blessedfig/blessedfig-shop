**Premise.** In year 844, beneath the royal capital, the Underground is a sunless slum of criminals and the poor. Levi, a thief who has taught himself the military's vertical maneuvering gear, runs a small crew with his clever friend Furlan Church and Isabel Magnolia, an energetic girl he once saved. Squad leader Erwin Smith of the Survey Corps, who is fighting a corrupt noble, Nicholas Lovof, to keep the Corps funded, captures the three and gives them a choice: enlist or go to the Military Police (ch. 1). Secretly, Lovof's agent has hired them to steal incriminating documents from Erwin in exchange for citizenship above ground, and Levi privately means to kill Erwin (ch. 2, 5). On their first expedition outside the Walls the trio prove their skill (ch. 4), but in a storm Levi leaves his friends to go after Erwin, and Titans kill Isabel and Furlan (ch. 7). Levi slaughters the Titans, finds his friends dead, and confronts Erwin, who reveals the documents were a decoy and that he knew of the plot from the start. Erwin asks Levi who is really to blame and invites him to fight for humanity's freedom; Levi lowers his blade and follows him (ch. 8) (fan wiki; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**No one can know how a choice will turn out, so choose, carry the cost without regret, and give your strength to a cause larger than yourself.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A tight kin-like band of three survives a predatory world (the Underground, then Titans that eat people). Loyalty to the small group is fierce; the story asks whether that loyalty can widen to the whole species (Isabel's question about dying for humanity's cause, ch. 5). |
| Pragmatic | Erwin is the pragmatist: he blackmails a corrupt noble to save the Corps' funding and recruits criminals because they are useful (ch. 2; Sean Gaffney's review). Levi learns that his plan, his pride and his split from his friends had costs no one could foresee (ch. 7-8). |
| Religious | No religion appears. Christian readers have found a parallel in Erwin calling unworthy people into his service (Geeks Under Grace, citing John 15:16). Faith readers will weigh the ending's "no regrets" against repentance, and the implied revenge killing (ch. 2) that the story never judges. |
| Socio-cultural | The Underground versus the sunlit capital is a picture of class: the poor are literally kept from the sun, and a noble diverts military money to a supplier (ch. 2). Isabel's wonder at sunlight and well-dressed citizens (ch. 2) and the trio's hope of a "proud life" above ground (Kodansha vol. 2 blurb) drive the tragedy. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Levi loses the two people he lived for, but the ending gives him a purpose and the bond with Erwin that carries the main series.
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting others: Erwin redirects Levi's grief from blame to the fight to free humanity, and Levi chooses that cause (ch. 8); friendship (F) is the love that is lost.

## Creator and publisher context

- Gun Snark (砂阿久雁, Sunaaku Gan) is a scenario writer at the visual-novel studio Nitroplus; he wrote the two "A Choice With No Regrets" visual novels bundled with the Attack on Titan Blu-rays in 2013, which the manga adapts (Wikipedia). Character designs for the novels were by Namaniku ATK of Nitroplus.
- Hikaru Suruga (art): her editor-in-chief sent her to the Metropolitan Area Outer Underground Discharge Channel to picture the Underground; she drew Levi younger than in the main series and wanted Isabel "very cute" but as capable as anyone in a fight (Wikipedia, citing the vol. 1 afterword).
- Hajime Isayama (original concept) created Attack on Titan; he modeled Levi on Rorschach from Watchmen and named him after a child in the documentary Jesus Camp (Wikipedia, Levi Ackerman).
- Publisher: Kodansha. The run in Aria, a shōjo magazine, is notable: the series raised the magazine's print run tenfold (Wikipedia). English publisher Kodansha USA rates it 16+.
- No creator interviews about the themes were found or opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.** No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you choose rightly when you cannot know how things will turn out? Levi has three choices in the book: to accept Erwin's deal (ch. 1), to leave his friends to go after Erwin in the storm (ch. 7), and to follow Erwin after his friends die (ch. 8). The middle choice is the one that haunts him.

**The answer the story gives.** Erwin's answer, which the book adopts: no one can know the outcome, so regret is useless; the blame belongs to the Titans, and the fitting response to loss is to fight them (ch. 8; Wikipedia OVA summary). Levi lowers his blade. The book does not ask whether Erwin, who knew of the plot and used a decoy, carries any of the blame.

**Characters as arguments.** Levi argues for loyalty to one's own. Furlan argues for cleverness: a plan to blackmail the blackmailer and win a life in the capital (ch. 5). Isabel argues for hope; she is the first to say she understands why the Scouts go outside the Walls (ch. 6). Erwin argues that a cause justifies hard means. Lovof is the case against using public trust for private gain.

**Symbols and motifs.** Birds seen through a grate in the Underground (prologue) and the Survey Corps' "Wings of Freedom" emblem (ch. 1 title); sunlight seen for the first time (ch. 2); the storm that blinds everyone (ch. 6-7); cleaning as Levi's way of making order in chaos (ch. 3; side story).

**Traditions it echoes.** The war-story pattern of the reluctant recruit, the noir figure of the thief with a code, and the Japanese ideal of resolve without regret. For some Christian readers, Erwin choosing outcasts echoes God choosing the unworthy (Geeks Under Grace, John 15:16); Hindu readers may hear the Gita's counsel to act without clinging to results; Buddhist readers may hear the teaching of impermanence.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism wins openly. Utilitarianism is embodied in Erwin and never refuted. Critical theory is present only as setting (the slum, the corrupt noble). Communitarianism is honored and then grieved. No religious lens is present.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (prologue, ch. 1-4) is capture, training and the first Titan kill, with political scheming. Volume 2 (ch. 5-8) turns to the expedition, the storm and the deaths, ending in Levi's choice. The comic side stories are light.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Levi leaves his friends to chase Erwin in the storm (ch. 7). Was that his mistake, or could no one have known?
2. Erwin knew about the plot all along and used a fake document. Is he partly responsible for the deaths?
3. What is the difference between "no regrets" and never admitting you were wrong?
4. Levi seems to have taken revenge on the people who hurt Isabel (ch. 2). The story never comments. What do you think?
5. Why do you think Levi chooses to follow Erwin in the end?
