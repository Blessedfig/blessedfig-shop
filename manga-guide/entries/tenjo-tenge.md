**Premise.** Street brawlers Souichiro Nagi and his childhood friend Bob Makihara enter Toudou Academy planning to rule it by force, as at their old schools, and find a school founded to teach combat, where some students wield ki-based powers (pyrokinesis, precognition, superhuman strength). Beaten by the ruling Executive Council under Mitsuomi Takayanagi, they join the last club that opposes it, the Juken Club, led by 17-year-old Maya Natsume, whose younger sister Aya has "dragon eyes" that see past and future (v1-4). A long flashback (v5-8) reveals how Maya's brother Shin, driven mad by the dragon eyes and a cursed sword, the Reiki, died, and what Mitsuomi and Shin's girlfriend Mana did. Later arcs widen into a feud among twelve founding families dating to the siege of Osaka Castle (1614-15), the centuries-old Sōhaku Kago (Souichiro's father), who cheats death by ki, a feudal-era flashback, and a final clash of reborn gods, Susano-o and Amaterasu (v9-22; Wikipedia; VIZ vol. 11).

## Life message

**Strength is worth having only when it serves something beyond itself, such as protecting the people you love and breaking cycles of hatred handed down by your elders; strength sought for its own sake destroys the one who seeks it.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A dominance tournament among adolescents: rank in the school is won by fighting, and sexual attraction is drawn as a constant motivator. Oh! great uses sex as a powerful motivator, for good and ill (Wikipedia, Reception). Bloodlines and inherited powers drive the plot. |
| Pragmatic | Training and technique decide fights, not virtue alone. Characters repeatedly stop to ask what strength is for; Wikipedia's reception notes the characters' reasoning about strength and truth as a rare strength of the series. |
| Religious | Buddhist and Shinto material is used as fuel for powers: the title's Buddhist phrase, a sword said to hold the power of evil Buddhist rites (the Reiki), a Buddhist priest warrior in the feudal arc, a shrine maiden, a demon-exorcist family and the gods Susano-o and Amaterasu reborn (Wikipedia; VIZ vol. 11). Ki or qi is treated as a cultivable force that can even revive the dead. Faith readers will weigh this alongside the sexual content. |
| Socio-cultural | A yankii (delinquent) school-fighting manga crossed with Sengoku and Tokugawa history. It treats hierarchies (the Executive Council, the Takayanagi family, the twelve houses) as dangerous and inherited feuds as a curse. Bob, of South African descent, is called "the Foreigner" by some students when he arrives (Wikipedia, characters). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the four-hundred-year conflict is resolved and the young fighters break with their elders' plans, but many characters die along the way (Shin, Mana, Chi'en, Midori and others), and ANN's Carlo Santos found the late story a "beautiful mess."
Hope source: P — the way through is protecting others: the characters who find a reason to be strong for someone else (Maya for her club, Souichiro for Aya and his friends) are the ones the story vindicates; friendship (F) is the second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Oh! great (Ito Ōgure), born 1972 in Hyūga, Miyazaki; a former salaryman who entered a manga contest for the prize money after pachinko debts; debuted in 1995 in the adult magazine Manga Hot Milk; also created the adult series Silky Whip and Naked Star; Tenjo Tenge was his first mainstream series. Later works: Air Gear (Kodansha Manga Award 2006) and the Bakemonogatari manga (Wikipedia, Oh! great).
- Style (Wikipedia): violent plots, English words amid kanji, and heavy fan service. Publishers Weekly's Kai-Ming Cha (via Wikipedia) says his use of sex in Tenjo Tenge is seen by readers as important to the story. Favorite childhood manga: Fist of the North Star and Ring ni Kakero.
- No statements on religion, politics or the series' sexual violence were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Ultra Jump, seinen). CMX's censored edition was said to be made with Shueisha's and the creator's review of each change (Wikipedia). VIZ (owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan; from general knowledge) published it uncut under VIZ Signature, rated Mature.
- Controversy: CMX's edits of nudity and sexual content drew a fan boycott and the resignation of its group editor (Wikipedia; Jason Thompson called it one of the greatest censorship fails). No controversy about the creator personally was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is strength for? Souichiro and Bob arrive to rule a school by force and find a place where force is the only law. The title, a phrase attributed to the newborn Buddha that now means arrogance, asks whether "I alone am the honored one" is enlightenment or ego.

**The answer the story gives.** Strength for its own sake destroys: Shin goes mad, Dōgen sacrifices his sons to an ideal, Sōhaku feeds on others' lives. Strength used to protect is redeemed. Gaffney sums up the early theme as strength needing a reason beyond looking cool, while doubting that the series goes deep.

**Characters as arguments.** Souichiro argues for loyalty and protection. Mitsuomi argues that one strong man can end a cycle of hatred if he is ruthless enough. Dōgen argues for the perfect warrior at any cost. Bunshichi, the strongest of all, argues by refusing to fight. Maya argues for leading others through strength and suffers for her pride. Chiaki, who cannot fight, sees the school's culture most clearly and blames its ego for everyone's misery (Wikipedia, characters).

**Symbols and motifs.** The dragon eyes (seeing past and future); the Reiki, a sword that swallows the user's will; Dragon Gates of ki; Maya's childlike small form to conserve ki; the twelve founding houses; Osaka Castle in flames; Amaterasu and Susano-o.

**Traditions it echoes.** The delinquent school manga, Fist of the North Star (a childhood favorite of the creator), martial-arts tournament stories, Japanese myth (Amaterasu and Susano-o's quarrel) and Sengoku history. Its Buddhist title points to a teaching about ego that the story uses more as irony than doctrine.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right is the world's law and is finally questioned. Hedonism and fan service are celebrated. Bushido appears in honorable duelists. Taoist and Shinto ideas supply the power system and the finale. Buddhist ideas give the title and the cursed sword. Traditionalism is shown as a trap that the young must escape.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-26) are the school war and the assault on Chiaki. Volumes 5-8 (ch. 27-51) are the past arc of Shin, Maya and Mitsuomi. Volumes 9-14 (ch. 52-87) bring the founding families, F and Sōhaku. Volumes 15-18 (ch. 88-111) include the feudal-era flashback. Volumes 19-22 (ch. 112-136) are the tournament's end and the gods' battle; Santos (ANN) found the late story stalled and confusing.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Souichiro came to Toudou to rule it. Why does he stop wanting that?
2. Bunshichi is the strongest character and refuses to fight. What does that say about strength?
3. The title means "I alone am honored." Which character lives like that, and how does it end for them?
4. The CMX edition cut the sexual content, and fans protested. What do you think the content adds or takes away?
5. Chiaki blames Maya's pride for everyone's suffering. Is she right?
