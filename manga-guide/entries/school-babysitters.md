**Premise.** After their parents die in a plane crash, Ryuichi Kashima (finishing middle school; he starts high school in vol. 2, per ciatr) and his little brother Kotaro, a preschooler, are taken in by Yoko Morinomiya, chairwoman of Morinomiya Academy, who lost her own son and daughter-in-law in the same crash. In return for a home and free tuition, Ryuichi must help run the academy's daycare for teachers' children (Wikipedia). The club grows to include his stoic friend Hayato Kamitani, whose toddler brother Taka is in the daycare, under the sleepy caretaker Usaida. Episodic chapters follow the toddlers (Kotaro, Taka, the twins Takuma and Kazuma, Kirin and baby Midori), their working parents, and Ryuichi's classmates, including Maria Inomata and Yuki Ushimaru, both of whom like him without his noticing (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). Later volumes bring a love letter (vol. 6), cross-dressing mix-ups (vol. 10; ch. 43), career thoughts as the older students look ahead (vol. 21, 27) and the toddlers' small adventures (vol. 28) (ciatr).

## Life message

**Those we lose do not come back, but no one has to be left alone: love grows by caring for others, and a family can be built from people who choose to look after each other.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you go on after the people who held your world together are suddenly gone? Ryuichi and Kotaro have lost their parents. The series asks what a home is, and whether one can be made again.

**The answer the story gives.** A home is made by caring for others, not only by being cared for. The chairwoman gives the boys a house, but it is Ryuichi's work with the toddlers, and the friendships that grow from it, that make it a home; by vol. 4 he realizes the Morinomiya house has become his (ciatr).

**Characters as arguments.** Ryuichi argues for responsibility worn lightly and kindly. The chairwoman argues that strictness can be a form of love. Usaida, who seems lazy, shows that steady presence matters more than effort on display. Hayato and Taka show love that is never said aloud. Inomata, the prickly study-obsessed girl, softens through the children. The absent fathers show that being there is part of loving.

**Symbols and motifs.** The daycare room as the heart of the school; the brothers' hands held; a chick that hatches and imprints on Kotaro (vol. 2); the late son's study, reopened to read to Kotaro (vol. 4); the chairwoman's big hair, which Kotaro names.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese iyashikei story and the found-family drama; Hari Tokeino's own Me & My Brothers. For religious readers, the care of the orphan that Jewish, Christian and Muslim scripture command.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and Confucian ethics are celebrated without argument: the good life is shared and rooted in family duty. Virtue is formed by daily practice. Individual ambition appears late and gently, as the older students consider their futures.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-6 (to ch. 34, the anime's range): grief, the club's founding, the toddlers and their parents. Vol. 7-20: episodic school life, crushes, the twins' quarrels and small adventures. Vol. 21-28: the older students think about careers; the toddlers grow (ciatr).

**Discussion questions.**
1. How does Ryuichi deal with losing his parents? What helps him most?
2. The chairwoman seems strict. How does she show love?
3. What does Kotaro do when he is sad, and why?
4. Who in your life looks after others the way Ryuichi does?
5. Some readers find Yagi's nosebleed joke strange. Why might it bother people?
