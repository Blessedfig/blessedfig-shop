**Premise.** In an alternate, steampunk 19th-century France, vampires and humans live in an uneasy peace after a war. Noé Archiviste, a young vampire sent to Paris by his teacher, meets Vanitas, a human who calls himself a "vampire doctor." Vanitas carries the Book of Vanitas, a grimoire made by the "Vampire of the Blue Moon," which can restore vampires whose true names have been corrupted (curse-bearers, who lose control and attack). The two form an uneasy partnership. They cross paths with Jeanne, the "Hellfire Witch" and executioner who guards the young noble Luca; Dominique de Sade, Noé's childhood friend; the Church's vampire-hunting Chasseurs and their elite Paladins; and Doctor Moreau, who experimented on children, including Vanitas. Mochizuki said she wanted the story to explore identities a person can lose and recover (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life faces its own shortness honestly, refuses to treat anyone, human or vampire, as a thing, and lets itself be loved even when it does not feel deserving. Hatred, revenge and purity crusades on either side destroy the people they claim to protect.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Jun Mochizuki was born December 22, 1980, in Kanagawa Prefecture. She was not accepted to art school and submitted her work to Square Enix; her debut was a Pandora Hearts one-shot. She has named Hiromu Arakawa (Fullmetal Alchemist) as her main inspiration and the reason she chose Square Enix (Wikipedia, opened).
- On-record statements about Vanitas (via Wikipedia): she wanted to explore identities a person can lose and recover, which she considered more central here than in Pandora Hearts; she called Vanitas "a person who hates himself"; the name comes from the vanitas art genre of skulls and mortality symbols; Vanitas was first planned as the vampire and Noé as a human Watson, until her editor called it generic; the Paladins are based on the Charlemagne legend; influences include Interview with the Vampire, Holmes and Watson, Mont Saint-Michel and her first trip to Paris.
- No public statement on religion, politics or private life was found. A French Manga News interview exists (found, not opened). Hiatuses in 2022-23 and 2024 were reported; the 2024 break was reported as for health reasons (Sportskeeda headline, not opened).
- Publisher: Square Enix (Monthly Gangan Joker); English: Yen Press (Hachette Book Group and Kadokawa joint venture, from general knowledge; to verify), plus Square Enix's Manga UP!.
- No reputable reports of personal controversy involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a self worth when it can be stolen, corrupted or cut short? Vampires in this world can lose their true names and become monsters against their will. Children in Moreau's lab are given numbers instead of names. Vanitas wears an hourglass and hates himself. Mochizuki says the story is about identities a person can lose and recover (Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** So far, the answer is relational. The Book of Vanitas can restore a name, but it cannot make a person whole. What restores Vanitas is being known: Noé reads his past through his blood (vol. 9), and Vanitas apologizes and calls him a friend (vol. 10). Jeanne's love unsettles him because he does not think anyone could love him (TV Tropes). The story suggests a self is recovered when someone else refuses to treat you as a thing. Because the series is ongoing and its opening narration looks back on events (Anime Feminist mentions a "flashforward"), the final answer may be darker.

**Characters as arguments.** Noé is the argument for honesty and trust. He believes people can be good and keeps acting on it. Vanitas is the argument that mercy can come from a broken motive; he says he heals vampires out of revenge (TV Tropes). Moreau is the argument against knowledge without conscience: he experiments on children and wants to become what he studies. Astolfo is the argument of grief turned into crusade: a vampire killed his family, so he kills vampires and anyone in his way (TV Tropes). Jean-Jacques is the argument for love as self-gift: he gave up his humanity to protect Chloé (Wikipedia). Jeanne embodies duty tested by friendship when she cannot kill Chloé.

**Symbols and motifs.** The vanitas still life, with its skulls and hourglasses, reminds viewers that life is brief (Wikipedia). Vanitas wears an hourglass earring. True names stand for identity, and their corruption for losing oneself. Blood is both food and intimacy; drinking it without consent is a crime (TV Tropes). Clockwork, airships and the Book's gears suggest a world that runs like a machine, which people break.

**Traditions it echoes.** European Gothic and vampire fiction (Interview with the Vampire is a named influence), the Holmes and Watson partnership, Charlemagne romances (the Paladins), French history and legend (the Beast of Gévaudan, the Marquis de Sade's name), and the Christian-era memento mori.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential self-making is the core. Religious faith appears through institutions that the story mostly criticizes and individuals it pities. Transhumanist ambition lives in Moreau and is condemned. Authority of every kind is distrusted. Feminist readers find active women and a contested romance; secular progressive and some religious readers both object to the forced kiss, from different starting points.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 are a buddy mystery in Paris with Gothic comedy. The Gévaudan arc (vol. 5-8) brings the Church's history, Chloé's suicide wish and Jeanne's crisis. Volumes 9-10 turn inward to the Moreau lab, Mikhail and Vanitas's past, and end with an apology and friendship. Volume 11 onward is unsummarized in sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Vanitas says he helps vampires out of revenge. Does a good act count if the reason is bad?
2. The story shows some Church members as cruel and others as wounded. How should we judge an institution by its worst members?
3. Many readers loved Vanitas and Jeanne as a couple, but CBR called the first kiss an assault. Should a love story be allowed to start that way?
4. Moreau gave children numbers instead of names. Why do names matter so much in this story?
5. Vanitas hates himself. What helps him change, and what helps people change in real life?
