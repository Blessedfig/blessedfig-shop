**Premise.** In an alternate, steampunk 19th-century France, vampires and humans live in an uneasy peace after a war. Noé Archiviste, a young vampire sent to Paris by his teacher, meets Vanitas, a human who calls himself a "vampire doctor." Vanitas carries the Book of Vanitas, a grimoire made by the "Vampire of the Blue Moon," which can restore vampires whose true names have been corrupted (curse-bearers, or malnomen). The two form an uneasy partnership. They cross paths with Jeanne, the "Hellfire Witch" and executioner who guards the young noble Luca; Dominique de Sade, Noé's childhood friend; the Church's vampire-hunting Chasseurs and their elite Paladins; Doctor Moreau, who experimented on children, including Vanitas; and Mikhail, Vanitas's adopted younger brother from the lab. AniList gives Vanitas's age as 18 and Noé's as 19. Mochizuki said she wanted the story to explore identities a person can lose and recover (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life faces its own shortness honestly, refuses to treat anyone, human or vampire, as a thing, and lets itself be loved even when it does not feel deserving. Hatred, revenge and purity crusades on either side destroy the people they claim to protect.**

|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Two species compete for the same city. Vampires feed on humans, but blood-drinking is also intimate, and drinking without consent is a crime (Noé says so when Chloé drinks from him, Mémoire 27, fan wiki). Kin ties drive several arcs: Louis is beheaded as a curse-bearer (Mémoire 8); Chloé's family turns on her (Mémoire 37); Mikhail's mother abuses him (Mémoire 47-48). | Pragmatic | Vanitas cures with a tool, not with goodwill: he says his motive is revenge on the Vampire of the Blue Moon (TV Tropes). Hostages move the plot: Vanitas uses Luca as a bluff hostage against Jeanne (Mémoire 4), and Mikhail holds Dominique atop a Ferris wheel (Mémoire 47-53). |
| Religious | The Catholic Church appears as an institution with armed orders. In the fan wiki's summary of the story's world, after the "Babel Incident" the Church declared vampires "an abomination before God" and taught that they were "beyond the reach of God's love"; it formed the Chasseurs, commissioned Moreau and supplied him with child test subjects until he began to idolize vampires. Mikhail was taken by an "anti-Vampire extremist faction" inside the Church (fan wiki). The Church is also tied to a massacre at Gévaudan (TV Tropes). Yet sincere believers are drawn sympathetically: Roland weeps and prays for God's mercy on the abused child Mikhail (Mémoire 47), and But Why Tho says Roland only "may come off as a religious fanatic." Beneath the Tangles, a Christian anime site, reads the malnomen curse as a parallel to "the nature of sin" and notes Vanitas calls Noé a "child of the Ark." The title refers to the vanitas still-life genre, a memento mori tradition (Wikipedia). |
| Socio-cultural | Belle Epoque Paris (the 1889 Exposition Universelle is a setting, Mémoire 47), aristocratic vampire houses, salons, a masked ball, a vampire queen and senate. Prejudice runs both ways; in the "Miel" arc, Paladins beat a dhampir informant in the street (Mémoire 63). Mochizuki drew on Interview with the Vampire, Holmes and Watson, Mont Saint-Michel and her first visit to Paris (Wikipedia). Anime Feminist notes Noé is "a man of color" in a European setting. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — curse-bearers are restored and Vanitas and Noé become true friends, but the story is heavy with self-hatred, abused children and a guardian revealed as a manipulator, and its narrated frame hints the partnership will end (Anime Feminist mentions a "flashforward"; its content is from general knowledge; to verify).
Hope source: F — what pulls Vanitas out of his self-hatred is not his grimoire but Noé's friendship and Jeanne's love: Jeanne kisses his cheek and he blushes (Mémoire 42), and after their fight Noé and Vanitas reconcile on the rooftop (Mémoire 55.5-56; fan wiki, Wikipedia).

**Where traditions disagree.** The dividing lines are the portrayal of the Church as an armed institution with an extremist doctrine (weighs most for Catholic, Orthodox and Conservative / traditional frames; read as fair critique by Mainline, Reform Jewish, Anabaptist, secular and Satanist frames), eroticized blood-drinking (a double concern for Jewish, Muslim and Jehovah's Witness frames, where consuming blood is itself forbidden), occult devices (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness) and the forced kiss (secular progressive and Satanist frames, on consent). Wiccan / Pagan readers find a sympathetic picture of witches and witch hunts.

## Creator and publisher context

- Jun Mochizuki was born December 22, 1980, in Kanagawa Prefecture. She was not accepted to art school and submitted her work to Square Enix; her debut was a Pandora Hearts one-shot. She has named Hiromu Arakawa (Fullmetal Alchemist) as her main inspiration (Wikipedia).
- On-record statements about Vanitas (via Wikipedia): she wanted to explore identities a person can lose and recover; she called Vanitas "a person who hates himself"; the name comes from the vanitas art genre; Vanitas was first planned as the vampire and Noé as a human Watson until her editor called it generic; the Paladins are based on the Charlemagne legend; influences include Interview with the Vampire, Holmes and Watson, Mont Saint-Michel and her first trip to Paris.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a self worth when it can be stolen, corrupted or cut short? Vampires in this world can lose their true names and become monsters against their will. Children in Moreau's lab are given numbers instead of names; Vanitas tells the newly arrived Mikhail that "Mikhail" is no longer his name, he is "No. 71" (Mémoire 48, fan wiki). Vanitas wears an hourglass and hates himself. Mochizuki says the story is about identities a person can lose and recover (Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** So far, the answer is relational. The Book of Vanitas can restore a name, but it cannot make a person whole. What restores Vanitas is being known: Noé learns his past through Mikhail's blood (Mémoire 47-51), and after a fight that nearly kills them both, the two reconcile (Mémoire 55.5-56). Jeanne's love unsettles him because he does not think anyone could love him (TV Tropes). The story suggests a self is recovered when someone else refuses to treat you as a thing. Because the series is ongoing and its opening narration looks back on events (Anime Feminist mentions a "flashforward"), the final answer may be darker.

**Characters as arguments.** Noé is the argument for honesty and trust. Vanitas is the argument that mercy can come from a broken motive; he says he heals vampires out of revenge (TV Tropes), yet he volunteered for Moreau's experiment to spare Mikhail (fan wiki). Moreau is the argument against knowledge without conscience. Astolfo is the argument of grief turned into crusade: as a small child he watched vampires kill his sister, and at fifteen he fights on "lost to madness" (Mémoire 42). Mikhail is the argument that love learned from abuse turns possessive: "I'm the only one who will" stay with you (Mémoire 52). Roland is the argument that faith can weep for a child instead of using him (Mémoire 47). Jean-Jacques is the argument for love as self-gift.

**Symbols and motifs.** The vanitas still life, with its skulls and hourglasses, reminds viewers that life is brief (Wikipedia). True names stand for identity, and their corruption for losing oneself; Beneath the Tangles reads the curse as a picture of sin. Blood is food, intimacy and memory: drinking it can read a person's past. Sleeping Beauty (Mémoire 47) recasts Jeanne as the rescuer. Astérisque flowers bloom where the world's formula was broken (Mémoire 42). The galette des rois (Mémoire 56) marks the one happy family the boys had.

**Traditions it echoes.** European Gothic and vampire fiction (Interview with the Vampire is a named influence), the Holmes and Watson partnership, Charlemagne romances (the Paladins), French history and legend (the Beast of Gévaudan, the Marquis de Sade, the Comte de Saint-Germain, the 1889 Exposition), fairy tales, and the Christian-era memento mori.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential self-making is the core. Religious faith appears through an institution the story criticizes and individuals it treats with respect. Transhumanist ambition lives in Moreau and is condemned. Authority of every kind is distrusted, including the heroes' own guardian. Feminist readers find active women and a contested romance; secular progressive and some religious readers both object to the forced kiss, from different starting points.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (Mémoire 1-10) are a buddy mystery in Paris with Gothic comedy. The Moreau arc (Mémoire 12-21, vols. 3-4) brings in the lab. The Gévaudan arc (Mémoire 22-46, vols. 5-8) brings Chloé's wish to die, Astolfo's crusade and Jeanne's love. Volumes 9-10 (Mémoire 47-56) turn inward to Mikhail, the lab and Vanitas's past, ending in reconciliation. Volume 11 and the "Miel" arc (Mémoire 57-69.5) widen to a drug epidemic in Paris, dhampirs, the Comte de Saint-Germain and a young Archiviste girl he pursues (Yen Press vol. 11).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Vanitas says he helps vampires out of revenge, yet he took Mikhail's place in the lab. Which tells us more about him?
2. The story shows some Church members as cruel and others, like Roland, as kind. How should we judge an institution by its worst and best members?
3. Many readers loved Vanitas and Jeanne as a couple, but CBR called their first kiss an assault. Should a love story be allowed to start that way?
4. Moreau gave children numbers instead of names. Why do names matter so much in this story?
5. Mikhail's love for his brother became possessive. How can love learned in a harmful home be healed?
