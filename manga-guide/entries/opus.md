**Premise.** Manga artist Chikara Nagai is racing a deadline to finish his series *Resonance*, in which the telepathic investigator Satoko Miura fights the Masque, leader of a brainwashing cult called the Nameless Faith, and the psychic Lin is meant to die defeating him. Lin steals the two-page spread of his own death, and Nagai is pulled into his manga. Inside, the characters treat their world as real and are unimpressed to meet their maker; some call him God, which he rejects (TV Tropes; World Comic Book Review). Satoko is shot by her brainwashed police chief, Nagai rescues her into the real world, and the two travel back through earlier volumes of *Resonance* to the serial-killer case where the Masque began. The Masque seizes control of the story and its world dissolves; the serialized story ends with the heroes falling. Kon's unfinished last chapter shows Kon himself learning of the magazine's cancellation and Nagai stepping out of the page to scold him for leaving the story unfinished (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A maker is answerable for what he does to the people he makes: suffering added just for effect is wrong, creatures have a dignity of their own, and the one who seeks total control, even in the name of freedom from self, is the true enemy.**

| Pragmatic | Problems are solved by learning the rules of a drawn world: places Nagai drew himself are solid, while backgrounds left to assistants are hazy (World Comic Book Review); a lost pen and a redrawn heroine fix what can be fixed. |
| Religious | The creator-creature relation is the core: characters call their author God, one curses him for her suffering, and he insists he is only a man (TV Tropes). | Socio-cultural | A satire of the weekly manga business (deadlines, editors pushing for shocking endings) and a dry run for Kon's films about fiction bleeding into reality (Carl Horn, Dark Horse; Ganriki). The real magazine's cancellation becomes part of the plot. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the creator learns to care for his characters and joins them against the would-be god, but the story breaks off with everyone falling, and the found last chapter turns to Kon's own unfinished work.
Hope source: P — protecting others: the way through is Nagai taking responsibility for the people he made and fighting to save Satoko's world; knowledge and discovery (K) is the close second, as both sides learn what their worlds really are.

## Creator and publisher context

- Satoshi Kon (1963-2010; birth year from general knowledge, to verify) was a manga artist before becoming a film director; he worked as an art assistant on *AKIRA* and made *Opus* in 1995-96 while preparing *Perfect Blue* (Carl Horn, Dark Horse). He died of cancer in 2010 at 46 (Dark Horse; World Comic Book Review).
- On-record view: Kon later called *Opus* a failure (Wikipedia, citing Andrew Osmond's *Satoshi Kon: The Illusionist*, 2009). In the found final chapter he draws himself told of the cancellation, lamenting he could have finished in three chapters (Wikipedia).
- His widow, Kyōko Kon, announced the French, Italian and Korean editions in 2012 (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Gakken (*Comic Guys*), then Tokuma Shoten; Dark Horse in English, edited by Carl Horn, rated 16+.
- No statements by Kon on religion or politics were found in sources opened. No controversies found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a maker owe the people he makes? Nagai plans to kill Lin because it will be a shocking ending; Lin, who does not want to die, steals the page. Once inside his own manga, Nagai meets people with histories, fears and opinions, and they are not impressed by their god (Ganriki; World Comic Book Review).

**The answer the story gives.** A maker is answerable. Nagai admits he added suffering for drama, and his characters find that repellent (World Comic Book Review). He refuses the title God, rescues Satoko, redraws her when she is erased, and fights beside his characters against the Masque. The story's last turn makes the same point about Kon himself: in the found chapter, Nagai steps out to scold his own author for leaving him unfinished (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Nagai argues for the author's freedom, then learns its limits. Satoko argues for the dignity of the made, angry at a "god" who let her world be cruel (TV Tropes), then surprised to find the real world harsher than hers. Lin argues for the right to live. The Masque argues for total control: his cult promises freedom from individuality, and his goal is to become god (TV Tropes YMMV), the dark mirror of an author who has that power and is learning not to abuse it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The stolen two-page spread of Lin's death. Cracks between pages. Backgrounds that turn to stage sets where an assistant drew them. The lost pen that lets Nagai redraw Satoko. The mask, which Carl Horn notes reappears in *Perfect Blue*. The fall at the end, which leaves everyone suspended.

**Traditions it echoes.** Metafiction from Grant Morrison's *Animal Man* (World Comic Book Review) to the a-ha "Take On Me" video and *Stranger Than Fiction* (MangaBlog, Dacey). For Christian readers it echoes Dorothy L. Sayers' *The Mind of the Maker* and the problem of evil; for Jewish readers, the tradition of arguing with God (Abraham, Job); for Buddhist readers, the teaching that the self and its world are constructed.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Postmodernism and existentialism are explored, not preached: the story enjoys its puzzles. Duty ethics wins the moral argument (people are not tools for effect). Collectivism and might-makes-right are embodied in the cult leader and condemned. Secular humanism appears in a god who turns out to be a tired human, though the story also lets that human grow into his responsibility.

**Shifts across the series.** The first half sets up the trap: Nagai's deadline, his fall into *Resonance*, Satoko's shooting and rescue into the real world. The second half travels back to the first case, where the young Satoko was kidnapped by a serial killer, and ends with the Masque's victory and the world's dissolution. The found chapter steps out of the story entirely.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nagai wanted to kill Lin because it would be a surprising ending. Is that a good reason?
2. Satoko is angry at her "god." Is she right to be? What changes when she meets him?
3. The Masque's cult promises freedom from being yourself. Why might that appeal to someone, and why is it dangerous?
4. Do authors owe anything to their characters? Do we owe anything to the people in our own stories?
5. The story never finished. Does that change what it means?
