**Premise.** Junior high student Mika Kashima has lost her best friend Mitsuha and wants to follow her into death. She steps in front of a truck (ch. 1) and is pulled clear by Hibino, a man who cannot die (Otaku USA). Hibino travels with Hani, a youthful, androgynous god near the end of their life (gods in this world can die when people stop believing in them), who is making a last journey to Yomi, the land of the dead in Japanese myth, whose entrance is said to be in Shimane. Hibino hopes the trip will show him how to end his own endless life; Mika joins them hoping to meet Mitsuha again. The three ride a motorbike west across Japan, visiting shrines where Hani says goodbye to other gods, and meet people facing death in their own ways: an elderly woman in Hakone whose meeting with them "ends in tragedy" (vol. 2 blurb), a folklore researcher studying immortality to save a gravely ill friend (vol. 2), and an old man at a temple who knew Hibino when they were young soldiers (vol. 3, Anika entre libros). In volume 3 Hani's body fails, Hibino reveals a long shared history with Hani, Hani enters Yomi, and Mika reaches its entrance and meets Mitsuha (Kodansha Japan vol. 3). At the very end Mika chooses to live, though the story gives no stated reason (AniList user review, spoiler; to verify in print).

## Life message

**Grief and the wish to die are met with patience and company rather than a lecture; on a last journey with others who are facing death, a grieving girl meets her lost friend at the edge of the land of the dead and chooses to go on living, carrying her memory.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you owe the dead, and what do you owe the living? Mika believes the only faithful thing is to follow Mitsuha. Hibino, who has outlived everyone, carries a notebook of people he has lost (Otaku USA). Hani, a god, is fading because fewer people remember. The book asks whether remembering the dead requires dying with them.

**The answer the story gives.** Mika reaches the entrance to Yomi, meets Mitsuha, and turns back to life (vol. 3; AniList review). The book refuses to give her a speech or a reason; one reviewer was frustrated by this, others found it true to grief (MAL; Anika entre libros). The implied answer is that the dead are carried by the living, and that company on the road matters more than an argument.

**Characters as arguments.** Mika argues for love that will not let go. Hibino argues from the other side: endless life is its own sorrow, and he wants an ending. Hani shows a death accepted with grace. The people met on the road (the woman in Hakone, the folklore researcher seeking immortality for a dying friend, the old man who knew Hibino as a soldier) each show another way of facing death, from acceptance to desperation (vol. 2-3 blurbs).

**Symbols and motifs.** The motorbike and the road west, toward the sunset and the land of the dead. Hibino's notebook of the lost. Shrines visited in farewell. The god who fades when forgotten, a mirror for memory itself. The title word, hiraeth, a longing for a home that cannot be returned to (from general knowledge; to verify).

**Traditions it echoes.** Izanagi's descent to Yomi and his return (Kojiki); Orpheus; the road story; the Katha Upanishad's young Nachiketa at the door of death; the Buddhist story of Kisa Gotami; for Christian readers, Elijah asking to die and being fed and accompanied (1 Kings 19). It also belongs with Kamatani's own Our Dreams at Dusk, which Gaffney says its readers should seek out.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism frames each traveler's choice. Shinto myth is honored, not explained away. Nihilism appears as a visual void and is left behind. The story is humanist in practice (people save Mika) inside a world where gods are real.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (likely ch. 1-6): the attempt, the meeting, the setting out. Vol. 2 (likely ch. 7-12): Hakone and the researcher, Mika learning to face death. Vol. 3 (likely ch. 13-17): Hani fails, Hibino's history, Yomi and the choice. Chapter split estimated from 17 chapters in three volumes (to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mika wants to see Mitsuha again. Is that the same as wanting to die? What else could it mean?
2. Hibino cannot die and wishes he could. What does his story say about why life has an end?
3. Hani fades when people forget. Who do you keep alive by remembering?
4. The book never says why Mika chooses to live. Why might the author leave it unsaid?
5. If a friend told you they wanted to die, what would you do first? (Parents: 988 in the US; findahelpline.com elsewhere.)
