**Premise.** Hiyori Iki, a middle-school girl, pushes a stranger out of the path of a bus. The stranger is Yato, a minor, shrineless god who takes any job for a five-yen offering. After the accident Hiyori's soul keeps slipping out of her body, and she seeks Yato's help to fix it. Yato's weapon is a shinki (Regalia): the spirit of a dead person whom a god names and turns into a tool. His new shinki is Yukine, a boy who died at about 14. The world of the gods (Takamagahara, the "Far Shore") lives beside ours. Gods exist only as long as humans remember them; ayakashi (phantoms) feed on human despair. Slowly the story reveals that Yato was once a god of calamity, made and used as an assassin by a human sorcerer he calls "Father," and his long struggle is to become a god who brings good fortune instead.

## Life message

**A good life is one you choose, not one you were made for: you can turn from a violent past, take responsibility for the people bound to you, and keep faith with the ties (en) that make you real to others.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Gods live only while humans remember them; this is a story about social bonds as survival. Yato's fear of being forgotten is a fear of social death. Kin are replaced by chosen households: Yato, Yukine and Hiyori; Bishamon's large "family" of shinki. Father, who made Yato, is a parent who exploits his offspring, and the story sides with the offspring breaking free. Romance (Yato and Hiyori) is slow and chaste and is resolved only after a ten-year gap (ch. 109). |
| Pragmatic | Small, steady jobs for five yen build a reputation and worshippers. A god's strength depends on his shinki's conduct: Yukine's thefts physically "blight" Yato until he nearly dies (v3), so responsibility runs both ways. Institutions are shown as flawed: Heaven judges by quick, harsh verdicts (TV Tropes). Results come from alliances (Kofuku, Daikoku, Bishamon, Kazuma) more than from raw power. |
| Religious | The world is openly Shinto and folk-Buddhist in its imagery: kami, five-yen offerings, a home-made shrine (ch. 26), ablution as purification of sin, the underworld Yomi and Izanami (ch. 30-35), Tenjin (the deified Sugawara no Michizane), Ebisu, Bishamonten and Amaterasu as ruler of Heaven. The gods are real and the series treats them with both affection and comedy (Beneath the Tangles: the hero god is mocked in ways that would be unthinkable for Christ in America). Christian bloggers read Yukine's blight and repentance as a picture of sin and atonement (Mage in a Barrel; Beneath the Tangles). |
| Socio-cultural | The god-shinki bond mirrors Japanese master-retainer and household (ie) loyalty, with names given by the master. The series questions authority: Heaven convicts Yato of treason (ch. 70-72) and is shown as harsh, and an abusive father figure is the main villain. Gender roles are partly reversed: a female war god heads her household, a muscular male shinki does the housework (Flis 2018). Death is common: every shinki is a dead person, and many died young or by violence. |

**Where traditions disagree.** The main dividing line is the premise: real gods of other religions as heroes and the spirits of the dead bound to serve them. Pentecostal and Jehovah's Witness frames grade it lowest; Orthodox Christian, Baptist, Anabaptist, Adventist, Christian Science, Orthodox Jewish and Muslim frames see real conflict. Several Christian bloggers, though, find strong echoes of sin, atonement and grace. Shinto, Hindu, Buddhist, Pagan and UU frames find the closest fit, with Shinto families possibly noting the comic gods and the severe Amaterasu. Secular frames weigh the gender reversals against the fan service.

## Creator and publisher context

- Adachitoka is the joint pen name of two women, Adachi (born in Murayama, Yamagata) and Tokashiki (born in Naha). Adachi draws characters and foreground; Tokashiki draws backgrounds. Before Noragami they drew *Alive: The Final Evolution* (2003-2010, story by Tadashi Kawashima). Noragami grew from their own one-shot. In 2020 they drew a manga for an anti-piracy campaign, and in 2026 a new series, *Yadoya no Oinu-sama*, was announced (Wikipedia, Adachitoka, opened).
- On-record statements: Kodansha USA posted a translated interview manga with Adachitoka by Kazushige Miyazaki (Monthly Shonen Magazine, October 2015); the text page says "very little else is known" about the creators, and the interview's content was not readable as text (opened). A 2015 New Year message from the duo was found but not opened. No public statement of intended themes, religion or politics was found. The duo keep a low profile. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Mythology: ANN's feature (Gabriella Ekens) notes that Yato and Kofuku are invented for the series, while Ebisu, Bishamonten, Tenjin and Izanami come from Shinto, Buddhist and folk sources, with liberties.
- Publisher: Kodansha, a large private Japanese publisher (from general knowledge; to verify). English edition by Kodansha USA, rated 16+.
- Controversy: in December 2015 the *Noragami Aragoto* production committee recalled the second soundtrack album and delayed the first Blu-ray/DVD after the track "Push Buttons" was found to sample the adhan (the Islamic call to prayer). The committee said it "had not confirmed the meaning of these sounds" and apologized (ANN, opened). This concerns the anime's music, not the manga or its creators.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you become something other than what you were made to be? Yato was created to be a god of calamity, a killer for a human sorcerer. Yukine is a dead boy whose short life was taken by his father. Both carry pasts they did not choose. The story asks whether a being's origin decides its nature, and what it costs to change.

**The answer the story gives.** Change is possible but costly, and it needs other people. Yato wants to be a god who brings good fortune. He works for five-yen offerings, which the ANN feature ties to the pun "go-en," respect or ties. His change only holds because Hiyori and Yukine refuse to let go of him. The story's metaphysics makes this literal: a god exists only while someone remembers him. In the end Yato kills Father, nearly vanishes and survives; Hiyori forgets him and then remembers his name years later (ch. 108-109, per Fiction Horizon and TV Tropes). The ending argues that bonds outlast even forgetting.

**Characters as arguments.** Yato is the argument that origin is not destiny. Father is the opposing argument: what he made is his to use. Yukine is the argument about responsibility. His thefts and anger "blight" Yato's body until Yato nearly dies, and only confession and purification restore them both (v3). Bishamon is the argument about vengeance: she hunts Yato for killing her shinki and lets go when she learns he did it at Kazuma's plea to save her. Ebisu argues that the dangerous dead can be tamed for good; Heaven executes him for trying (ch. 35). Hiyori argues for faithfulness, a single believer who builds a god a shrine with her own hands (ch. 26).

**Symbols and motifs.** The five-yen coin stands for a tie between worshipper and god. Names are power: a god names a shinki, and learning a shinki's living name can corrupt it. The blight is a visible stain of sin. Ablution is cleansing. Hiyori's slipping soul and her tail mark a person caught between worlds. Yato's tracksuit is the comic picture of a god without dignity.

**Traditions it echoes.** Shinto ideas of kami, ties, impurity and purification. Folk Buddhism through Bishamonten and rebirth. Japanese myth: Izanami and Yomi, Ebisu as the cast-off Hiruko, Tenjin as an appeased angry spirit (ANN feature). The household and master-retainer ethic. Christian bloggers have read it through sin and atonement: Mage in a Barrel writes, "Like Christ, Yato very literally takes on Yukine's sins in the form of the blight."

**How each lens is portrayed.** The Shinto world is shown as real and loved, with gods who can be petty, poor or ridiculous. Existential choice is affirmed in Yato. Authoritarian order is questioned through Heaven's quick sentences. Might-makes-right lives in Father and loses. Feminist readers find gender reversals (Flis 2018) alongside fan service. Christian readers find parallels the text does not intend.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes (ch. 1-11) are episodic comedy with a sharp moral turn in Yukine's sins and purification. The Bishamon and Kugaha arc (ch. 12-24) adds war and vendetta. The Ebisu arc (ch. 28-39) moves into myth, the underworld and execution. The middle (ch. 40-72) reveals Yato's past and pits him against Heaven. The late arcs (ch. 73-109) turn darker, with Yukine's corruption and the war against Father, and end with a time skip and reunion.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yato was made to destroy. Can someone become good if they were raised to do harm?
2. Yukine's wrongs hurt Yato's body. How do our choices affect people tied to us?
3. Gods in this story exist only while people remember them. What does it mean to be remembered?
4. Heaven executes Ebisu for a dangerous experiment. Was it justice? Who should decide?
5. The story treats gods with jokes as well as respect. How does your family's tradition think about humor and sacred things?
