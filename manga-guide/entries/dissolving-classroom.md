**Premise.** Yuuma Azawa, a polite, handsome second-year high-school student, transfers into class 2-B and apologizes constantly, for everything. His younger sister Chizumi, a middle-schooler with long nails, a long tongue and a nasty grin, stalks and torments whoever crosses their path. Each chapter shows a new circle of people around the siblings (classmates, a girlfriend, apartment neighbors, a boy Chizumi likes) and ends the same way: Yuuma's apologies, which are really acts of devotion to the Devil, make people's brains melt, and his compliments disfigure women's faces. Chizumi drinks the remains. In the last chapter a television reporter who has been hunting the siblings turns out to be the Devil in disguise; he arranges a live broadcast apology, and Yuuma's apology melts everyone watching, ending with Tokyo flooded by liquefied people (Wikipedia; Otaku USA; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Showy apologies and perfect manners can hide real evil: an apology that is a performance, not true repentance, is worse than useless.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Little survival or mating drama; the horror plays on disgust (bodily dissolution, contamination) and on a predator who wears the face of a harmless, well-mannered young man (Manga Critic). |
| Pragmatic | Nothing works: kind neighbors, investigators and police all fail. The only person who survives the first chapter does so by passing out (ch. 1; Wikipedia). The book offers no strategy, only a warning about trusting appearances. |
| Religious | The Devil, drawn in a goat-headed Baphomet style, is real and is the main source of evil; Yuuma worships it through self-abasing apologies, and backstory hints include animal killings and a secret animal cemetery (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; Manga Critic). No God or good spiritual power appears. World Literature Today faults the book for naming the Christian Devil, absolute evil, as the source of its horror. Faith readers will weigh a story where evil wins outright against its sharp point about false contrition. |
| Socio-cultural | A satire of the public apology as ritual: Ito's afterword mocks televised apologies as entertainment that excuses bad behavior (The Beat; Wikipedia). World Literature Today ties it to Japan's culture of filmed celebrity apologies and reads Chizumi's primping for a crush as a comment on the sexualization of adolescent girls. |

Outlook: Bleak — the Devil and the siblings win, nearly every character melts or dies, and the final chapter ends with a mass melting across Tokyo; the bonus stories are equally dark (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: kindness (the neighbors, Keiko) is punished and the one investigator is the Devil himself; the only sliver is the melted neighbors helping a captive boy escape (ch. 4), and he is later found dead (ch. 5).

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame agrees with the book's target: an apology that is only a performance is a lie. The split is over the Devil. For most Christian, Muslim and Latter-day Saint readers a story built on worshiping a real Devil who wins, with no God, grace or rescue, fits poorly (C or D), and some (Pentecostal, Jehovah's Witness, Seventh-day Adventist) see the occult center itself as the problem. Secular, Reform Jewish, Wiccan and Unitarian Universalist readers treat the Devil as a horror device and grade the satire more warmly, though several (World Literature Today, Nothing in particular) dislike the hopelessness and the simple good-versus-evil frame.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Evil wins. The Devil and the Azawa siblings prevail in every chapter, while everyone who tries to help them or stop them melts, is maimed, or dies; the siblings themselves are implied to be engulfed by their victims' remains at the end (TV Tropes), but the Devil's plan succeeds.

## Creator and publisher context

- Junji Ito (born July 31, 1963, Nakatsugawa, Gifu) worked as a dental technician before his 1987 Kazuo Umezu Prize honorable mention launched Tomie; he cites Umezu, Hideshi Hino and H. P. Lovecraft as influences (Wikipedia, Junji Ito). His wins include Eisner Awards in 2019, 2021 (two) and 2022 and an Inkpot Award (2023) (Wikipedia); Otaku USA reported his Eisner Hall of Fame induction in 2025 (headline seen on Otaku USA).
- The title nods to Umezu's The Drifting Classroom, and the book uses Umezu's shock and gross-out methods (The Manga Critic). Wikipedia notes it departs from Ito's usual Lovecraftian horror and quotes Ito, from interviews, on mood and atmosphere and on rumor as a spreader of horror.
- On-record intent: Ito's one-page afterword presents the "actor" who played Yuuma and jokes about the trend of public apologies, inviting readers to make their own (Wikipedia); The Beat reads it as Ito calling televised apologies entertainment that excuses misconduct.
- No statements by Ito on religion in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (no interview specific to this book was opened).
- Publishers: Akita Shoten (Comic Motto!, which closed with the final chapter); Vertical (2017, rated Mature per ICv2); Kodansha USA Collector's Edition (2022, 16+).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is an apology for? Yuuma says sorry for everything, all the time, so the word stops meaning anything (The Beat). The book asks whether a show of humility can itself be a kind of pride, and whether people reward the show because it lets everyone move on.

**The answer the story gives.** An apology that is about the apologizer, not the one harmed, is a lie, and in Ito's nightmare logic it is literally deadly. Yuuma's "sorry" feeds his own pleasure and his devotion to the Devil; it never repairs anything. The final broadcast apology (ch. 5), arranged by the Devil disguised as a reporter, is the satire's punch line: the public apology as mass entertainment that destroys its audience.

**Characters as arguments.** Yuuma is the polished surface: polite, handsome, apologetic, and the true monster (The Manga Critic). Chizumi is the opposite: openly rude and frightening, and in her way more honest about what the siblings are. Keiko and the neighbors argue for ordinary decency and are destroyed for it. Nao (ch. 2) is vanity fed by flattery until she is grotesque and still believes she is beautiful. Hamaoka, the reporter, is the media itself, and he is the Devil.

**Symbols and motifs.** Melting: identity and the mind dissolving under words. Bottles of labeled remains (ch. 4): victims reduced to collected objects, who still manage one act of mercy. The bow and the downcast eyes, which Ito draws so that shame looks like ecstasy (The Manga Critic). The goat-headed Devil.

**Traditions it echoes.** Kazuo Umezu's school horror, signaled by the title (The Manga Critic). Christian ideas of the Devil as deceiver and of contrition that must be sincere. Buddhist and Confucian concern with right speech and with ritual done for show. Readers of Isaiah 29:13 or Matthew 15:8 will recognize "lips without the heart."

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult is the source of evil and is condemned. Absurdism shapes the tone: death by compliment is a joke as much as a fright. Nihilism hangs over the ending, where the good are punished and nothing is explained; World Literature Today argues the named Devil actually makes the book more morally simple than Ito's other work.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-2 are self-contained school and dating horror. Ch. 3 turns to family abuse and Hell. Ch. 4 is the darkest, with a captive child. Ch. 5 widens to the media and the nation. The two bonus stories are unrelated: "The Return" is a sad love story with a gruesome image, "Children of the Earth" a short nightmare about lost children.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yuuma apologizes all the time. Why does that make people trust him? Why should it not?
2. What is the difference between saying sorry and making things right?
3. Keiko and the neighbors try to help and are hurt. Was helping still the right thing to do?
4. Why does Ito make the reporter, the one who seems to be uncovering the truth, the Devil?
5. Nao is flattered until she is grotesque. What does that say about flattery and vanity?
