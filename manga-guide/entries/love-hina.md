**Premise.** Keitaro Urashima has failed the Tokyo University (Todai) entrance exam twice and his parents stop supporting him. He goes to his grandmother Hina's inn, only to find it has become a girls-only dormitory; the residents want him gone until his aunt Haruka announces that his grandmother has made him the manager (ch. 1). He keeps studying for Todai to keep a promise he made at age five with a girl he can no longer name. The residents are Naru Narusegawa (17, top of the national mock exams, quick to punch him), Motoko Aoyama (15, a kendo swordswoman of the Shinmei school), Shinobu Maehara (13, shy, the house cook), Kaolla Su (13, a hyperactive inventor and princess of the island Molmol) and Mitsune "Kitsune" Konno (19, a hard-drinking freelancer) (Wikipedia, characters). Keitaro and Naru fail again, meet Mutsumi Otohime (v3), and finally pass with her (v8); after a broken leg and a trip abroad, Naru confesses (v10-11), Keitaro's adopted sister Kanako tries to take over the inn and him (v11-12), and Grandma Hina reveals that Naru was the promised girl. Three years later they marry at the inn (ch. 117-118) (Wikipedia, chapter list).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you owe a promise you barely remember? Keitaro has staked his life on a vow made at five, to enter Todai with a girl whose name he forgot. He keeps failing the exam and keeps trying, mostly because of the promise.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep it. Keitaro passes on the third try (v8), finds that Naru was the girl all along (ch. 115-116) and marries her (ch. 117-118). Persistence and faithfulness are rewarded, in love and in study. The comedy beneath it, though, rewards something else: the reader's enjoyment of nudity and of Keitaro's punishment.

**Characters as arguments.** Keitaro argues for meek persistence: he almost never hits back. Naru argues for pride and ambition, and her fists are the main joke; Anime Feminist reads this as normalizing abuse of a man. Motoko argues for the discipline of the sword and the fear of feeling; she must choose her own way (v13). Mutsumi argues for gentle generosity, losing a contest on purpose so Naru can have Keitaro (v10). Kanako argues for possessive love and gives it up (v12).

**Symbols and motifs.** The promise; the Todai exam results board; the hot spring, where most of the fan service and many confessions happen; Tama-chan, the flying turtle; the inn as a home that is passed down.

**Traditions it echoes.** Maison Ikkoku and Kimagure Orange Road (Ex.org, via Wikipedia), the dating-sim structure of a hero among many heroines, and Japan's exam-hell stories.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and traditional promise-keeping frame the plot; hedonism runs the comedy. Bushido is affectionate parody through Motoko. Feminist critics turn their lens on the series itself, for its sexualized teenagers and for its violence against a man.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-24) set up the house and the first failed exam. Volumes 4-8 (ch. 25-69) bring Mutsumi, the promise mystery and the pass. Volumes 9-12 (ch. 70-105) are Todai, Motoko's sister, Okinawa, Kanako and Naru's confession. Volumes 13-14 (ch. 106-118) resolve Motoko, the Molmol trip and the wedding. Manga Bookshelf finds it about five volumes too long.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Keitaro failed the exam twice before he passed. What kept him going?
2. The girls hit Keitaro for accidents. Would the jokes work if he hit them? What does that tell us?
3. Mutsumi loses on purpose so Naru can be with Keitaro. Was that kind, or unfair to herself?
4. Many jokes involve seeing people without clothes. How would it feel to be the person in the joke?
5. Is a promise made at age five still a promise?
