**Premise.** Hiroki Dan heads a homicide squad in the Tokyo Metropolitan Police: polite, admired, the department's ace, and the son of a former police commissioner (AniList; Mangapedia). He lives in a tower apartment, cycles to work, and makes pottery at a kiln on a mountain he owns. In secret he has killed more than a hundred people he judges to be beyond the law's reach: offenders who escaped conviction for lack of evidence or got light sentences (Comikey; Bleeding Cool). He fits each death to the victim's crime, photographs their faces as they die, sometimes burns bodies in his kiln, and often plays The Exorcist, his favorite film, during the killings; after each one he confesses in a church confessional (Mangapedia; Stitch notes the priests do not report him). Mangapedia says he is drawn to people's faces in despair and is working toward reaching heaven, where someone he loves waits; reviewers note his past is only hinted at. A principled junior officer, Ryō Mamiya, admires him (Mangapedia); a Spanish reviewer notes hints she may come to suspect him. Cases run two to five parts each: a man who murdered two children as a juvenile and now profits from a book about it (parts 1-3); a university club whose members drug and sexually assault women (likely parts 4-10); a reporter who hounds criminals' families (parts 11-12); a man who endangers a baby on a crowded train; a video streamer whose stunts cause a death; students who attack homeless people; a television commentator praised as a model father who is abusive (likely parts 30-38); workplace harassment at a company retreat; a teacher who preys on a student (parts 46-47); and suspected abuse of children by a single mother's boyfriend (Mangapedia; Bleeding Cool; MangaHot part titles). Dan later adopts a homeless victim's shiba dog and names it Pazuzu, after the demon in The Exorcist (Mangapedia).

## Life message

**When the law fails the victims, a hidden killer who punishes the guilty in kind feels like justice; the story invites readers to cheer him and seldom asks what that makes him, or them.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A predator who hunts predators: nearly every target preys on someone weaker (children, women, homeless men, an infant, employees). Dan's pleasure in his victims' despair (Mangapedia) is dominance stripped of any group purpose. |
| Pragmatic | Dan wins by planning: alibis, a private kiln, a detective's knowledge of evidence. Police procedure is shown failing to convict for lack of proof (Comikey blurb), so the story rewards effectiveness over legality. |
| Religious | The Japanese title promises "confessions": Dan confesses in a Catholic-style confessional after each killing, without stopping (Mangapedia; Stitch), and hopes for heaven (Mangapedia). He loves The Exorcist and names his dog after its demon. Faith readers will see confession without repentance and a man who thinks he can earn heaven by killing. |
| Socio-cultural | A catalogue of Japanese public anxieties: juvenile offenders released and profiting (a MAL reviewer says the first case is modeled on a real offender), campus sexual-assault circles, tabloid harassment, abuse on crowded trains, attacks on homeless people, "ikumen" celebrity fathers, workplace harassment (Mangapedia). Bleeding Cool calls it Japan's answer to Dexter and a fantasy of control and retribution. |

Outlook: Bleak — no one in the story is redeemed or healed; the guilty die in agony, the hero is never caught, and the series stops on hiatus without resolving Dan's past or fate.
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: its only remedy for evil is a secret killer, and his private hope of heaven is left unexamined; Mamiya's honest policing is the faint counterweight.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What should happen to people who do terrible things and escape the law? Brutal answers through Hiroki Dan, a model detective who privately does to them what they did to others.

**The answer the story gives.** It gives the satisfying answer and leaves the hard one open. Each case is built so the reader wants the punishment; then the punishment comes, in detail. A Spanish reviewer notes how the narration makes the reader almost an accomplice. Reviewers disagree on whether the series ever questions this (MAL); the sources opened show no case where Dan is wrong about guilt.

**Characters as arguments.** Dan argues for retribution without limits, and for his own right to judge. His targets are arguments for why: people who prey on children, women, the homeless and employees and are protected by law, fame or institutions. Mamiya, an honest officer who admires him, stands for lawful justice, so far without a voice against him. The priests who hear his confessions stand for mercy that cannot act (Stitch).

**Symbols and motifs.** The confessional: guilt spoken but not repented. The Polaroids: faces of despair collected. The kiln: pottery and cremation in one fire. The Exorcist and a dog named Pazuzu: evil as a beloved film. Heaven: the place Dan believes he is working toward (Mangapedia).

**Traditions it echoes.** Dexter (Bleeding Cool), Death Note's question of who may judge, the Japanese revenge drama, and the Christian practice of confession, which in Catholic teaching requires sorrow and a purpose of amendment (from general knowledge). The series borrows the form of confession without its substance.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Strength-as-right is explored and largely enjoyed; liberal due process is cast as the obstacle; Christian imagery frames the killer's private religion without judging it; utilitarian protection of future victims is implied but never argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (parts 1-10): the juvenile killer and the university club establish the formula. Volumes 2-3 (parts 11-29): harassment, a train, a streamer, homeless victims. Volumes 4-5 (parts 30-61): abusive fathers, workplace harassment, a predatory teacher and child abuse; Dan's past is hinted. The series stops on hiatus with no resolution; a sequel, DOOM, began in 2025.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do we want Dan to punish these people? What does the story do to make us want it?
2. What does the law require before someone is punished, and why?
3. Dan confesses after every killing but never stops. Is that a confession?
4. What would justice for the victims look like, if not this?
5. If Dan were wrong about someone, how would anyone know?
