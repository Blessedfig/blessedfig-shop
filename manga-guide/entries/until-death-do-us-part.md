**Premise.** Haruka Toyama is 12 and can see the most likely future. Yakuza killed her parents when they kidnapped her (TV Tropes), and corporations and gangs want her power. Escaping from a car, she picks out a blind man in a Tokyo crowd and begs him to protect her "until death do us part" (ch. 1). He is Mamoru Hijikata, a master of old sword schools who sees the world as wireframe outlines through special glasses and carries a monomolecular blade in his cane. With his technical partner Ryotaro Igawa he belongs to the Element Network, a secret, privately funded group led by the billionaire Tatsumi Daiba and made up largely of victims of terrorism and crime. It goes after criminals the law cannot reach but has a rule against killing, so its members cripple and humiliate instead (TV Tropes; ANN; Sean Gaffney). The story runs from the yakuza and the Ex-Solid company through the master criminal Wiseman, a school guarded by an assassin called "Jesus" (Yen vol. 8), and an elite unit that captures and tortures Mamoru (Yen vol. 9), to a war with Zashid Turus, the hidden ruler of two fictional states (Yen vols. 12-13). Haruka grows from a frightened child into a trained fighter and strategist who leads a team of her own (Yen vol. 12; The Fandom Post). She falls in love with Mamoru. Zashid is defeated, surrenders and hands Haruka authority over his states (ch. 213, Wikipedia). After a seven-year time skip, Mamoru and Haruka marry (ch. 214, TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A promise to protect someone, kept at any cost, can become a bond for life, and justice can punish the wicked without killing them.**

**Where traditions disagree.** All frames welcome the protector story and the condemnation of trafficking and child soldiers. Three things divide them. First, the romance: most religious frames, progressive frames and many families read a girl's love for her adult guardian, with kisses while she is a young teen, as part of the message even though marriage waits until she is grown; Hindu, Sikh and Shinto readings put more weight on the kept vow. Second, private justice: law-centered traditions (Reformed, Lutheran, Bahá'í, Conservative / traditional) object to vigilantes, while Sikh and Satanist readings welcome protection and retribution. Third, the no-kill rule wins some credit from Catholic and Buddhist readers but not from Anabaptists, who see maiming as no better.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to protect someone "until death do us part"? A girl who can see the future picks a stranger, and he keeps the promise through ten years of serialization.

**The answer the story gives.** Protection is total commitment, but it should not become killing. The Network refuses to kill even monsters, and the worst villain is spared (ch. 213). The promise grows into love and, after Haruka is grown, marriage (ch. 214).

**Characters as arguments.** Mamoru argues that justice can be private and still restrained, though his "restraint" includes cutting off limbs. Genda, trained by the same master, argues for the law (Yen vol. 11). Haruka argues that the protected can become protectors: she trains, plans, and leads (The Fandom Post; Yen vol. 12). Edge and Zashid argue that people with gifts are property. Fang shows the cost of a life of killing (ch. 42).

**Symbols and motifs.** Seeing and blindness: the man who cannot see and the girl who sees too far. The cane that hides a sword. The wedding-vow title, which turns out to be literal.

**Traditions it echoes.** Zatoichi, The Professional (Léon), Minority Report and Mission: Impossible (The Manga Critic); the bodyguard romance. The guardian-and-ward romance has older literary roots, and modern readers often question it (Sean Gaffney).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Duty ethics wins: the rule against killing holds to the end. Might makes right is defeated in every villain. Transhumanist technology is a tool for both sides, and its darkest uses (cloning a superman, biochips from fetuses) belong to the villains. Liberal rule of law gets one serious arc but loses to private justice.

**Shifts across the series.** Yen vols. 1-3 (likely ch. 1-50): the rescue, yakuza and Ex-Solid, the Network introduced. Vols. 4-7 (likely ch. 51-116): Wiseman, mercenaries, Haruka's training, the Chechnya backstory. Vols. 8-10 (likely ch. 117-167): the school under guard and the war with the Trump unit. Vols. 11-13 (likely ch. 168-217): Genda, the Galboa-Duhana war, the confession and the marriage.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Haruka chose a stranger to protect her. Why did she trust him?
2. The Network will not kill, but it maims. Is that better, worse, or just different?
3. Genda follows the law and Mamoru does not. Who is right, and when?
4. Haruka says at 12 that she will marry Mamoru. How should a grown-up protector respond to a child's crush?
5. The villain is spared at the end. Why might the story make that choice?
