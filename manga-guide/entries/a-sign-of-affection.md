**Ages checked.** The leads are adults. Yuki Itose is 19 and Itsuomi Nagi is 22 (English Wikipedia); Otaku USA also calls Yuki "19-year-old." Kodansha calls her a "college student" and Itsuomi "an upperclassman"; the fan wiki says she is a second-year university student, TV Tropes says first-year (a discrepancy to check in print; either way she is at university). Itsuomi is a university senior (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). The supporting cast are university students or working adults (a bar owner, a hair-salon worker). Common Sense Media notes that characters under Japan's drinking age (20) do not drink. No sexual content involving a minor was found in any source.

**Premise.** Yuki, deaf from birth, went to a school for the deaf through high school and is now at university, communicating by sign language, text and lip reading. On a train she meets Itsuomi, a well-travelled senior who speaks three languages but not sign language. He treats her "like any other person" (Otaku USA) and sets out to learn Japanese Sign Language. They become a couple (vol. 4), meet each other's friends and parents, and move in together before he leaves Japan for two years to pursue a dream (vol. 7-10). Side stories follow Oushi, Yuki's protective childhood friend who loves her; Emma, who has loved Itsuomi since high school and later dates Shin; and Rin, Yuki's note-taker friend, and Kyoya, Itsuomi's bar-owning cousin (English Wikipedia; fan wiki; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life is lived in honest connection: learning another person's language, saying what you feel, accepting help without being defined by it, and choosing a partner who widens your world. Disability is part of an ordinary, full life, not the whole of it.**

| Socio-cultural | A Japanese university and young-adult romance about disability inclusion. It contrasts Deaf culture and sign language with families who do not sign (Yuki's parents do not, per Anime Corner), and a hearing friend whose care turns condescending (Oushi, ANN). Outlook: Hopeful — the couple meets obstacles of language, rivals, family worry and distance, but each is met with patience and honest talk, and the story keeps the relationship and friendships growing rather than breaking.

## Creator and publisher context

- suu Morishita is the pen name of a duo who met in high school as aspiring manga artists: Makiro writes (storyboards, layouts, Yuki's monologue, dialogue) and Nachiyan draws (Kodansha USA interview, July 21, 2020; Anime Corner interview, October 5, 2025). Their earlier work includes Shortcake Cake (from general knowledge; to verify).
- Why sign language: Nachiyan proposed it (Kodansha interview); Makiro cites a hard-of-hearing neighbor and a nearby school for the deaf, and found sign language beautiful: "How cool is it to speak in this way?" (Anime Corner). They wanted Yuki's life to feel ordinary: "how normal would it be to live when you have a disability" (Anime Corner).
- Research: books, interviews with teachers at a school for the deaf, and Deaf consultant Yuki Miyazaki, who "supervised our use of sign language" and shared her daily life (Kodansha interview; English Wikipedia).
- On sensitive scenes: Makiro was "very nervous" about the scene where Itsuomi learns Yuki's parents don't sign, and "didn't want to hurt anybody" (Anime Corner). On romance: love scenes should be "very romantic and beautiful," not "raunchy or vulgar" (Nachiyan, Anime Corner). The adult setting was chosen to be "grounded to reality," including drinking (Anime Corner).
- The 2021 hiatus was announced because the storyboard writer gave birth (English Wikipedia; ANN news, found in search). The creators have not spoken publicly about religion or politics in sources opened. No controversies involving the creators were found.
- Publisher: Kodansha (Tokyo); English edition by Kodansha USA, which briefly pulled the series from its digital services in early 2023 before adding it to K Manga in August 2023 (English Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take for two people who live in different "worlds" to really reach each other? Yuki lives in a world of silence, sign and text. Itsuomi lives in a world of many spoken languages and many countries. The chapter titles keep returning to this word: "Yuki's World," "Oushi's World," "Itsuomi's World" (English Wikipedia volume list).

**The answer the story gives.** You have to look, learn and say things plainly. The creators stress that sign language requires you to "see that person" (Nachiyan, Anime Corner). Itsuomi, who already speaks three languages, learns a fourth because it is hers (Kodansha interview). Yuki, in turn, tells him what she fears, that her deafness makes her a burden, and then lists what she can do (Sign 28). The story's answer is that love is shown in effort to understand, and that being understood is not the same as being protected.

**Characters as arguments.** Itsuomi is the argument for openness: he treats Yuki "like any other person instead of someone to be avoided or treated like glass" (Otaku USA). Oushi is the counter-argument: he learned sign young and loves Yuki, but believes she must be shielded, which ANN calls "the classic trap of patronizing a person because of their disability." Emma is the argument that long attachment does not give a claim on someone. Rin and Kyoya show a quiet, mutual ease. Yuki's parents, who do not sign, show love that is real but limited by what they did not learn.

**Symbols and motifs.** Fingertips and hands (the title). Snow, Yuki's name, and the snowy opening chapter. Text messages, drawn as square, left-aligned boxes; gray text for lip reading and black text for what she cannot catch (Anime Feminist). Trains, where they meet. The shared apartment, a "world" both now live in. Itsuomi's travels, which pull against it.

**Traditions it echoes.** Shojo romance, with its focus on feeling beyond dialogue (Kodansha interview). Disability-rights ideas of autonomy and accommodation. Japanese family courtesy. A cosmopolitan ideal of crossing languages.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanist and progressive values carry the story: dignity, capability and accommodation without control. Expressive individualism shows in Yuki's choices and Itsuomi's dream. Confucian-style family courtesy is kept but does not decide. Religious lenses are absent; faith readers will notice the cohabitation and drinking more than any message about God.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 are a slow first-love story told from Yuki's silent point of view. Volumes 4-6 bring the couple together and widen to friends, rivals and Oushi's own chapters. Volumes 7-10 turn to commitment: the move-in proposal, meeting her parents and living together. Volumes 11-14 open Itsuomi's past and dream and resolve Oushi's feelings. The series is now in its final chapters.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Itsuomi already speaks three languages. Why does learning Yuki's matter so much to her?
2. Oushi says he wants to protect Yuki. Where does protecting someone become controlling them?
3. Yuki worries she will be a burden. What does she do with that fear, and how does Itsuomi respond?
4. The couple move in together after asking her parents. How does your family think about that step?
5. The story was written by hearing creators with a Deaf consultant. What would a story by a Deaf author add?
