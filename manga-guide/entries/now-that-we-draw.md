**Premise.** High schooler Yuuki Uehara, a short, shy otaku, dreams of becoming a manga artist. When an editor at his dream publisher dismisses his romantic comedy, starring a perfectly chaste, idealized heroine, as unrealistic, he sinks into despair (Seven Seas; Wikipedia). His popular, tall gyaru classmate Niina Miyamoto has seen his manuscript; she encourages him and reveals that she too wants to be a manga artist and got the same note from her editor. Since neither has romantic experience, she proposes a fake relationship so both can gather material, and the two act like a couple while pushing each other's work forward (Wikipedia; Comic Natalie). By volume 6 Uehara has made his professional debut (a newcomer award ceremony is ch. 23), Miyamoto realizes her feelings are real, and Uehara's childhood friend Ren Shiori ("Love-chan"), a reserved, Kansai-speaking professional shonen manga artist, joins a love triangle; in volume 7 she transfers to their school and the three form a manga club (Seven Seas, v6-7; Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you write honestly about love without having lived it? Uehara's heroine is perfect and chaste, and an editor tells him she is unreal. The series asks where good art comes from: imagination, or experience.

**The answer the story gives.** Experience, honestly faced. The fake relationship begins as research and becomes real feeling; by volume 6 Uehara has debuted and Miyamoto knows she loves him (Seven Seas). Hard work after rejection is rewarded. How the story finally judges the pretense is not yet known; a chapter title, "I Don't Want to Make Her a Liar" (ch. 9), suggests it takes the lie seriously (to verify).

**Characters as arguments.** Uehara argues for the safe, idealized fantasy and learns its limits. Miyamoto argues for boldness and practical effort. Ren, a professional already, argues for craft and old loyalty, and complicates the romance. The editors stand for the market's hard realism.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rejected manuscript; the "perfect heroine" against the real girl; the manga club; the newcomer award.

**Traditions it echoes.** Bakuman and other manga about making manga; the fake-dating romantic comedy; the artist's apprenticeship story. For faith readers, the parable of the talents; for Buddhists, right effort; for secular readers, the value of honest critique.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and virtue ethics are affirmed: effort and self-expression win. Postmodern questions about fiction and reality are explored lightly. Hedonism appears in the fan service rather than in the story's argument.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 set up the fake relationship and the first submissions; volumes 3-5 move toward Uehara's debut (ch. 23) and growing feelings; volume 6 brings the love triangle with Ren; volume 7 starts a new school year and the manga club (Wikipedia; Seven Seas).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Uehara's editor says his heroine is unrealistic. What makes a character feel real?
2. Is it fair to the classmates that the two pretend to date? Who could be hurt?
3. How does Miyamoto help Uehara when he wants to quit?
4. What would you do after a rejection of something you made?
5. Why do you think the art includes fan service, and does it fit the story's message?
