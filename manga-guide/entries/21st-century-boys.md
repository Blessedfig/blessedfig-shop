**Premise.** At the end of 20th Century Boys, Friend, the masked leader who ruled the world as a god, is killed and the war is over. Viz's blurb: no one knows who Friend was or where he came from, and the only clue is "hidden deep within the memories" of Kenji. A last entry in Friend's prophecy points to an anti-proton bomb (MyAnimeList review; Slings and Arrows; fan wiki). Kenji enters the Friends' virtual reality of the late 1960s and early 1970s, where he meets his own childhood self, to learn who Friend was and to find the bomb's remote control. In the real world Kanna, Yukiji and their allies hunt for the same remote while UN forces occupy Tokyo and the UN investigator Chono leads an inquiry (Comic Book Bin; Wikipedia; BookDragon). The answer lies in a forgotten classmate, Katsumata, and in a small wrong Kenji did as a boy: he stole a prize from the local candy shop and let Katsumata take the blame, after which classmates treated Katsumata as "dead" (Wikipedia; fan wiki). Chapter 15 is titled "I'm Sorry," and the last chapter, "20th Century Boy," closes the saga (Wikipedia chapter list).

## Life message

**The worst harm can start with a small wrong that one forgets and another never does. A good life means looking honestly at your own past, owning what you did, saying sorry to the person you hurt, and not letting anyone, including a lonely child, become invisible.** Reviewers stress memory and whether childhood recollections are "the truth or a falsified truth" (Fandom Post) and note that the flashbacks no longer show whose memory is being scanned, "deliberately disorienting" (Slings and Arrows, Perfect Edition). Kenji's apology to the adult Katsumata is refused (fan wiki; TV Tropes via companion entry), but in the virtual past he befriends the boy, apologizes for the theft and tells him "live strong" (fan wiki; chapter 15 "I'm Sorry," Wikipedia). Slings and Arrows calls it a "humane and thrilling ending."

## Creator and publisher context

- Naoki Urasawa (born 1960) wrote and drew the series; Takashi Nagasaki is credited for cooperation. Their on-record statements on the saga's origin, the September 11 change and Nagasaki's proposal of a religious villain are collected in the companion entry (Japan House LA 2019; Nikkei 2021; eiga.com 2008). Urasawa does not plan stories in advance and says about a tenth of the scenes reflect his own childhood (Wikipedia).
- On Expo '70, the backdrop of this part's virtual past: Urasawa was ten, and like Kenji his family would not let him go, though he read the guidebook closely. He says "those who went had their dreams broken; I, who could not go, still have the dream," and that this feeling led to 20th Century Boys about thirty years later. He says he aims for manga every generation can share, avoiding trendy words and not presenting "values of good and evil that easily shift" (Sankei Shimbun interview, January 2, 2017, Japanese, opened; my translation).
- The kanzenban's new ending was "specifically created for the publication" (ANN news, 2016). No statement of Urasawa's reasons was found.
- Viz licensed both series in 2005 but delayed them until after Monster; in a 2012 ANN interview Urasawa said he did not know of the art-style reason that had been reported. Viz licensed the kanzenban editions in 2017 (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shogakukan. Viz Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge). No reputable reports of creator controversy were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who was Friend, and why? After twenty-two volumes of a masked god, 21st Century Boys turns the question around: who made him? Viz's blurb says the only clue is buried in Kenji's memory. The answer is not a conspiracy but a classroom.

**The answer the story gives.** Kenji stole a prize from the candy shop and let Katsumata take the blame (Wikipedia). The boy was scolded in front of his class and then shut out so completely that classmates spoke of him as dead; a rumor said his ghost dissected fish in the science room at night. Chapter 8 is titled "You Died Today." The forgotten boy grew up to deem the world "unnecessary." The story's answer to "why" is that a small wrong, forgotten by the one who did it, was never forgotten by the one who paid for it. Its answer to "what now" is confession. The grown Katsumata refuses Kenji's apology (fan wiki). But in the virtual past Kenji has his younger self befriend the masked boys and apologize, and he tells the young Katsumata, "Along the way a lot of things are gonna happen, but... live strong" (fan wiki). The chapter is titled "I'm Sorry." The story does not make the apology magic: it cannot undo the dead, and it reaches only a remembered child. Slings and Arrows still calls the result "humane."

**Characters as arguments.** Kenji argues that a hero must also be a penitent. Katsumata argues that exclusion can turn a child into a monster, without excusing the monster. Sadakiyo, the other masked boy, who in the end holds a knife to Friend (Wikipedia), argues that the overlooked can still choose the right side. Kanna, distrusted as the "devil's daughter" (BookDragon), argues that children are not their parents' crimes. Fukubei, whose role changes between editions, suggests that the mask matters more than the face: anyone ignored long enough could wear it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The stolen prize. The Friend mask, and a face surgically copied (Wikipedia). The virtual past, a rebuilt childhood that is both the regime's tool and the place where truth is found. The science room and its ghost story. The rock song that gives the saga and its last chapter their names. A reviewer praises the ending's "symmetry" (Fandom Post), and the final panel is reproduced as the Perfect Edition's cover (Slings and Arrows).

**Traditions it echoes.** Confession narratives in many faiths: an examination of conscience that finds the sin one had hidden. Teshuvah and tawba, which hold that a wrong against a person needs that person's pardon. Stephen King's It (noted in the companion entry), where adults return to a childhood wound. Japanese stories of ijime (bullying) and its long shadow. Urasawa's own memory of Expo '70 as the line between "past" and "present" (Sankei 2017).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarian rule is broken in the first chapter; what remains is its source. Secular and naturalistic readings find a god reduced to a man. Faith readings find a parable of hidden sin and repentance. Virtue ethics finds honesty and courage at the climax. The psychic thread (Kanna's powers, Katsumata's precognition) is left standing, which some readers find weak (Fandom Post).

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-8) opens with Friend's death, the UN in Tokyo and Kenji's entry into the virtual past, and ends on "You Died Today." Volume 2 (ch. 9-16) resolves the bomb threat ("Button," "Punch Out," "I'm Not Letting It End"), gives the apology ("I'm Sorry") and reveals Katsumata in the last chapter. The 2016 kanzenban changes the reveal so that Katsumata was Friend all along.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kenji forgot the theft; Katsumata never did. Why do we remember the wrongs done to us better than the wrongs we do?
2. Does Kenji's apology matter if the grown Katsumata refuses it? What does your family's tradition say about forgiveness that is asked but not given?
3. Kenji apologizes to a remembered child in a virtual world. Can you make amends to someone in the past?
4. How responsible is a whole class for treating one child as if he were dead?
5. Urasawa changed who Friend was in a later edition. Does it change the story's message if the mask was worn by one person or two?
