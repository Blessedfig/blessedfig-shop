**Premise.** At the end of 20th Century Boys, Friend, the masked leader who ruled the world as a god, is dead and the war is over. Viz's blurb: no one knows who Friend was or where he came from, and the only clue is "hidden deep within the memories" of Kenji. Kenji enters the Friends' virtual reality of 1971, where he watches and speaks with his own childhood self, to find out who Friend was and to find the remote control for an anti-proton bomb. In the real world Kanna and her allies hunt for the same remote while UN officials distrust her as the "devil's daughter" (Fandom Post; BookDragon). The answer lies in a forgotten classmate, Katsumata, and in a small wrong Kenji did as a boy: he stole a plastic Expo badge from a local shop and let Katsumata take the blame (TV Tropes). A few closing chapters revisit the cast and end where the saga began, with Kenji playing "20th Century Boy" over the school PA (TV Tropes, Book Ends).

## Life message

**The worst harm can start with a small wrong that one forgets and another never does. A good life means looking honestly at your own past, owning what you did, saying sorry to the person you hurt, and not letting anyone, including a lonely child, become invisible.** Reviewers stress memory and whether childhood recollections are "the truth or a falsified truth" (Fandom Post); the companion entry's scholars read Kenji's climax as an apology rather than a victory (Bancha 2020, via companion entry).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who was Friend, and why? After twenty-two volumes of a masked god, 21st Century Boys turns the question around: who made him? Viz's blurb says the only clue is buried in Kenji's memory. The answer is not a conspiracy but a classroom.

**The answer the story gives.** Kenji stole a plastic Expo badge and let Katsumata take the blame (TV Tropes). The boy was shut out so completely that classmates spoke of him as dead. The forgotten boy grew up to want the whole world to look at him. The story's answer to "why" is that a small wrong, forgotten by the one who did it, was never forgotten by the one who paid for it. Its answer to "what now" is confession: Kenji's climax is an apology (Bancha, via companion entry). The story does not make the apology magic. The companion entry records that Katsumata refuses it (TV Tropes, to verify on the page).

**Characters as arguments.** Kenji argues that a hero must also be a penitent. Katsumata argues that exclusion can turn a child into a monster, without excusing the monster. Kanna, distrusted as the "devil's daughter" (BookDragon), argues that children are not their parents' crimes. Fukubei, whose role changes between editions (Wikipedia), suggests that the mask matters more than the face: anyone ignored long enough could wear it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The plastic Expo badge. The Friend mask. The virtual 1971, a rebuilt past that is both the regime's tool and the place where truth is found. The school roof. The PA system and T. Rex's song, which bookend the saga (TV Tropes). A reviewer praises the ending's "symmetry" (Fandom Post).

**Traditions it echoes.** Confession narratives in many faiths: an examination of conscience that finds the sin one had hidden. Teshuvah and tawba, which hold that a wrong against a person needs that person's pardon. Stephen King's It (noted in the companion entry), where adults return to a childhood wound. Japanese stories of ijime (bullying) and its long shadow.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarian rule is already broken; what remains is its source. Secular and naturalistic readings find a god reduced to a man. Faith readings find a parable of hidden sin and repentance. Virtue ethics finds honesty and courage at the climax. The psychic thread (Kanna's "extrasensory powers") is left standing, which some readers find weak (Fandom Post).

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-8) sets up the twin hunts: Kenji in the virtual past, Kanna in Tokyo. Volume 2 (ch. 9-16) resolves the bomb threat, reveals Friend and closes with a tour of the cast and the PA bookend. The 2016 kanzenban changes the reveal so that Katsumata was Friend all along.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kenji forgot the badge; Katsumata never did. Why do we remember the wrongs done to us better than the wrongs we do?
2. Does Kenji's apology matter if Katsumata refuses it? What does your family's tradition say about forgiveness that is asked but not given?
3. How responsible is a whole class for treating one child as if he were dead?
4. Kanna is called the "devil's daughter." Should children carry their parents' guilt?
5. Urasawa changed who Friend was in a later edition. Does it change the story's message if the mask was worn by one person or two?
