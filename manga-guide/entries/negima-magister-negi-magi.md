**Premise.** Negi Springfield, a 10-year-old Welsh boy just graduated from magic school, is sent to Mahora Academy in Japan as English and homeroom teacher of Class 2-A (later 3-A), 31 middle-school girls, while hiding that he is a mage and searching for his missing father, the legendary "Thousand Master" Nagi. His roommate Asuna becomes his first partner through a Pactio, a magic contract sealed with a kiss, to face Evangeline, a 600-year-old vampire in a child's body who is also his student (ch. 16-25). A class trip to Kyoto brings battles with Eastern mages and the rival Fate Averruncus (ch. 27-53); a demon from Negi's past attacks (ch. 63-71); the school festival holds a martial-arts tournament and a time-traveling classmate, Chao, who wants to change history (ch. 72-162). Negi and his partners then travel to the Magic World (ch. 186-335), learn it is a magically made world on Mars whose people Fate's group plans to move into an eternal dream-paradise, and find Negi's father possessed by the Mage of the Beginning. Negi wins with a plan to save the Magic World without sacrifice by terraforming Mars, and an epilogue shows his class's long, happy lives (ch. 336-355) (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Keep training, keep your promises and protect the people you are responsible for; face the real world with courage instead of escaping into a perfect dream, and look for a way to save everyone rather than sacrificing some for the rest.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Ken Akamatsu (story and art), born July 5, 1968, in Nagoya; studied Japanese literature at Chuo University; debuted in 1993; known for the romantic comedies A.I. Love You and Love Hina, then Negima! and its sequel UQ Holder! (Wikipedia, Ken Akamatsu).
- In 2010-2011 he founded J-Comi (now Manga Library Z), a free, ad-supported site for out-of-print manga (Wikipedia).
- Public role: managing director of the Japan Cartoonists Association since 2018 and an advocate for freedom of expression in manga and anime; as the association's spokesman he opposed a 2013 proposal to extend Japan's child pornography law to wholly fictional drawings; elected to the House of Councillors in 2022 for the Liberal Democratic Party on a free-expression platform (Wikipedia).
- On the English edition, Akamatsu said he was amused rather than angry at Del Rey's planned edits (Wikipedia); ANN reports he compared them to strict censorship in Korea years earlier (ANN, 2004).
- No statements by Akamatsu on the series' themes were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Shonen Magazine). English: Del Rey, then Kodansha USA.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What should you do when saving the world seems to require sacrificing someone? Negi begins as a child trying to be a teacher and to reach a father he barely remembers. By the end he faces an enemy offering every person their happiest dream and a world that can only be saved, it seems, by giving up its people.

**The answer the story gives.** Refuse the dream and refuse the sacrifice. Negi rejects the perfect world (its password means "a little courage," fan wiki) and looks for a solution where no one is erased, which becomes a plan to terraform Mars (ch. 336-343). Friends and allies make it possible.

**Characters as arguments.** Negi argues for earnest effort and duty. Evangeline argues that strength sometimes means taking darkness into yourself, and Negi learns her Dark Magic (from ch. 208). Chao argues that changing history is justified to prevent suffering. Fate argues that an eternal dream is kinder than a dying world. Asuna argues for loyalty, and her own sacrifice of a century's sleep is the cost of Negi's plan.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Pactio card (a bond that gives each partner a power); the World Tree; Nagi's staff; the dream-garden of Cosmo Entelecheia; Mars as a new home.

**Traditions it echoes.** The boarding-school magic story, the harem comedy, the tournament shonen and the planetary space epic. Its rejection of a perfect illusion echoes old warnings about lotus-eaters and comforting lies, and Buddhist teaching on craving; its refusal to sacrifice the few for the many echoes duty ethics against utilitarian bargains.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and duty ethics win. Utilitarian sacrifice and hedonistic escape are rejected. Magic is the world's furniture and is shown as good in a hero's hands, which some religious families will not accept. The harem and fan service work against the series' own respect for its heroines.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-25) are classroom comedy and the Evangeline arc, with the heaviest fan service. Volumes 4-8 (ch. 26-71) are the Kyoto trip and the demon attack. Volumes 9-18 (ch. 72-168) are the school festival, tournament and Chao. Volumes 19-36 (ch. 169-335) are the Magic World war and its revelations. Volumes 37-38 (ch. 336-355) are the Mars project and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Fate offered everyone their happiest dream. Why did Negi say no?
2. Negi wanted a plan where no one had to be sacrificed. Is that always possible?
3. Evangeline teaches Negi a power that eats at the user. Should he have learned it?
4. The story jokes a lot about the girls' bodies. How does that fit with how it says they should be treated?
5. Are people made by magic "real" people? How would you decide?
