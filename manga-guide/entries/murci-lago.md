**Premise.** Kuroko Koumori, 25, has murdered 715 people since childhood and sits on death row (fan wiki; Wikipedia). Because her city, Ruruie, is overrun by killers the police cannot stop, the government stays her execution indefinitely in exchange for her work as a "national executioner," hunting and killing murderers beyond the law's reach. She is driven by Hinako Tozakura, a cheerful, childlike 13-year-old middle schooler and expert driver whom Kuroko looks after like a parent (fan wiki; TV Tropes; ANN calls her a high school student), and backed by Chiyo Yanaoka, 19, a yakuza boss's daughter and Kuroko's girlfriend. Kuroko is a lesbian who flirts with and seduces nearly every woman she meets, often mid-case. The arcs pit her against a drugged wrestler (ch. 1), a mansion murder party (ch. 4-10), a killer with vibrating blades whose 9-year-old daughter Rinko comes into Kuroko's care (Domestic Killer, ch. 12-17), a rose-growing cult (Rose-Colored Prison, ch. 19-26), a man who abducts and strangles young girls (Between Sora and Me, ch. 28-33), a school bombing and bullying case (School Destruction, ch. 35-41), a drug ring tied to a performance drug called Cesare ("Sakura" of Oblivion and later), and Lovecraft-tinged horrors (The Deep One) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**In a city overrun by killers, it takes a monster to stop monsters: the story enjoys its psychopathic heroine's freedom and appetites, gives her a protective love for the girls in her care, and asks little about whether licensed killing is justice.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do when the law cannot stop the monsters? Ruruie's answer is to hire a worse one. The series asks, mostly by not asking, whether a society protected by a mass murderer is safe or simply ruled by her.

**The answer the story gives.** That it works and that it is fun to watch. Kuroko wins, the killers die, and the girls in her care are protected. The story is more interested in Kuroko's appetites than in justice.

**Characters as arguments.** Kuroko argues that freedom means taking what you want. Hinako, cheerful and forgiving, is innocence that does not hold grudges (fan wiki). The villains, many once decent people broken by tragedy (TV Tropes), argue that anyone can become a monster.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bat (murciélago, koumori): a creature of the night that hunts. Roses grown from corpses (ch. 19-26). Cesare, the drug that makes people into weapons. Hinako's car.

**Traditions it echoes.** The licensed-killer story (Golgo 13, which Yen Press's editor cites), the serial-killer thriller, Lovecraftian horror and yuri. Readers of faith may set it beside Romans 13:4 and 12:19: the sword belongs to rightful authority, and vengeance to God.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism and expressive individualism are celebrated; might makes right is the world's rule; utilitarianism is the unquestioned premise. The occult appears only in villains.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-6 (to ch. 41) are short, brutal cases: the Murder Party, Domestic Killer, the cult and the school bombing. Vols. 7-10 build the Cesare and "Sakura" conspiracy. Later volumes bring swordsmen, a mafia family, legendary killers and the "Eternal Life" arc (to ch. 188).

**Discussion questions.**
1. The government lets a mass murderer go free to kill other killers. Is that justice?
2. Kuroko protects Hinako and also says she desires her. How should a story treat an adult who wants a child?
3. Many of the villains were once good people. What turned them, and could anyone have stopped it?
4. Does knowing a story is fiction make cruelty in it acceptable to enjoy?
5. What makes Ruruie so dangerous, and what would really fix it?
