**Premise.** Five centuries ago, scientists seeded Mars with algae and cockroaches to warm the planet. In 2577 the first crewed ship lands and its six crew are killed by giant humanoid cockroaches, the "Terraformars." The second expedition, BUGS 2 (vol. 1), sends fifteen people whose bodies carry insect DNA through the "Bugs Procedure," activated by injecting a drug; nearly all die, and only Shokichi Komachi and Ichiro Hiruma return. Twenty years later, the Annex I mission (vols. 2-17) sends six national squads, each member given a "Mosaic Organ" operation with a 36% survival rate, to find a cure for the A.E. virus that is killing people on Earth. Most die, in battles with the Terraformars and in betrayals among the nations (the Chinese squad, a scheming American officer of the eugenic Newton clan); a few survivors bring back samples. In the Earth saga (vol. 18 on), Terraformars are found to have been hiding in Japan for 17 years, and a secret Rome-based group, "We Are The Cosmos," is breeding human-Terraformar hybrids (Wikipedia; fan wiki; TV Tropes; VIZ synopses).

## Life message

**In a universe where evolution rewards only strength, people prove what humanity is worth by loyalty and self-sacrifice for their comrades, while the powerful who treat people as tools are the real danger.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | The engine of the whole story: cockroaches out-evolve humans; chapter titles name "The Selfish Gene" and "Extended Phenotype" (ch. 128, vol. 13); humans borrow other species' traits to survive; the Newton clan breeds humans selectively for 600 years (fan wiki). Strength, adaptation and kin loyalty decide who lives. |
| Pragmatic | Survival depends on planning, knowing your powers' limits and using the right weapon; the Annex I squads win when they cooperate and lose when nations pursue their own interests (ComicsAlliance notes nationalism and self-interest hinder unity). |
| Religious | No faith shapes the plot, but the Bible is quoted at the heroes' last stands: each deliberate overdose is tied to a plague passage (Thien's locusts; Adolf reciting Exodus 9; Komachi citing Deuteronomy 7:20 on hornets) (TV Tropes). Chapter titles borrow sacred words ("The Holy Land," "Coming of the Gods," "The Black Mass"). The Terraformars' leaders wear robes, and the word "Rahab" is an unexplained mystery (TV Tropes). |
| Socio-cultural | A war story about nations using people as tools: poor recruits join to escape poverty or debt (the Mexican trio seeking US citizenship; Keiji paying for eye surgery; Eva sold to U-NASA by her family) (Wikipedia). Three 2014 reviewers (ANN, Sequential State, Manga Bookshelf) say the Terraformars' faces resemble racist caricatures of Black people; ANN also notes the only competent fighters are Asian and only Japanese men survive vol. 1. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — most of each crew dies horribly and governments scheme throughout, but sacrifices buy humanity a cure and a few survivors come home; Manga Bookshelf's reviewer called vol. 1 bleak and cynical, and the Earth saga is unfinished.
Hope source: P — protecting others: hope comes from people who die so comrades can live (Donatello holding off a horde; Thien's overdose so Komachi and Hiruma can escape, vol. 1; Liu Yiwu giving Komachi his own heart, Mars finale) (Wikipedia); loyalty within the squads (F) is close behind.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame honors the self-sacrifice of Thien, Donatello and Liu and the story's condemnation of states that use people as tools. They divide over how much a war story built on gore and engineered bodies can carry: peace churches, Adventists, Witnesses and Buddhists read killing as the message itself (D), while Sikh, conservative, secular humanist and Satanist frames read courage against an existential threat more warmly (B). The racial-caricature concern raised by three 2014 reviewers weighs most heavily for Historically Black Protestant and secular progressive readers; it is a depiction concern, since the story never states contempt for a people, but these frames note that the creatures drawn this way are hordes to be exterminated.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yu Sasuga (story) and Kenichi Tachibana (art). Sasuga's health caused the breaks of 2017-2018 and December 2018 to April 2024 (fan wiki; TV Tropes trivia); TV Tropes reports Tachibana said in 2021 that the series would resume once his partner recovered. No earlier works or biographical details were confirmed in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- No interview with either creator about intent, religion or politics was found in sources opened. The Bible-plague motif and the evolution-themed chapter titles are the clearest authorial signals (TV Tropes; fan wiki).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Miracle Jump, Weekly Young Jump, seinen). VIZ Media publishes it under VIZ Signature, rated M. Shueisha also licensed seven spin-offs by other artists, including an erotic gag spin-off (Wikipedia).
- Controversy: three English-language reviews in 2014 (ANN, Sequential State, Manga Bookshelf) said the Terraformars' features resemble racist caricatures of Black people, while judging it unintentional or not malicious in intent; Sequential State asked why VIZ licensed it. No response from the creators or VIZ was found. TV Tropes notes the Boichi-drawn spin-off and the 2016 film present the creatures differently.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes humanity worth saving, in a universe where evolution rewards only what survives? The Terraformars are the perfect evolutionary product: strong, adaptive, without mercy. The humans sent to fight them are not even fully human any more. If survival is the only rule, why does it matter who wins?

**The answer the story gives.** Humanity is shown at its worst in its governments, which bargain and betray, and at its best in ordinary crew members who die for each other. "Don't underestimate humanity" is the refrain (TV Tropes), but what the story honors is not strength alone: it is Thien choosing to die so his friends can escape, and Liu giving his heart to a former enemy. Manga Bookshelf's Sean Gaffney found the view of humanity too bleak and cynical; the sacrifices are the counterweight.

**Characters as arguments.** Komachi argues for protective violence, from killing Nanao's abuser to leading his squad. Nanao, who wanted to talk rather than fight, dies first, a hard verdict on gentleness. Hiruma argues for self-interest and becomes Prime Minister, neither punished nor praised. Victoria Wood and Joseph Newton argue that power or breeding make some people better than others; both die. Liu, sent by a state that later tries to kill him, chooses comrades over country.

**Symbols and motifs.** Insects and animals as borrowed strength: every power comes from a creature, and the creatures' habits shape character (the parasitic wasp for the would-be puppet-master). The drug injection as the price of power. Bible plagues invoked against the enemy, reversing who plays Egypt. The red planet and the blue one: the Mars saga's red logo turns blue for Earth (fan wiki).

**Traditions it echoes.** Starship Troopers and Gantz (Manga Bookshelf; Sequential State), the war film's doomed squad, and the cautionary tale of hubris against nature: humans engineered the cockroaches and the cockroaches came back. Its evolutionary framing echoes Darwin and Dawkins by name in chapter titles.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is explored, not endorsed: the enhanced humans are heroes, but the programs that made them are condemned. Naturalism is the stage. Nationalism is mostly a problem, except for the Japanese squad's loyalty. Strength-worship is the enemy's creed, yet the plot also rewards whoever is strongest. Religious readers find Scripture used as a battle cry, which some will find stirring and others misused; secular readers find a world with no gods in which human loyalty is the only light.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (BUGS 2) is a short horror tragedy. Vols. 2-17 (Annex I) add national squads, politics and betrayal, ending with a few survivors returning in 2619. Vol. 18 onward (Earth saga) moves the war to Japan, adds a global conspiracy and human-Terraformar hybrids, and has been interrupted for years by the writer's health.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Thien and Liu give up their lives for others. Why do their deaths feel different from the others?
2. The governments send poor and indebted people to Mars. Who should take risks for everyone?
3. Three reviewers said the Terraformars are drawn like old racist caricatures. Look at the art: what do you notice, and why might it matter even if no harm was intended?
4. Nanao wanted to talk instead of fight. Was she wrong, or was the story unfair to her?
5. Is a person with insect DNA still fully human? What makes someone human?
