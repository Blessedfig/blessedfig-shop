**Premise.** Chihiro Furuya, a high school boy who lives at his family's Buddhist temple, is obsessed with zombie films and dreams of kissing a zombie girl; his younger sister loves ghosts and his senile grandfather was once a zombie researcher (Wikipedia). When his cat Bub is killed by a car, Chihiro brews a reanimation potion from poisonous hydrangea using an old manuscript. Rea Sanka, 16, the sheltered daughter of a rich, controlling father, finds him and helps; she makes him promise to bring her back if she dies (ch. 1-2; ANN; fan wiki). After her father slaps her and plans to keep her from Chihiro, she drinks the potion in a suicide attempt and survives, then dies in a fall from a cliff while shielding the revived Bub from her father, and rises as a zombie who must eat hydrangea leaves to stave off decay (ch. 3-4; Wikipedia). She moves in with Chihiro; her father fights Chihiro, then lets her stay and leaves to seek a cure (c. v2-3). A foreign researcher, Darin, and the zombie research body ZoMA arrive (v3-8); experiments there cost Rea her memories (ch. 34; TV Tropes); and her growing hunger ends in a rampage in which she eats Chihiro's heart (ch. 55), which he survives (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). An epilogue jumps to their descendants (ch. 56.1).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can love hold on to someone whose body is falling apart, and can a person own her life when her father thinks he owns her? Rea would rather die than stay a perfect daughter; Chihiro would rather love a zombie than a living girl.

**The answer the story gives.** Love means accepting the whole person, and freedom is worth almost any cost. Rea's escape is costly (her life), and the story does not hide that; but it presents her new existence with Chihiro as better than the life she had. Chihiro keeps his promise through decay, memory loss and finally her hunger, offering his own heart (ch. 55).

**Characters as arguments.** Rea argues for self-ownership. Her father argues, and loses, that a parent's love can justify control; his grief for his dead wife explains but does not excuse him (Wikipedia). Chihiro argues for love as responsibility. Ranko, the living girl he does not choose, is the case for ordinary love. ZoMA's chief shows obsession with the dead turned monstrous (v8).

**Symbols and motifs.** Hydrangeas as poison and preservation; the tent Chihiro pitches in his room to study Rea; the framed photographs as ownership; the heart eaten and replaced.

**Traditions it echoes.** Romero's zombie films (named in-story), Frankenstein, Romeo and Juliet's death-defying love, and Japanese tales of love with the dead. Christian readers may compare resurrection; Buddhist readers may see impermanence and clinging.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: Rea's freedom is the happy outcome. Transhumanist hopes (curing death) are explored and limited. Feminist themes are present in the story and undercut by the art. Traditional authority is shown mainly as abuse.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3: Rea's death and escape and her father's defeat. Volumes 4-6: decay, hunger and the cat's decline. Volumes 7-8: ZoMA abroad. Volumes 9-11: memory loss, the grandfather's past and the final rampage and reunion.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rea wanted to die to escape her father. What else could she have done, and who could have helped?
2. Rea's father says he is protecting her. What is the difference between protection and control?
3. Chihiro loves Rea as her body fails. What does that say about love?
4. The story says Rea's body belongs to her, but the art often shows her body for readers. Do those fit together?
5. Why do you think the story ends with their descendants?
