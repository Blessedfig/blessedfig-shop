**Premise.** Yuito Amami, a kind young doctor newly posted to a village with no doctor, is struck by lightning and wakes in another world of beastfolk, elves, dwarves, dragons and slimes. There, healing magic exists but is monopolized by the human race, illness is often treated as a curse, and other races suffer without care (v1; MyAnimeList review). With a few instruments and his own knowledge, and no cheat powers, he treats a chimera in a beastfolk village and frees Coroneh, a beastfolk girl sold into slavery, who becomes his apprentice (v1; AniList). In the elf country he treats the dragon king Lindwurm, whose 'blood-vomiting disease' is reflux esophagitis (v2), then must decide whether to treat Saia, one of the very humans who hoard healing (v3). He sets out to build a hospital where anyone can be treated regardless of race or status (v4), performs the world's first abdominal surgery under anesthesia and spreads preventive medicine (v5). On the human side, Archbishop Ecres and his six disciples, the Six Stars, guard the healing monopoly; Ecres later starts to look beyond healing magic and clashes with his superiors (v6-8), while a Demon King schemes in the background (v7, v11). Later arcs treat dwarves' occupational disease and their king's surgery during an earthquake (v8-9), a harpy who cannot fly (v10), an orc's ingrown toenail (v11) and a giant troll's bile-duct disease (v12) (Kodansha volume blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is healing worth if only some people can have it? In Amami's new world, healing magic exists, but one race holds it, other races suffer, and sickness is called a curse. A doctor with a bag of instruments and no powers asks whether knowledge can do what privilege will not.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly. Each case shows that illness has causes (a dragon's "blood-vomiting disease" is reflux, v2), that prevention and diet can heal (v5, v6, v10), and that care builds trust across old hatreds. The answer is not against magic as such: the archbishop himself begins to look beyond healing magic (v6), and the aim is a hospital for everyone (v4).

**Characters as arguments.** Amami argues for the physician's duty to all. Coroneh argues that the oppressed can learn and heal their own people. Saia, a human from the hoarding race, tests whether the doctor's duty includes enemies; Amami's answer is the moral center of v3. Archbishop Ecres argues for humanity-first healing, then for reform, and pays for it with his superiors (v6). The dragons argue for rule by fear, and are healed out of it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The lightning that brings him (and Clarke's law: knowledge that looks like magic). The hospital under construction at the World Tree. Anesthesia as mercy. The Spirit Festival and the restless mine (v9): science and ritual both trying to calm the mountain.

**Traditions it echoes.** The medical isekai (Parallel World Pharmacy is a close cousin), Japanese medical dramas, and the long religious history of hospitals and healers; religious readers may hear the Good Samaritan or the unmercenary physicians, and Buddhist readers the Medicine Buddha.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism and pragmatism carry the plot; duty ethics and virtue ethics carry the hero; critical theory appears in the monopoly and discrimination, without becoming the narrator's voice. Religion appears mainly as the human institution, whose portrayal (corrupt hierarchy, reforming archbishop) needs a full read to judge.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3: the beastfolk village, the elves and dragons, and the choice to treat Saia. Volumes 4-5: the hospital plan and the first surgery under anesthesia. Volumes 6-8: the Night Country princess, the Six Stars and the archbishop's reform. Volumes 9-12: the dwarf country, the harpy, the orc and the troll.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Amami treats Saia, one of the humans who kept healing from others. Would you? Why might it matter?
2. Why might people in a world without medicine call illness a curse?
