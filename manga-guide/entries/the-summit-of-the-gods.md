**Premise.** In 1924 George Mallory and Andrew Irvine vanished near the top of Everest; no one knows whether they reached the summit. In 1993, after a Japanese expedition fails and two of its members fall to their deaths, the photographer Makoto Fukamachi finds an old Kodak in a Kathmandu junk shop that may be Mallory's. It is stolen from his hotel and traced to a reclusive Japanese climber the locals call Bikha Sanp, 'the viper,' whom Fukamachi recognizes as Jōji Habu, a brilliant, abrasive solo climber who vanished from the climbing world years earlier (vol. 1; Japanese Wikipedia; Experiments in Manga). Back in Japan, his own relationship over, Fukamachi digs into Habu's life: a climber who would not hold a job, his rivalry with the gifted Tsuneo Hase, the fall that killed his young rope partner Buntarō Kishi, for which Habu blamed himself and sent money anonymously to Kishi's sister Ryōko for years, a near-fatal winter solo on the Grandes Jorasses (1979), and his walk-out from a 1984 Everest expedition (vols. 1-2). Fukamachi returns to Nepal with Ryōko; a fence's men kidnap her over the camera; Habu lives illegally in Nepal with the Sherpa Ang Tshering's family (in the novel he has married Ang Tshering's daughter) and is preparing a solo, winter, no-oxygen climb of Everest's southwest face (vols. 3-4). Fukamachi follows him under a rule of no talking and no help; in a storm Habu breaks his own rule to save him (vol. 5). Habu goes on alone and does not come back; Fukamachi returns to Japan to a media frenzy over the illegal climb and the camera (vol. 5; Experiments in Manga). In the novel, Ang Tshering later hands Fukamachi the camera with a letter from Habu; the manga's ending, which differs from the novel on some points including Mallory, is to be confirmed (Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A person can give his whole life to one hard, worthy summit, and the meaning lies in the giving everything, not in fame or even in coming home; but the cost falls on everyone who loves him, and even the most solitary climber is saved, and saves others, through another person.**

Survival under extreme cold and thin air is the constant test. |
| Pragmatic | Climbing is shown as gear, training, logistics, permits and money: Habu cannot get sponsors, climbs without a permit, and plans every step of the southwest face (vol. 4; Experiments in Manga). The story respects competence and shows that small mistakes kill. |

| Socio-cultural | Japan's postwar alpine clubs, the race for national 'firsts,' a man who will not fit the salaried life, and how foreign expeditions treat Sherpas (ANN House of 1000 Manga). Habu and Hase are modeled on the real climbers Masaru Morita and Tsuneo Hasegawa (Japanese Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Habu does not come back, Hase, Kishi and others die in the mountains, and Fukamachi returns changed; yet the story treats Habu's last climb as a life fulfilled, and Fukamachi has found what he lacked.
Hope source: E — the way through is giving everything to a hard goal: the series' hope is endurance and effort, Habu's refusal to stop and Fukamachi's growth from drifting to committed. Friendship and rescue on the mountain (F) are the close second.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

## Creator and publisher context

- Jiro Taniguchi (1947-2017) began as an assistant to Kyūta Ishikawa, worked with Natsuo Sekigawa (The Times of Botchan, Tezuka Osamu Cultural Prize 1998) and with Moebius (Icaro); he illustrated Yumemakura's Garōden (1989-90) before this series (Wikipedia, Jiro Taniguchi).
- On-record context (Japanese Wikipedia, citing the book Egaku Hito Taniguchi Jirō): Taniguchi had wanted to make a manga about the real climber Masaru Morita; when he learned Habu was modeled on Morita he agreed to adapt the novel. The manga changes some points from the novel, including what is revealed about Mallory's summit.
- Baku Yumemakura (born 1951) is a climber and outdoorsman who first went to Nepal in 1975 and has published photo collections of Nepalese mountains; he also writes martial-arts and fantasy fiction (Wikipedia). He later wrote creative notes on the novel for the climbing magazine Gakujin (2014-) and a spin-off story about Hase (Japanese Wikipedia).
- No statements by either creator on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (no English-language interview opened).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Business Jump, a seinen magazine). English publisher Fanfare/Ponent Mon specializes in European-style editions of Japanese literary manga and published most of Taniguchi's English work (Wikipedia; ANN).
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why climb? Or, for Fukamachi, why live, and for what? A camera that might prove whether Mallory reached the top of Everest in 1924 leads him to a man who has given up everything else for the mountain.

**The answer the story gives.** Because some people must give everything to something. Habu's life is the answer in action: no job, few friends, a partner's death on his conscience, exile in Nepal, and a plan to do what no one has done. The story honors that devotion and the beauty it reaches, while showing the people left at the foot of the mountain.

**Characters as arguments.** Habu argues for total commitment. Hase is the same drive in a more sociable form, and dies by it. Kishi shows what admiration can cost. Ryōko is love that waits and finally lets go. Fukamachi is the reader: comfortable, adrift, slowly drawn into a commitment of his own. Narada Rajendra, a former Gurkha officer who regrets serving as a foreign mercenary and wants to serve Nepal, sees Habu as his opposite: a man who lives only for his own passion (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Symbols and motifs.** Mallory's camera and its undeveloped film: the unanswerable question. The southwest face in winter. The rope that joins climbers and the rule of the solo climb. Habu's name in Nepal, 'the viper.' Taniguchi's mountains, which reviewers describe as a living presence.

**Traditions it echoes.** Mountaineering literature from Mallory's 'because it's there' to modern solo climbing; the Japanese gekiga drama; the existential quest. For faith readers it echoes the ascent as a spiritual image (Climacus's ladder; mountains as places of encounter) and the warning about idols.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential meaning-making is the heart. Individualism is admired and questioned in the same portrait. Stoic endurance and classical courage are the climbers' virtues. A rule kept and a rule broken for love meet in the rescue (vol. 5).

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the camera, Kathmandu and the search. Vol. 2: Habu's past, Hase and Kishi's fall. Vol. 3: Fukamachi and Ryōko return to Nepal; Habu's rescue feat and his goal. Vol. 4: the kidnapping's aftermath (likely), preparations and the start of the climb. Vol. 5: the climb, the rescue, Habu's disappearance and Fukamachi's return.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think Habu climbs? Is his reason a good one?
2. Habu sent money to Kishi's sister for years without signing his name. What does that tell you about him?
3. On the mountain Habu breaks his own rule to save Fukamachi. Which mattered more to him in the end, and why?
4. Who pays the price for Habu's dream? Was it fair to them?
5. Is there something you would work this hard for? What would you not give up for it?
