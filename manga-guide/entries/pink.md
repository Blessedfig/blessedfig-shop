**Premise.** Tokyo, 1989, at the height of the bubble economy. Yumi (Yumiko), 22, works as an office lady by day and as a call girl by night, because her salary cannot feed Croc, the pet crocodile who lives in her bathroom (Wikipedia; Kodansha USA blurb on AniList). Her stepmother, whom Yumi believes married her father for money, keeps a much younger lover, Haruo "Haru" Yoshino, a college student and would-be novelist with nothing to write about (Wikipedia; ANN). Yumi's much younger half-sister Keiko, a school-age child who roams at all hours and knows about Yumi's night job, brings the two together (ANN; AniList). Yumi and Haru fall in love; after her apartment floods she moves in with him, and Yumi, Haru, Keiko and Croc start to resemble a family. Haru writes a prize-winning novel by cutting and pasting passages from other books. Enraged by the affair, the stepmother has Croc taken and made into a leather suitcase, and Yumi sinks into depression. Haru plans to use his prize money to take Yumi to a tropical island, but he is killed in a car accident on the way home from an interview, and the book ends with Yumi waiting for a life that will not come (Wikipedia). ANN reads the story as a retelling of Snow White.

## Creator and publisher context

- Kyoko Okazaki, born 1963 in Tokyo, debuted in 1983 with short stories in Manga Burikko, an erotic manga magazine for adult men, and later worked for josei magazines such as Feel Young; she produced about 20 volumes from the mid-1980s to 1996, including River's Edge and Helter Skelter, and has not published new work since a 1996 traffic accident (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: in writing about Pink she linked her picture of love to capitalism, describing love not as warm and fuzzy but as a fierce, frightful and cruel monster, and capitalism likewise (Wikipedia, citing Spies; paraphrased). Her afterword to the English edition describes the book as the everyday life of a girl born, raised and "normally" wrecked (BookDragon; paraphrased).
- Critical context: scholars credit her with capturing the zeitgeist of bubble-era Tokyo and with breaking the norms of 1970s shojo manga through frank depictions of female sexuality; some feminist critics faulted the male readership's objectifying reception (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Magazine House (New Punch Zaurus); English by Vertical (now part of Kodansha USA; from general knowledge), rated 17+ in ANN's database.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If everything has a price, is there anything left that is not for sale? Pink asks this of a young woman who sells sex to feed a crocodile, and of everyone around her.

**The answer the story gives.** Love seems to be the exception, and then it is taken away. Yumi and Haru find a tenderness neither of them paid for, and the household of two lovers, a child and a crocodile looks for a while like a family (Wikipedia). The stepmother, who buys what she wants, destroys Croc out of jealousy, and chance kills Haru. Okazaki's own comment that love, like capitalism, is a fierce monster (Wikipedia, paraphrased) is the key: love is not outside the market, it is just as powerful and just as cruel.

**Characters as arguments.** Yumi argues for self-reliance without shame (AIPT). Haru argues that art and love can be assembled from what is at hand: his prize-winning novel is cut and pasted from other books (Wikipedia). The stepmother argues for possession: a husband kept for money, a lover kept for pleasure, a pet destroyed for revenge. Keiko, the child who sees everything, is the book's quiet witness and its most unsettling figure (ANN).

**Symbols and motifs.** Pink itself, reclaimed as a color for adult women (Wikipedia). The crocodile, who must be fed endlessly, as appetite, autonomy or capitalism (Sequential State). The suitcase made from Croc: a living thing turned into a luxury good. The Snow White pattern of the jealous stepmother and the "mirror" (ANN). The tropical island that is never reached.

**Traditions it echoes.** The fairy tale (Snow White), bubble-era social satire, and the josei manga it helped create (Wikipedia). Religious readers may hear Ecclesiastes ("all is vanity") or the Buddhist teaching on impermanence in its ending, though the book itself draws no religious lesson.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Consumerism is critiqued by being shown in full. Hedonism is explored without judgment. Feminist readings disagree: Spies sees liberation, other critics objectification (Wikipedia). Nihilism waits at the end, when chance takes everything.

**Shifts across the series.** A single volume of 20 chapters: comic and sexy for most of its length, it turns sharply to loss in its last chapters (Wikipedia; MyAnimeList readers call the ending abrupt).

**Discussion questions** (for adult readers or older teens with a parent).
1. Yumi works to feed Croc. What does Croc stand for?
2. Is Yumi's relationship with Haru different from her work? How?
3. The stepmother buys a young lover and destroys Croc. Is she simply the villain?
4. Haru wins a prize for a novel built from other people's words. What does the book think about that?
5. Keiko sees everything the adults do. What does the book suggest about children in this world?
