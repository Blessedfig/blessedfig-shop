**Premise.** Takao Kasuga is a bookish middle schooler in a small mountain town in Gunma who prides himself on reading Baudelaire. On impulse he steals the gym clothes of Nanako Saeki, the classmate he idolizes. Sawa Nakamura, a sullen outcast, saw him do it, and she blackmails him into a "contract" of humiliating tasks meant to strip away his pretense of being normal. At the same time Kasuga starts dating Saeki. The pair's escalating acts (vandalizing the classroom, running away, a hideout decorated with stolen underwear) lead to arson, a scandal and a plan to set themselves on fire on a float at the summer festival, which Nakamura aborts by pushing Kasuga off. The second half follows Kasuga in high school in Saitama, years later. A new classmate, Aya Tokiwa, revives his love of literature, and he eventually travels to find Nakamura, reconciles with his past and begins to write (Wikipedia; chapter list).

## Life message

**The series does not hold up a single good life. It shows the adolescent wish to escape an empty, conformist world ("the other side of the mountain") as real and understandable, follows it to the edge of death, and then suggests that a livable life comes from facing what one did, accepting it, and making something (writing, a relationship) out of it.** One MyAnimeList reviewer sums the second half as: the past cannot be erased, only accepted (opened, reader review).

## Creator and publisher context

- Shūzō Oshimi was born in 1981 and grew up in the countryside of Kiryū, Gunma, the setting of the series (Wikipedia). Other works include *Inside Mari*, *Happiness*, *Blood on the Tracks* and *Welcome Back, Alice* (Wikipedia).
- He first read *Les Fleurs du mal* in middle school and found it "suspicious, indecent, yet nastily noble." The eye-flower motif comes from an Odilon Redon illustration for the book (Wikipedia).
- The settings are based on his hometown. Nakamura is partly based on a person who insulted him, and Kasuga's friend Hiruta on a middle-school friend who betrayed him. He wanted to explore "perversion" as a hidden trait in everyone and where adolescence ends (Wikipedia, citing interviews).
- 2024 ANN interview (opened): "Flowers of Evil is the first manga where I felt I was able to fully express myself." He imagines his reader as "myself when I was 14," calls his protagonists "projections of myself," and hopes younger readers see beyond the world they were given. Asked about recurring sexual themes, he called them "kind of a rebellion towards my parents." He also said he wanted to express "my own experience of having some ambiguity about my own gender" in his work (in context of later series). The article reports that he has been working through anxiety by expressing it in his work.
- He approved of the anime's rotoscoping and preferred it to anime-style drawings (Wikipedia).
- No statement by Oshimi about religion or politics was found in the pages opened. This entry does not discuss his private life beyond what he has said on the record. No widely reported personal controversy was found.
- Publishers: Kodansha (Japan). Vertical (English) is now part of Kodansha USA (from general knowledge; to verify); the series page is on kodansha.us. Sentai Filmworks licenses the anime.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is there a real self under the mask of being "normal," and is it something to escape into or something to live with? Kasuga believes he is a refined reader of Baudelaire. One impulsive theft shows him a self he cannot fit into that picture, and Nakamura insists that this hidden "pervert" is the truth about him, and about everyone.

**The answer the story gives.** The first arc follows Nakamura's answer to its limit. If the world is full of hypocritical "shitbugs," the only honest act is to break out, and the logic leads to a planned self-immolation on a festival float. ANN's reviewer calls this "the extreme limit" of the arc's "downward spiral" (vol. 7). Then the story turns. Nakamura pushes Kasuga off; the act is not completed. The second arc gives a different answer: the self one is ashamed of is real, but it is not the whole person. Facing it, confessing it to people who matter, and making something from it allows a life. One reader puts it simply: the past cannot be erased, only accepted (MAL review).

**Characters as arguments.** Kasuga is the argument that a romantic self-image can hide ordinary desire and cowardice, and that growing up means owning both. Nakamura is the argument that a hypocritical world deserves contempt; the story treats her with tenderness, and its last chapter is told from her side. Saeki is the argument that the "good girl" is also a person with hunger and self-hatred; her assault on Kasuga (vol. 5) shows the ideal breaking. Tokiwa is the argument that literature can be a way into life rather than out of it.

**Symbols and motifs.** Baudelaire's *Les Fleurs du mal* gives the title and the theme of beauty found in corruption. The eye-flower, taken from Odilon Redon's illustration, recurs as a watching, judging presence (Wikipedia). The mountains around the town stand for the walls of a small world, and "the other side" for escape. The stolen gym clothes are the original sin of the story. The festival float is the stage where the private wish to burn the world becomes public.

**Traditions it echoes.** French decadent and symbolist poetry (Baudelaire, Redon); the Japanese tradition of confessional fiction; existentialist novels of guilt and self-invention; the coming-of-age story. Oshimi names Tetsu Adachi's *Song of Cherry Blossoms* and other manga as influences (Wikipedia), and says his protagonists are "projections of myself" (ANN 2024).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is given its best voice in Nakamura and followed to its conclusion, then quietly set aside rather than defeated in argument. Expressive individualism, the hope of shedding the mask, is shown as partly true and partly destructive. Existentialism shapes the second arc: Kasuga chooses who he will be. Religion appears only through Baudelaire's vocabulary of sin and in Kasuga's talk of atonement.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-3 (ch. 1-17): theft, contract, vandalism and running away, with dark comedy. Vol. 4-7 (ch. 18-33): the hideout, the assault and arson, and the festival climax. Vol. 7-10 (ch. 33-52): a quiet high-school arc of guilt, books and a new relationship; the art becomes more realistic (ANN vol. 7). Vol. 11 (ch. 53-57): the beach reckoning, a dream of everyone's futures and Nakamura's own view. The anime stops at the end of the first arc, so viewers of the anime alone see the descent without the recovery.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kasuga thinks he is different because he reads Baudelaire. What changes when he learns he is not as different as he thought?
2. Nakamura calls everyone a "shitbug." Is she right about anything? What does her view leave out?
3. Who was harmed by Kasuga's theft, including the girls who never appear on the page? What would making it right look like?
4. Saeki and Nakamura both pressure Kasuga in ways he does not agree to. How should a story, or a friend, respond when a boy is coerced?
5. The festival plan is stopped. If someone you know talked about wanting to "go to the other side," who could they talk to? What does your family's tradition say about hope?
