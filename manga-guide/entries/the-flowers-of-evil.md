**Premise.** Takao Kasuga, a bookish middle-school boy in a small town in Gunma, loves Baudelaire's The Flowers of Evil and idolizes his classmate Nanako Saeki. One evening he steals her gym clothes on impulse (ch. 1). Sawa Nakamura, a classmate who despises the town and everyone in it, saw him, and blackmails him into a "contract" to strip away his respectable "skin" (ch. 2-3). Kasuga starts dating Saeki while being pulled deeper into Nakamura's acts of defiance: vandalizing their classroom at night (v2), running away toward the mountains (v3), stealing classmates' underwear for a secret hideout (v4). Saeki sexually assaults Kasuga in the hideout and burns it down (v5). The arc ends when Kasuga and Nakamura try to set themselves on fire on a festival float; Nakamura pushes him off and her father stops her (v6-7). Years later, in high school in Saitama, Kasuga meets Aya Tokiwa, a classmate who writes fiction, falls in love, and finally visits Nakamura to put the past to rest (v7-11) (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Growing up means facing the darkness in yourself honestly instead of worshiping an ideal or trying to escape through destruction; an ordinary life shared with real people is the "other side" worth reaching.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Puberty is the engine: Kasuga's shame at sexual desire for the girl he idealizes, Nakamura's disgust at a world she sees as driven by sex (Wikipedia), and rivalry between two girls over one boy. Oshimi says he wanted to ask where adolescence ends (Wikipedia, Themes). |
| Pragmatic | Every attempt to escape by force fails: the bike ride (v3), the hideout (v4-5) and the festival (v6-7) end with police, fire and a family that has to move. What works is slow and ordinary: a new school, a friend who lends books, writing (v7-11). |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot. The frame is Baudelaire, whose poems turn on sin, spleen and the ideal; chapter titles borrow his poems, including "De Profundis" (ch. 17). Faith readers will find a serious picture of shame, guilt, confession and recovery, alongside sexual transgression and a planned suicide, with no religious answer offered. |
| Socio-cultural | A portrait of suffocating small-town life and adolescent alienation, drawn from Oshimi's own hometown and youth: Nakamura is based on someone who used to insult him in her words (Wikipedia, Production). Reviewers read it as a study of shame (The Manga Critic) and of masks and the hidden self (Ristola, via Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the first half ends in ruin and a near-suicide, and both girls are hurt, but Kasuga recovers, finds love and starts to write, Saeki and Nakamura find their own peace, and the last chapter shows that Kasuga gave Nakamura's world its color.
Hope source: L — love between two: Kasuga's way out is his relationship with Tokiwa, built on shared books and honesty about his past (v8-11); writing and an ordinary family life (F) are the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame finds the story's moral arc serious: hidden wrongdoing grows, is exposed, ruins lives and is slowly repaired, and an ordinary life is chosen over the fantasy of escape. The split is over what a reader must pass through to get there. Secular, Buddhist and some Catholic and Reformed readings value the honest portrait of shame and desire (A or B); holiness, Adventist, Witness, Latter-day Saint, Muslim and traditional readers weigh the long sympathetic voicing of nihilism, the sexual content involving 14-year-olds and the suicide plan more heavily (C). All frames treat the explicitness itself as a Discuss or Significant concern.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

No one wins in the first arc: every attempt at escape ends in exposure, fire or the police. In the second, those who let go of fantasies and face the truth find a quieter happiness.

## Creator and publisher context

- Shūzō Oshimi, born 1981 in Gunma; grew up in Kiryū, whose riverbank, school and park appear in the manga; won the Tetsuya Chiba Award in 2001 and debuted in Monthly Shōnen Magazine (Wikipedia). Known for stories drawn from his own experience about puberty, psychosexual development and coming of age (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent (via Wikipedia, citing Japanese interviews): he first read Baudelaire in middle school and found it "suspicious, indecent, yet nastily noble"; he wanted to treat "perversion" as a hidden quality of everyone rather than something misunderstood; and he wanted to ask where adolescence ends, which he believes each person must discover. Influences include Tetsu Adachi's Song of Cherry Blossoms and Odilon Redon, whose flower illustration became the series' eye-flower. Nakamura is based on a person who used to call Oshimi "shitbug"; another character is based on a friend who betrayed him to a bully.
- Oshimi approved the anime's rotoscoping and said it matched his own aim of figuratively wounding the reader (Natalie interview, via Wikipedia). ANN's This Week in Anime (2026) notes his confessional afterwords in later works and his public statements about ambiguous feelings about his own gender (work-related, from his afterwords).
- No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (no full English interview about this series was opened; Anime Feminist's interview covers later work only).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Bessatsu Shōnen Magazine). English: Vertical (print, later part of Kodansha USA) and Kodansha Comics (digital), rated 16+ on Kodansha USA's site.
- No controversies involving the creator were found. The anime's rotoscoping was controversial among manga fans (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What lies under the "skin" we show the world, and is it the truer self? Kasuga believes he is special because he reads Baudelaire; one stolen bag of gym clothes shows him he is not who he thought. Nakamura offers him a way out: strip off every layer of respectability and reach "the other side." The series asks whether that is liberation or self-destruction, and where adolescence ends.

**The answer the story gives.** Neither the ideal nor the abyss is the answer. Kasuga's worship of Saeki as an angel and Nakamura's contempt for everyone are two ways of not seeing real people. Every escape fails, ending in fire, police and a family uprooted (v3-7). Kasuga heals only by telling the truth to someone who sees him as he is (Tokiwa, v10) and by facing Nakamura one last time (v11). He ends by writing, turning his darkness into a book (ch. 56).

**Characters as arguments.** Kasuga argues for the cultivated, superior self and learns its emptiness. Nakamura argues that everyone is a fraud and that destruction is honesty; the story gives her a full hearing, saves her life and lets her find peace without converting her. Saeki argues for being loved as she really is, and her collapse into assault and arson (v5) shows what that wish becomes when it turns possessive. Tokiwa argues that literature can connect people instead of setting them apart. Kinoshita is plain moral sense, and she pays for it by being left behind (v10).

**Symbols and motifs.** Redon's eye-flower, which wilts in Kasuga's final dream and leaves no scar on his hand (v11). The mountain on the edge of town that no one crosses (v3). The hideout and its fire (v4-5). The festival float (v6-7). The sea at Chōshi, where the last meeting happens (v11). Monochrome versus color in the last chapter, where Kasuga gives Nakamura's world its color (ch. 57).

**Traditions it echoes.** Baudelaire's spleen and ideal and the Romantic outsider; the Japanese coming-of-age story of shame (Adachi's Song of Cherry Blossoms); the film Don't Deliver Us from Evil, in which girls who worship Baudelaire fall into ruin (Wikipedia). For religious readers it echoes the old teaching that hidden sin grows in the dark and loses its power when confessed.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is given its strongest voice and then refused. Existentialism shapes the whole: identity is chosen, painfully. Expressive individualism is treated with suspicion, since the "true self" can be cruel. Secular humanism wins quietly: love, honesty and art bring Kasuga back. Faith is absent as an answer but present as imagery.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-22) are the blackmail and the love triangle, adapted in the anime. Volumes 5-7 (ch. 23-35, likely) bring the assault, the fire and the festival. Volumes 7-11 (from about ch. 34 to 57) jump to high school in Saitama: slower, quieter, about recovery and love, ending with the visit to Nakamura, an epilogue dream and a last chapter from Nakamura's eyes.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kasuga felt superior because of what he read. What happened to that feeling?
2. Nakamura says everyone is a fake. Is she right about anyone? Is she right about herself?
3. Every attempt to escape the town ends badly. What finally helps Kasuga?
4. What does Saeki want, and why does it go so wrong?
5. Kasuga tells Tokiwa the truth about his past before they visit Nakamura. Why does that matter?
