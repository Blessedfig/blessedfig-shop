**Premise.** A Japanese woman who loved villainesses wakes as Alicia Williams, 7, the noble villainess of her favorite otome (dating) game, and resolves to become the greatest villainess in history. She trains her body, studies a hundred books a day and turns out to be a "divergent," a mage of rare talent. Visiting Roana, a walled village of exiled criminals and their families near her home, she sees a boy, Gill, beaten nearly to death and saves him with the help of Will, a blind old man; Gill becomes her retainer (v1, likely ch. 2-6). Her blunt criticism of the kingdom's rigid aristocracy impresses the king, who at 13 enrolls her early at the magic academy to be the "watcher" of Liz Cather, the idealistic commoner saint and heroine of the game, and to toughen her up by playing the villain (v2, ch. 7-10). After a kidnapping, Alicia trains for two years in seclusion, gives one of her eyes to Will by high-level magic so he can see, and returns with an eyepatch (v3-4, ch. 11-20). At school she exposes doctored evidence, defends a bullied girl, and helps reveal that Liz has been unconsciously charming people with magic; Crown Prince Duke, about five years older, declares his love. To be free to explore the neighboring country of Laval, Alicia lets herself be exiled (likely ch. 21-25). Throughout, every "villainous" act makes people admire her more (Wikipedia episode summaries; Yen Press volume blurbs; LN reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it better to be good, or to look good? Alicia wants to be remembered as a villain, but everything she does (studying, training, saving a beaten boy, feeding an exiled village) is good. Liz, the saint, looks good and means well, but her idealism and an unconscious charm cause harm.

**The answer the story gives.** Deeds over appearances, realism over wishful kindness. The author wanted to set Liz's idealism against Alicia's pragmatism (Suitable for Treatment), and the story rewards Alicia's blunt honesty, hard work and willingness to be disliked. In the end Alicia tells Liz to stop playing the ideal saint and be herself (ep. 12).

**Characters as arguments.** Alicia argues for hard truth and hard work. Liz argues for kindness without knowledge, and learns its limits. Gill argues that one rescued life can become loyal strength. Will, blinded and exiled for a crime he did not commit, argues that the cast-out may hold wisdom. Duke argues for devotion, though reviewers find his pursuit of a much younger Alicia uncomfortable (ANN; Suitable for Treatment).

**Symbols and motifs.** The villainess's black dress; the eyepatch, worn proudly after she gives an eye away (ep. 8); the golden rose of the saint and the black rose of the one who will change the country (ep. 13); the wall around Roana, broken at last (ep. 13).

**Traditions it echoes.** The villainess-reincarnation genre, which usually has the villainess avoid her doom; here she embraces it. Noblesse oblige and the reforming aristocrat. The Machiavellian advisor (Yen Press calls her a "Machiavellian maiden") who does good by seeming bad.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism wins every argument. Expressive individualism is affirmed in Alicia's freedom. Class reform is affirmed. Romance follows shojo and josei habits that reviewers question when the heroine is a child.

**Shifts across the series.** v1 (ch. 1-6): childhood, training, Roana and Gill. v2 (ch. 7-10): the king's offer and the academy. v3 (ch. 11-15): kidnapping and the level-90 ultimatum. v4 (ch. 16-20): training complete, the eye, Duke. v5 onward (ch. 21-): Liz's charm exposed, exile, Laval (Yen Press blurbs; anime summaries).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Alicia wants to be a villain but keeps doing good. What makes someone a villain: actions, reputation or intentions?
2. Liz means well. How can good intentions cause harm?
3. Alicia gives up an eye for Will. Was that wise, brave or reckless?
4. Duke is much older than Alicia when he first shows interest. How should the story have handled that?
