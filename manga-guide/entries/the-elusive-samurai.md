**Premise.** In 1333 Ashikaga Takauji betrays the Kamakura shogunate, and its eight-year-old heir, Hojo Tokiyuki, sees his clan die (ch. 1). Tokiyuki is weak at fighting but has an uncanny gift for running and hiding. Suwa Yorishige, the eccentric priest-lord of Suwa Shrine who can see the future, hides him in Shinano and prophesies that he will shake the realm. With a band of young retainers (the strategist Fubuki, the swordsman Kojiro, the strong girl Ayako, the thief Genba and Yorishige's daughter Shizuku) Tokiyuki outwits local enemies, including the archer-governor Ogasawara Sadamune, the bandit chief Shokan (v3) and the tyrannical governor Kiyohara (v4-9). In 1335 he leads the Nakasendai Rebellion and retakes Kamakura, but Takauji crushes it and Yorishige dies (about v8-13). Tokiyuki joins the Southern Court army of Kitabatake Akiie (v14-20), takes Kamakura again, and later, as an adult, marries Shizuku, Ayako and Mima (v21-22). After the Battle of Musashino (1352) he is captured; to end the fighting he chooses to be executed, as history records, in 1353 (v27) (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; VIZ).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it ever braver to run than to fight? In 1333 the honorable choice for a defeated samurai was death; Tokiyuki, eight years old, runs instead, and the story asks whether that can make a hero.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, when you run to live and to protect others. Tokiyuki's escapes save his friends, villages and even enemies; he talks warriors out of throwing their lives away (ch. 26-31); and at the end he stops running and gives himself up so the fighting can stop (v27).

**Characters as arguments.** Tokiyuki argues for life; Takauji for power that needs no reason, and the story reveals him as possessed and empty; Yorishige for faith joined to love; Shokan for the belief that no Buddha exists, until he meets the boy; Kiyohara for authority without justice; Kojiro, born of a crime, for loyalty freely chosen.

**Symbols and motifs.** Hide-and-seek, the game of the opening; the "oni" faces of the villains; the Suwa Shrine pillars (onbashira); the shaved head promised at Tokiyuki's coming of age; the arrow that frees Takauji from his god.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese war tales (the Taiheiki), Matsui's own comic shonen style, and for religious readers the stories of those who fled persecution to survive (Joseph; the holy family fleeing to Egypt).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Bushido is split: loyalty affirmed, the cult of a glorious death critiqued. Pragmatism wins battles. Might makes right is defeated morally though Takauji wins historically. The gods are real, but human choices decide the meaning.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4 (1333-1334): escape and training in Suwa; vols. 5-13 (1335): the Nakasendai Rebellion and Yorishige's death; vols. 14-20 (1337-1338): war with the Southern Court; vols. 21-22: time skips and marriage; vols. 23-27 (1351-1353): the final wars and Tokiyuki's death (chapter list).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tokiyuki's family chose death over capture. Why does the story treat his running away as courage?
2. Shokan did terrible things but was spared. Was that the right choice?
3. Takauji is kind and charming but betrays everyone. What makes a leader trustworthy?
4. Tokiyuki starts out wanting revenge. Does he still want it at the end?
5. How much of the story is real history? What would you look up first?
