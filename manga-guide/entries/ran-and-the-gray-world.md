**Premise.** Ran Uruma, a fourth-grader of about ten in the town of Haimachi (No Flying No Tights; Smart Bitches, Trashy Books), comes from a family of sorcerers: her mother Shizuka is a powerful sorceress whose work away from home matters to the whole world, her father Zen is a calligrapher, and her older brother Jin can become a wolf with a fur cloak. Ran misses her mother and cannot wait to grow up. A pair of magic sneakers turns her into a grown woman, and in that body she runs off on her own and crash-lands in the penthouse garden of Otaro Mikado, a wealthy playboy (SKJAM!; ANN). Otaro, an adult (in his thirties, per Smart Bitches, Trashy Books), falls for her adult form and keeps pursuing her. In vols. 2-3 magical insects invade; Otaro is stung and falls into a deep sleep, and Ran enters his dreams to save him (No Flying No Tights; Comic Book Bin). Shizuka comes home, Jin finds love, and Ran's classmate Makoto Hibi loves her. In the last volume (ch. 38-45) Ran wakes from a long sleep grieving Otaro's loss; her sorrow, through her magic, spreads over the whole town, and with Hibi's and her family's support she learns to grow up at a normal pace (VIZ; I Read Sous).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is it to be grown up? Ran can make her body grown with a pair of sneakers; the series asks whether that makes her an adult, and what happens when adults treat her as one.

**The answer the story gives.** Being grown is not a body but a heart formed over time. Ran's adventures in adult form bring an adult man's attentions she cannot handle, her rescue of him leads to bad decisions, and the last volume opens on grief (I Read Sous). Irie's own summary is that growing up is change and choice (VIZ blog): Ran learns to choose to grow at her own pace.

**Characters as arguments.** Ran argues for impatience and courage. Otaro argues for adult appetite without restraint; reviewers disagree whether the story critiques him enough (ANN; Smart Bitches, Trashy Books; No Flying No Tights). Jin and Zen argue for protection; Shizuka for power used in service of others. Hibi argues for patient love among equals, and he is the one at Ran's side at the end (VIZ).

**Symbols and motifs.** The magic sneakers (a shortcut to adulthood); the "gray world" of the title and the town of Haimachi (likely "ash town," from general knowledge; to verify); Jin's wolf cloak; insects that invade dreams; tears that flood a town.

**Traditions it echoes.** Fairy tales of children who wish to grow up (Big, Peter Pan in reverse), Japanese witch stories, and the old warning, shared by many traditions, that each stage of life has its season.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism wins: family and town carry Ran through. Pagan/occult imagery is the world's texture and is shown as good. Expressive individualism is explored and shown to have limits. Feminist readers praise the girl hero and object to how the romance treats her.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: Ran's escapes and meeting Otaro. Vols. 2-3 (to ch. 18): the insect invasion, Otaro's sleep, the dream rescue, Shizuka's return, Jin's romance. Vols. 4-6: the deepening triangle and the invasion's climax (thin evidence). Vol. 7 (ch. 38-45): grief, Hibi and growing up.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ran wanted to be grown up right away. What is good about being a kid that she missed?
2. Otaro knew Ran was really a child. What should an adult do in that situation?
3. Ran's family kept bringing her home. Were they right? How did she feel about it?
4. Irie says growing up is about change and choice. What choices did Ran make that helped her grow?
5. Why do you think Hibi was the one at Ran's side at the end?
