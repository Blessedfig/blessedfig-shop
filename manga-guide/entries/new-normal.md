**Premise.** A little before Hata's generation was born, a pandemic changed the world. In near-future Tokyo, everyone wears a mask, and young people who have never seen their friends' faces feel the mouth as something intimate, even sexual; for unmarried boys and girls to show each other their mouths is taboo (ITmedia Nlab). Hata catches his classmate Natsuki drinking water with her mask off. Natsuki, who longs for the world before the pandemic, starts a secret arrangement with him: in private they show each other their faces (vol. 1, ch. 1-7). Tokyo is sealed behind a quarantine wall, and what lies beyond it is classified (ITmedia Nlab; Kana gives the year as 2042). The story widens: Hata is quarantined as a close contact of the V-21 virus and befriends another patient, Nanami Shiina, by video call (vol. 2); Natsuki is drawn by Airi Mochizuki into a "nostalgia salon" and an anti-mask group, and envoys arrive from beyond the wall (vol. 3); the anti-mask group TEETH and the people beyond the wall bring a new threat of pandemic (vol. 4); Hata and Natsuki chase the kidnapped Erika Hinata into a ruined district where an armed "black quarantine squad" opens fire (vol. 5-6); the survivors return to life under lockdown, each having lost something (vol. 7); Hata and Natsuki drift apart through things left unsaid (vol. 8); Natsuki is taken to a quarantine facility that turns out to be beyond the wall, and the facility is attacked (vol. 9) (Manga Oukoku volume synopses).

## Life message

**Real closeness means risking honesty with another person, even in a world built on hiding; questioning the rules you were raised in, and the people who hide the truth behind them, is part of growing up.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | When faces are hidden, the mouth becomes a mating signal: ITmedia notes the young feel sexual attraction to it and shame when it is seen. A love triangle (Hata, Natsuki, Erika; Kana) plays out under rules that keep the sexes apart. |
| Pragmatic | Masks, quarantine and walls are shown as public-health tools that have become a way of life; the story asks which are still needed and who decides (Kana). Characters survive by cooperating (the rescue of Erika, vol. 5-6). |
| Religious | No religion appears in sources opened. The mouth taboo works like a modesty code (only married couples may see each other's mouths; ITmedia), so faith readers may read it as a mirror of their own modesty customs. |
| Socio-cultural | A COVID-era fable. Kana's feature says the story distrusts official information, criticizes a society that follows rules without asking why, and criticizes two extremes: government secrecy and anti-mask radicalism. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the romance is tender and the friends survive the ruined district, but each loses something (vol. 7), Hata and Natsuki drift apart (vol. 8), and the latest volume leaves Natsuki held beyond the wall (vol. 9); the series is ongoing.
Hope source: L — the secret bond between Hata and Natsuki is the story's light in a closed world; friendship among the classmates (F) is the close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to love when faces are hidden? New Normal turns a safety measure into a modesty code, so that the plain act of showing your mouth becomes a confession. The deeper question is about truth: what else is being hidden, by whom, and why (ITmedia; Kana)?

**The answer the story gives.** Honest closeness is worth the risk. Hata and Natsuki's small secret (vol. 1) grows into a willingness to face larger hidden truths: the quarantine wards (vol. 2), the anti-mask underground (vol. 3-4), the ruined district beyond the wall (vol. 5-6). The story so far does not side with the rule-breakers wholesale: Kana's reading is that it criticizes extremes on both sides.

**Characters as arguments.** Natsuki argues for remembering what was lost. Hata argues for caution that learns courage. Nanami, the patient behind glass (vol. 2), argues that connection is possible even at a distance. TEETH argues for throwing off the rules and is shown as dangerous; the black quarantine squad argues for order kept by secrecy and force and is shown as worse.

**Symbols and motifs.** The mask and the mouth. The wall around Tokyo. Video calls across a sickroom (vol. 2). Drones (Erika's club). The "world before," seen in old films (Kana).

**Traditions it echoes.** Dystopian fiction about walled cities and hidden truths, the Japanese school romance, and COVID-era fiction (my own framing; no source opened makes these comparisons). Its modesty code echoes real traditions in which faces or bodies are veiled before marriage.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarian order is critiqued; individual connection is affirmed; the utilitarian trade between safety and freedom is left as an open question in the sources opened.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-7): the secret and the romance. Vol. 2 (ch. 8-14): quarantine. Vol. 3-4 (ch. 15-28): the salon, TEETH and the envoys. Vol. 5-6 (ch. 29-42): kidnapping and the ruined district, the most violent stretch. Vol. 7-8 (ch. 43-56): grief, lockdown and the couple's drift. Vol. 9 (ch. 57-63): Natsuki beyond the wall.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does showing your mouth feel like a confession in Hata's world? What in our world feels that private?
2. Is keeping the face-showing secret a betrayal of the rules, or a way of being honest with each other?
3. Some characters reject masks entirely. What does the story say about them?
4. Who decides which safety rules stay? What should they have to explain?
5. What would you miss most if you could never see your friends' faces?
