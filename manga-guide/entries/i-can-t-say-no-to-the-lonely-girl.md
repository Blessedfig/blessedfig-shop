**Premise.** Ayaka Sakurai is a model student and class representative who froze in her high school entrance exam and wants to become a teacher like her brother. Her homeroom teacher, Ms. Egawa, offers her a university recommendation letter if she can get the chronically absent Sora Honda back to school. Sora overhears the deal and uses it as leverage: she will come to school if Ayaka grants one request a day, and her first request is a kiss (ch. 1). Ayaka tutors Sora, learns during a storm sleepover that Sora has lived alone for years since her parents' divorce, and slowly realizes Sora has loved her since middle school (vols. 1-2). After Sora faints at sports day, Ayaka apologizes for having approached her for selfish reasons and asks to start over as friends (vol. 3). When Sora's mother returns to move her away, Ayaka confronts the mother and confesses her love at the airport; the mother lets Sora stay (vols. 3-4). Friends Wakana and Yuina help the couple and later become a couple themselves; Wakana confesses to an old friend who cannot return her feelings and they stay friends (vol. 4). Ayaka beats her exam nerves, enters the university she wanted without the recommendation, and after graduation lives with Sora, who attends culinary school (vols. 5-6) (Wikipedia; Kodansha synopses).

## Creator and publisher context

- Kashikaze (story and art) decided to become a manga artist after quitting a job she disliked; she chose a school setting on the advice of an online article about classic yuri, and added the secret agreement between two girls as her own element (Wikipedia, citing a 2024 video interview). She says she created Sora first, from her own preferences and personality, then Ayaka to pair with her, and that early drafts made Sora much more forward before it was toned down (Wikipedia, citing Yuri Navi 2021).
- On craft: she focused on the characters' psychology and drew emotions through hand gestures, and for romantic scenes aimed for an atmosphere readers could follow (Wikipedia, citing Yuri Navi). No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Ichijinsha (Comic Yuri Hime, a magazine devoted to yuri). Kodansha USA published the English edition; its product pages rate volume 1 16+ and volumes 4-6 13+. Kodansha markets it as a "juicy yuri manga" (AniList, quoting Kodansha).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can something that begins as a transaction become love? Ayaka helps Sora for a recommendation letter; Sora gives Ayaka what she wants only in exchange for a daily request. Both are using the other, and both know it.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, but only when the transaction is dropped. The turning point is not a kiss but an apology: after Sora collapses at sports day, Ayaka admits she came for selfish reasons and asks to start again as friends (vol. 3). From there the story rewards plain honesty: confessing, standing up to Sora's mother, telling friends (vol. 6). Ayaka even passes her exam without the recommendation that started everything.

**Characters as arguments.** Ayaka argues that duty and ambition are not enough; she has to learn what she wants. Sora argues that loneliness makes people grab at love clumsily; her blackmail is the only way she knows to be near Ayaka. Wakana argues for saying your feelings before it is too late, and accepts a kind no. Ms. Egawa, comic and absurd, stands for a world where people are means to results, and the story quietly lets her lose the bargain.

**Symbols and motifs.** The daily request, a small contract that becomes a ritual of care. The stuffed chick Sora wins at the arcade (ch. 1). Sora's empty house. Hands: Kashikaze draws splayed fingers for inner turmoil (Yuri Navi). The airport, where leaving is stopped (vols. 3-4). The final line inverts the title: Ayaka still cannot say no, now freely.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese yuri romance (Comic Yuri Hime; Bloom Into You as the creator's model), and the 'fake relationship turns real' romantic comedy. For religious readers, the arc from using a person to loving her echoes teaching on repentance and right intention, while the romance itself is where traditions part.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and a progressive acceptance of same-sex love are affirmed without argument. Community is strong: friends carry both couples. Duty ethics enters through the plot rather than talk: treating a person as a means is the original wrong the story must undo.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-10) are the deal, the kisses and Sora's loneliness, the part reviewers found most uncomfortable. Volume 3 (ch. 11-15) is the apology, the mother and the climax. Volumes 4-6 (ch. 16-32) are a gentle couple story: friends' romances, exams, coming out to friends, and life after graduation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ayaka first helps Sora to get a recommendation letter. Is it wrong to help someone for a reward? When does it become wrong?
2. Sora's first request is a kiss she has not been given freely. How should a relationship begin?
3. Why does Ayaka's apology change things more than any of Sora's requests?
4. Sora's mother left her alone for years. What do children need from parents besides money?
5. Wakana tells an old friend how she feels, and the friend says no. Was it still worth saying?
