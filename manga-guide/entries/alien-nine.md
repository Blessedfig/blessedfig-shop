**Premise.** In 2014, sixteen years after first contact, aliens land at schools as a matter of routine. At Elementary School No. 9, timid twelve-year-old Yuri Otani is voted by her sixth-grade class, against her will, onto the "Alien Party" with the cheerful Kasumi Tomine and the capable, bossy Kumi Kawamura. They fight with Borgs, frog-like symbiotic aliens worn on the head that sprout drill-tendrils and lick their hosts' sweat. Their adviser, Ms. Hisakawa, is secretly a human fused with a Borg of the Drill Clan and is releasing the aliens herself: the Alien Party exists to raise hosts for fusion. Yuri's terror makes her Borg kill every alien in a storeroom and her friends' Borgs (vol. 1); Kasumi is swallowed by a "Yellow Knife" alien and fuses with it (vol. 2); Kumi is killed in the library and rebuilt by her Borg until her body is no longer human (vol. 2). With both friends changed, Yuri is the last possible host, and Kumi vows to protect her from any fusion. In the final term the Sunflower Clan invades, forcibly fusing with students; Kumi reaches into Yuri's mind and helps her throw the Sunflower off (vol. 3). The students may choose their junior high schools; the three girls move in together, and Yuri finds one lock of her hair curling into a drill (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Growing up is frightening change that adults may push on children for their own ends; holding on to yourself, and to friends who protect you, is how a child gets through it.**

| Evolutionary biology | Symbiosis and reproduction are the plot: alien clans compete for human hosts the way species compete for niches; Kumi's rebuilt body loses the capacity to reproduce and so is useless as a host (vol. 2). | Pragmatic | The adults are cold pragmatists: children are trained, tested and pushed into danger because it works for the clan. The girls survive by practical skill (Kumi) and by refusing (Yuri). The ending turns pragmatic too: the headmistress says letting humans choose among clans may work better. |
| Religious | No religion appears. The aliens' takeover of minds and bodies reads to some faith readers like possession, and the story values bodily integrity and the inner self. There is no God, prayer or afterlife; rescue comes from a friend's love reaching into the mind (vol. 3). |
| Socio-cultural | A dark send-up of Japanese school life: class elections, duty rosters and entrance exams become the machinery that feeds children to aliens. Outlook: Bittersweet — Yuri survives as herself and the three friends live together, but two of them are no longer fully human, the adults face no real reckoning, and the final drill-shaped curl says the change is coming anyway.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to a child when the adults in charge want her to become something else? Yuri is twelve, frightened of everything, and elected by her classmates to a job she never wanted. The aliens she is told to fight are, in the end, what the school wants her to become.

**The answer the story gives.** A child can say no, and a friend can help her hold on. Kumi and Kasumi do their duty and pay with their bodies; Yuri refuses, and with Kumi's help keeps her self (vol. 3). But the answer is not a clean victory. The adults keep their posts, the system only adjusts (perhaps humans should be allowed to choose their clan), and the last page shows one curl of Yuri's hair turning into a drill. Change is coming; the question is whether it will be chosen.

**Characters as arguments.** Yuri argues that fear is not weakness and that refusal is a right. Kumi argues for duty and protective love, and becomes the proof that duty can cost everything. Kasumi argues for cheerful acceptance and is swallowed by her own loneliness. Ms. Hisakawa argues that children are a resource for a larger purpose; the story lets her speak calmly and shows the wreckage. The headmistress, who suggests choice, is the system learning to be more persuasive rather than more just.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Borg as a hat that licks your sweat: something intimate placed on a child by adults. Drills, which pierce and enter. The storeroom where the children are cornered. The Yellow Knife that swallows a lonely girl and becomes her brother. The final curl of hair. Critics read many of these as images of puberty or sexual violation (SFE; Thompson); others read them as posthuman change. The story allows several readings.

**Traditions it echoes.** The body-horror tradition of Otomo (Akira), whom Tomizawa names as an influence, and the hard science fiction of Niven and Heinlein; the Japanese "magical girl" team, turned inside out. Its moral center, that those who hold power over children will answer for how they use it, is shared by almost every tradition; its refusal to show that answer is what makes it horror.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is explored with dread: merging with another being is power and loss at once. Authoritarianism is the enemy, though it is never overthrown. Existentialism shows in Yuri's choice to remain herself. Liberalism's idea of consent is the story's moral line. Utilitarian reasoning belongs to the adults and is shown to be cruel.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 is the setup and the storeroom catastrophe, with a summer break. Volume 2 brings the Yellow Knife, Kumi's death and rebuilding, and the Forest of Spaceships (the OVA ends partway through). Volume 3 is the Sunflower invasion and the ending. The sequel Emulators (2002) moves to junior high, reveals more of the adults' scheme and ends on a happier note (TV Tropes).

4. What does Yuri's refusal to merge mean to you? When is it right to say no to adults?
5. Some critics think the aliens stand for growing up. What else could they stand for?
