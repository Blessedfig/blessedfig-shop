**Premise.** At 17, aspiring manga artist Rohan Kishibe spends a summer at his grandmother's old inn, where the only other boarder is Nanase Fujikura, a moody, beautiful 21-year-old who says she is about to be divorced. He sketches her without asking, she confronts him, and an uneasy friendship and a crush follow. She tells him of the blackest painting in the world, made by the painter Nizaemon Yamamura with a pigment from a sacred ancient tree he cut down; he was executed for it and his works were cursed (NBM's blurb says the Emperor ordered it; the fan wiki says the tree was protected and a lord had him killed). After a tearful return, Nanase destroys the drawings he made of her and vanishes. Ten years later, famous, Rohan goes to Paris. With an interpreter, Emma Noguchi, a curator and two firemen, he descends to an abandoned storage vault deep beneath the Louvre. The painting raises the dead of each person's bloodline and their worst memories: the firemen are shot by ghosts from their past, the curator is crushed by a car-crash victim, and Noguchi is killed by the drowned son she neglected. Rohan escapes by using his power to erase his own memory. He learns that Nanase was Nizaemon's widow and his own ancestor, whose ghost had worked to lay her husband's hatred to rest; she and the painter are freed (JoJo wiki; CBR; Experiments in Manga).

## Life message

**The past lives on in our blood and memories: hatred can reach across centuries and destroy those who come near it, and it is love and the willingness to let go, not curiosity, that finally lays it to rest.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure (Weekly Shonen Jump, from 1987; later Ultra Jump) (from general knowledge; to verify). In 2009 he was one of five artists featured in the Louvre's comics exhibition, the first manga artist in its comics series (Wikipedia; Experiments in Manga).
- On-record intent (Ultra Jump interview, 2010, translated on the JoJo wiki): the Louvre asked for a new character, but he used Rohan to save pages of introduction; he chose the basement because he wondered where unseen paintings are stored; he colored each part differently (sepia for Japan, pink for Paris, blue underground); he drew Japanese details on purpose for French readers; and he mixed French "erotic suspense" film moods into the first-love story.
- It was his first full-color story, hand-colored in acrylics (Otaku USA).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it cost to see something no one is meant to see? Rohan wants to look at the blackest painting in the world, the way he wants to look at everything, and the story asks whether the past should be dug up.

**The answer the story gives.** Some things should be let go. The painting holds a man's hatred and turns each viewer's memories against them; Rohan survives only by giving up his own memories for a moment. What finally ends the curse is Nanase's long love for her husband and her care for her descendant (JoJo wiki).

**Characters as arguments.** Rohan argues for experience and art at any cost, and learns their price. Nanase argues for love that protects even by hurting: tearing up his drawings to keep him from loving her too much. Noguchi shows how guilt can be used against us. Nizaemon is art turned to hatred by injustice.

**Symbols and motifs.** Black as the absence of light; the painting as memory; the descent from the bright Louvre to its forgotten tunnels; the book as a person's life (Rohan's power); the sacred tree. Araki's palette marks the shift from warm memory to cold present (interview; Manga Bookshelf).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese kaidan and Kwaidan (Manga Bookshelf), the cursed-painting tale, and Araki's own JoJo body horror (The Comics Journal via NBM). Its message that hatred binds and love frees speaks to many religious readers, though they will judge the ghosts and curse differently.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult lens is explored as atmosphere and danger, not endorsed as practice. Expressive individualism is admired in Rohan but shown to have costs. The ancestor theme gives the book its emotional turn.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 (the inn) is a nostalgic first-love story; part 2 (Paris) is a museum tour; part 3 (the vault) is horror and resolution (JoJo wiki; Araki interview).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Nanase tear up Rohan's drawings? Was it cruel or kind?
2. Rohan chose not to read Nanase's mind. Why does that matter?
3. The curse used each person's memories against them. Are there memories worth letting go?
4. Was the painter wronged, or did he do wrong by cutting the sacred tree? Can both be true?
5. Should Rohan have opened the vault? Who paid for his curiosity?
