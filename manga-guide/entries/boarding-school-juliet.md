**Premise.** Dahlia Academy is a boarding school for students from two hostile countries, the eastern Nation of Touwa and the Principality of West. The students live in segregated dorms, the Black Dogs (Touwa) and the White Cats (West), and brawl constantly. Romio Inuzuka, first-year leader of the Black Dogs, has loved his rival Juliet Persia, first-year leader of the White Cats, since childhood. After he rescues her from three Black Dogs who meant to molest her, he confesses during a duel and pledges to help her change the world; she agrees to date him in secret (ch. 1). Juliet, the only child of a noble house that can pass its title only to a son, wants to prove a woman can lead (character list). The pair hide their romance from both dorms, from the princess Char (who blackmails Romio, then becomes an ally, ch. 4-5), from Romio's best friend Hasuki (ch. 6-7) and from his strict brother Airu (ch. 15-18), with Juliet often disguised as a Black Dog boy, "Julio." Romio works up the student government to change the school. During the prefect elections a segregationist Black Dog, Reon Inugami, exposes the relationship to the whole school (ch. 85-86); classmates side with the couple one by one and both are elected head prefects (ch. 87-89). After a class trip to the West, Juliet's father's attempt to send her away (ch. 110-115) and Char's debutante ball (ch. 116-117), they graduate (ch. 118). Seven years later Romio, now a politician, and Juliet, the West's first female viscount after reforming the aristocracy, marry at the academy in a wedding seen as a symbol of peace between the nations (ch. 118.5-119; fan wiki; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Love and friendship that cross a hostile divide, held with courage and finally made public, can change a prejudiced world: the enemies' children end as reformers whose wedding stands for peace.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the peacemaking and the end of segregation, and the series' ending (a public reveal, family consent and marriage) answers much of the religious concern about secret dating. The split is over the means and the gags: most Christian, Jewish, Muslim and Latter-day Saint readings weigh the years of deception and the fan service with teenagers, and peace churches object that fighting is the comedy's engine. Progressive and secular readers rate the anti-prejudice and gender themes highest but fault the groping gags and the way the ch. 1 attempted assault is dropped.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yōsuke Kaneda (story and art) debuted the idea as a one-shot in Bessatsu Shōnen Magazine (December 2014); this was his first long series. His later series is The Classroom of a Black Cat and a Witch (Wikipedia). No interview was opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- The series borrows names and setup from Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet; the dorms and characters are named after dog and cat breeds (Persia, Chartreux, Scott Fold, Somali; Inuzuka, Komai, Tosa) (Wikipedia; character list).
- Publisher: Kodansha, in two shōnen magazines (Bessatsu Shōnen Magazine, then Weekly Shōnen Magazine). Kodansha USA publishes it in English, rated 13+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can two people from groups taught to hate each other love openly, and can their love change the groups? Shakespeare's answer was that the feud ends only over the lovers' graves. Boarding School Juliet asks for a comic, hopeful answer instead.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, with patience, allies and courage. Romio's first promise is not "I love you" alone but "I will help you change the world" (ch. 1). He works his way up the school's institutions, the secret is forced into the open by an enemy (ch. 85-86), and one classmate after another stands with the couple (ch. 87-89). The epilogue makes the private romance a public treaty: their wedding is a symbol of peace between two nations (ch. 119).

**Characters as arguments.** Romio argues for love as action; Juliet for the equal dignity of women and of the other side. Hasuki shows loyalty surviving heartbreak. Char moves from possessive love to generous help. Airu stands for family honor and is won over by his brother's resolve. Reon is the strongest counterargument: her hatred comes from a family torn apart by the feud, and the story lets her lose, explain herself and return (ch. 85-90). Maru, one of the ch. 1 attackers, becomes a friend through "Julio"; the story forgives him without revisiting what he did, which many readers dislike.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rosaries exchanged as love tokens and then pierced in the staged duel (ch. 18); matching rings; Julio, Juliet's disguise, which lets her live on the other side; the wall between dorms; first names (Romio and Juliet call each other by first names only once they no longer have to hide, ch. 75-78 onward).

**Traditions it echoes.** Romeo and Juliet, West Side Story and the long line of star-crossed lovers, retold as a school romantic comedy like Nisekoi (ANN). Its political hope (reconciliation through a marriage between rival houses) is also an old dynastic idea, here joined to modern equality.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Progressivism and feminism are affirmed through the plot's goals; nationalism is the obstacle. Liberal means (elections, persuasion, assemblies) win the day. Traditional institutions are reformed and kept, and the ending is a church wedding with both families present.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-20, the anime) are a secret-romance comedy. Volumes 5-9 (ch. 21-58) widen the cast and bring family pressure (Airu, Romio's mother). Volumes 10-13 (ch. 59-94) build to the election and the public reveal. Volumes 14-16 (ch. 95-119) turn to politics between nations: the class trip, Juliet's father, Char's debutante ball, graduation and the wedding.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Romio and Juliet keep their love secret, and was it right? When did hiding stop helping?
2. Reon hates the other side because of what happened to her family. Does knowing that change how you see her?
3. Maru tried to hurt Juliet in the first chapter and later becomes her friend. Should the story have dealt with that? How?
4. Juliet wants to prove a woman can lead. What does she have to do that a boy would not?
5. Are there "Black Dogs and White Cats" at your school or in your town? What would it take to bring them together?
