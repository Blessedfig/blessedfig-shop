**Premise.** In 1970s Japan, in a rural Kansai town, the children of the Star Kids home live with caretakers Mr. Adachi and Miss Mitsuko because their parents cannot or will not raise them. Their refuge is a junked Nissan Sunny on the lawn, where in imagination they drive home, race, or fly into space. Sei, a quiet boy in glasses, arrives sure his parents will fetch him in spring (ch. 1); white-haired Haruo fights, smokes and keeps a tin of Nivea because it smells like his mother, whose visits are painful (ANN; Garrity). Junsuke and his little brother Shosuke visit their sick mother in the hospital; Megumu, whose parents died, fears dying alone; Kiko tells lies for attention; teenage Kenji, son of an alcoholic father, hides adult magazines in the car and threatens to quit school; gentle Taro, an adult with an intellectual disability, picks flowers and sings. Episodes follow a former resident who returns to apologize (vol. 3), a TV crew's visit, a party with schoolmates who live with their parents, a baseball meet with other homes (vol. 5) and Sei's secret plan to reunite his parents (vol. 5). In the last volume Haruo, under pressure from neighbors over his truancy, and Sei slip onto a truck bound for Tokyo, where Haruo's mother lives (Japan Times; manga-passion); an eight-month time skip follows, and posters' accounts say the children scatter, some to new homes or back to a parent (to verify).

## Life message

**Children who have been left behind still deserve wonder, belonging and adults who stay; imagination and friendship help them bear it, but cannot replace a family.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Taiyō Matsumoto (story and art), born 1967 in Tokyo; debuted in Kodansha's Morning in 1987; known for Tekkonkinkreet (1993-1994), Ping Pong (1996-1997), No. 5 and Takemitsu Zamurai; influenced by Moebius, Katsuhiro Otomo and French bande dessinée (Wikipedia, Taiyō Matsumoto).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, Production, citing Japanese interviews): he had planned since his debut to draw a manga about his six years in a children's care home but waited; Sunny is "half real and half fiction"; he used Kansai dialect to lighten the tone; his wife helped draw the girls. The Japan Times review says names were changed and about 70 percent of characters and events draw on real life. Garrity notes Sei resembles Matsumoto and that he has been reticent about the details.
- Critic Natsume Fusanosuke groups Sunny with works that break manga-industry conventions (Wikipedia, Taiyō Matsumoto).
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Monthly Ikki, then Big Comic Spirits, both seinen); VIZ Media in English, rated Teen, translated by Michael Arias, who is directing the planned film.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a child need when the people who should have stayed did not? The Star Kids are fed, schooled and looked after, yet each carries an absence: Sei's denial, Haruo's Nivea tin, Megumu's fear of dying alone. Sunny asks what can fill that space and what cannot.

**The answer the story gives.** Imagination, friends and adults who stay can carry a child a long way, but they do not erase the wound. The Sunny takes them anywhere and nowhere. The caretakers' constancy is quietly heroic. The ending refuses a neat rescue: an eight-month leap, children scattered, some with a parent again (to verify), the future open (VIZ; manga-passion).

**Characters as arguments.** Haruo argues that toughness protects you, and shows it does not. Sei argues that if you wait and plan, parents will come back, and the story is gentle with that hope. Megumu argues for being "normal" and is torn between her schoolmates and the home (vol. 3). Kenji is the teenager with no direction. Taro, the gentle adult with a disability, shows that worth does not depend on ability. Mr. Adachi argues for patience without lectures.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Sunny itself: a broken car that goes everywhere in the mind. The Nivea tin: a mother reduced to a smell. White hair on a child. Snot and red cheeks (ANN; AnimeClick): Matsumoto's children are never prettified. The truck to Tokyo as the escape that is also a test (vol. 6).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese I-novel and autobiographical manga (Garrity); children's stories of orphans from Heidi to Annie, which ANN notes it is darker than; and, for faith readers, the scriptural concern for the orphan (James 1:27; Qur'an 93; Psalm 68). Its restraint is closer to realist literature than to melodrama.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism is affirmed in the daily life of the home. Existential searching marks the older characters. Secular humanism is the default frame: human care is what there is, though one chapter lets the children wonder about God (ch. 19). Critical theory appears only as quiet observation of stigma and poverty.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-12) introduce the children and the Sunny; vols. 3-4 (ch. 13-24) widen to visitors, school and the outside world; vol. 5 (ch. 25-30) gives each child a vignette and Sei's escape plan; vol. 6 (ch. 31-37) brings Haruo's run toward Tokyo, a time skip and the ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sei says he is only staying until spring. Why do you think he needs to believe that?
2. Haruo keeps a tin of his mother's hand cream. What do people keep to remember someone?
3. What do the caretakers do that matters most, even though they cannot be the children's parents?
4. How do the neighbors and schoolmates treat the home's children? Have you seen something like that?
5. Where would you drive the Sunny, and why?
