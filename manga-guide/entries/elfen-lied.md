**Premise.** Off the coast of Kamakura, a secret research institute holds the Diclonius, a new mutant species of girls with small horns and invisible telekinetic arms ("vectors") that can tear people apart; their touch spreads a virus that makes infected men father more Diclonii. Lucy, the strongest, escapes in a massacre (ch. 1), is shot in the head and wakes on the beach as Nyu, a childlike second personality who can say only one word. College student Kouta and his cousin Yuka take her in at an old inn, unaware that Kouta has buried the memory of seeing his father and little sister killed years earlier, or who did it. The inn fills with other strays: Mayu, a homeless 12-year-old who fled her stepfather's sexual abuse; Nana, a Diclonius child raised in the lab, whose limbs Lucy tears off (ch. 14-16) and who calls the researcher Kurama "Papa"; and Nozomi, an aspiring singer abused by her father. Hunters include the soldier Bando, whom Lucy maims; Kurama, who killed Diclonius babies for the institute but spared his own daughter Mariko; and Director Kakuzawa, who plans to father a Diclonius race and rule it as a god. Lucy's backstory shows her abuse by other children, the beating death of her puppy, and her jealous killing of Kouta's family (likely ch. 32-38). In the end the island is destroyed, Lucy kills Kakuzawa, begs Kouta's forgiveness and promises never to kill again, then dies after healing him; ten years later Kouta and Yuka are married with a daughter named Nyu, and twin girls appear at Lucy's puppy's grave (ch. 98-107; Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Cruelty breeds monsters and only love and acceptance can break the cycle; the story holds up kindness to the rejected, while showing at length how abuse and discrimination destroy children.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | The plot is a species war over reproduction: the vector virus makes human men father Diclonii, Lucy is the only fertile "queen," and Kakuzawa schemes to breed a new race (Wikipedia). A third personality, an instinctive "DNA voice," urges Lucy to kill. The story asks whether violence is in the genes or learned from abuse (Wikipedia; But Why Tho). |
| Pragmatic | Institutions act from cold calculation: babies euthanized in maternity wards, Mariko raised in restraints and fitted with bombs, Nana discarded when she fails (Wikipedia). Kouta's household works by plain kindness and shared chores. |
| Religious | No real faith is practiced, but the imagery is heavy: Kakuzawa wants to be a god of the new race, a creature called the Embryo of God appears near the end, a chapter is titled "The Annunciation" (ch. 60), and Kouta and Yuka confess their love at an Inari shrine (Wikipedia). Beneath the Tangles (Christian) finds in it a picture of human depravity that points to the need for a Savior. Readers also note the Lucy/Lucifer echo. Rebirth is hinted in the epilogue's twins (TV Tropes). |
| Socio-cultural | Discrimination and othering are the stated themes (Wikipedia); the anime's director said he meant to question how people divide each other by difference and how cruelty shapes atrocities. The institute's grotto is named Lebensborn after the Nazi eugenics program (Wikipedia). Bullying, abuse at home and child neglect drive nearly every character. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the institute falls and Lucy repents and dies saving Kouta; the survivors build a family and the epilogue hints at rebirth, but most of the story's children are dead, maimed or deeply wounded.
Hope source: R — the way through is repentance and redemption: Lucy asks forgiveness and promises never to kill again, and her last act heals the man whose family she killed; the household of strays (F) is the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Lynn Okamoto, born January 6, 1971, in Wakayama Prefecture; worked at Bandai and Arc System Works before manga. Elfen Lied grew from a 2000 one-shot of the same name; later serials in Weekly Young Jump (Nononono, Brynhildr in the Darkness) and Kodansha's Weekly Young Magazine (Parallel Paradise). An asteroid, 49382 Lynnokamoto, is named after him (Wikipedia).
- In his author notes he calls his early art less refined (Anime UK News). No interview about the series' themes was opened; the stated themes on record come from the anime's director, Mamoru Kanbe, who called it a love story and said he wanted to question how people divide each other by difference (Wikipedia). **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Young Jump, seinen). Dark Horse publishes the English omnibuses, rated 16+.
- The series was among 38 anime and manga titles banned by China's Ministry of Culture in 2015 (Wikipedia). No controversies involving the creator personally were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Are monsters born or made? Lucy has killed dozens, including Kouta's family. The story keeps asking whether her violence comes from her genes (the instinctive voice in her head) or from what humans did to her as a child, and whether someone like her can be loved or forgiven.

**The answer the story gives.** Mostly that they are made. Lucy's killing begins when other children beat her puppy to death; Mayu, Nana and Nozomi are all wounded by adults; the institute treats girls as specimens. Kindness reaches each of them at the inn. Lucy's end is repentance, a promise never to kill again, and a last act that saves Kouta's life. The story does not pretend this undoes the past: Kouta does not easily forgive.

**Characters as arguments.** Kouta argues for mercy toward the stranger. Lucy argues, at first, that humans are not people, and her life refutes her own creed. Kurama is the case of duty against love: he killed other people's babies and could not kill his own. Kakuzawa is the arrogance of a man who would be god. Bando is violence for its own sake. Mayu, who saves the man Lucy maimed, is goodness that survives abuse.

**Symbols and motifs.** Horns as the mark of difference. The music box or the song "Elfenlied" (taught by Nozomi in the manga). The stone steps above the sea where Kouta and Lucy met as children. The puppy's grave, where the epilogue's twins appear. The Lebensborn cave.

**Traditions it echoes.** Frankenstein and other created-monster stories; Japanese oni legends; Gunslinger Girl and Chobits (Wikipedia). Christian readers may see a story of sin and the cost of redemption (Beneath the Tangles); Buddhist readers, the cycle of hatred and rebirth.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Critical theory's concern with othering is the story's own. Transhumanism and eugenics are its villains. Naturalism's question of instinct is left open. Nihilism is Lucy's starting creed and is defeated by love. Religion appears as imagery around a false god.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-27): the escape, Nyu at the inn, Nana's maiming, Mayu's arrival and backstory. Volumes 4-6 (ch. 28-60): Lucy's past and the killing of Kouta's family, Mariko and Kurama, the experiments. Volumes 7-9 (ch. 61-82): the institute's war, new hunters, the attack on Mayu. Volumes 10-12 (ch. 83-107): Kouta remembers, Kakuzawa's plan, the island's fall, Lucy's repentance and death, the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Was Lucy born to kill, or made to? What evidence does the story give each way?
2. Kouta takes in every girl who has nowhere to go. Why does that matter so much in this story?
3. Lucy asks Kouta to forgive her. Should he? Can he?
4. Kurama killed other people's babies but spared his own. What does the story say about duty and love?
5. The story condemns treating girls as objects, yet draws its heroines nude often. How should a reader weigh that?
