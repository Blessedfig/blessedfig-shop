**Premise.** Shintaro Kisaragi, very bright (an IQ of 168, Wikipedia) and very lonely, has not left his room for two years, since his classmate Ayano died. His only company is Ene, a cheeky girl who lives in his computer. On August 14 he spills soda on his keyboard, goes out to buy a new one, and is caught in an armed hostage-taking at a department store (ch. 4-7). He is rescued, and recruited, by the Mekakushi Dan ("Blindfold Gang"), a group of teens with red-eyed powers, including his idol sister Momo, the leader Kido, Seto, Kano and Marry, a shy girl who is part Medusa (fan wiki; Yen Press). Each power came from a "snake" received when its owner died, or nearly died, on August 15 and touched the Kagerou Daze, a heat-haze world made long ago by the Medusa Azami. The real villain is the Snake of Clearing Eyes, which possesses bodies and wants to merge the worlds (fan wiki). In the manga's first timeline the gang is massacred on August 15 and Marry rewinds the world. A second timeline, told as a long flashback to Shintaro by his own snake in Ayano's form, follows the group as the snake kills them one by one; in the last volume the survivors trap the snake in the Kagerou Daze, and Marry rewinds time again in hope of a summer in which everyone lives (fan wiki; TV Tropes; Yen Press vol. 13).

## Life message

**Lonely, wounded young people can become a family for one another, and together they refuse to let despair have the last word, even when they must try again and again.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can people who have lost everyone learn to live again, and can they change what has already happened? Shintaro has stopped living since Ayano's death; Marry has hidden from the world for a hundred years; each gang member received their power by dying, or nearly dying.

**The answer the story gives.** Not alone. The gang's answer to despair is to stay together and keep trying. The manga does not show a final victory: it ends with the world rewound, carrying the hope that this time everyone will live. The answer is loyalty, not success.

**Characters as arguments.** Shintaro argues that even the most withdrawn person can choose to act for someone else. Ayano argues for self-giving love, though her suicide makes that argument painful and needs framing. Kano argues that hiding feelings behind lies costs the liar. Marry argues that fear can turn into courage. The Snake of Clearing Eyes argues for despair and control, and it is the story's evil.

**Symbols and motifs.** Red eyes and Ayano's red scarf, worn as the heroes' color. Heat haze, a shimmer where the real and unreal blur. Snakes, the Medusa and stone. The repeating August 15. Falling, the way several characters die.

**Traditions it echoes.** The time-loop story, the Greek Medusa myth recast with sympathy, and Japanese summer ghost stories of the Obon season. Faith readers may hear echoes of the serpent as the tempter who feeds on despair; Buddhist readers may hear the wheel of rebirth.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and courage are celebrated. Nihilism is the villain. Myth is the setting, never mocked. Transhumanist ideas (a mind in a computer, an ideal body) are explored as sad rather than hopeful.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-9) are the bright, comic hostage story. Vols. 3-6 (ch. 10-30) fill in backstories (Takane and Haruka, Hibiya and Hiyori, Seto and Marry) and end the first timeline in a massacre. Vols. 7-13 (ch. 31-69) tell Azami's myth and the second timeline, darker and more violent, ending with a rewind.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shintaro stayed in his room for two years. What finally got him out, and what might have helped sooner?
2. The gang treat red as the heroes' color. What makes someone a hero in this story?
3. Ayano believed her death would protect her family. What else could she have done, and who could she have told?
4. The villain grows stronger when people despair. Why might despair make someone easier to control?
