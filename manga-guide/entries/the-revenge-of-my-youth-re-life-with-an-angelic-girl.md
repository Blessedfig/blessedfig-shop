**Premise.** Shinichiro Niihama, about 30, has spent years at an unethical company under a boss who detests him (One Peace blurb). He regrets one thing above all: Haruka Shijoin, the bright, kind girl he admired from afar in high school, was later harassed and bullied at her workplace and took her own life (Anime UK News; MyAnimeList review). One day he collapses at work and wakes 14 years earlier, in his second year of high school (MangaUpdates; The Outerhaven), with his late mother alive again. Using an adult's calm and the work skills he learned the hard way, he stands up to the bully who took his lunch money, wins over his cold younger sister Kanako, takes charge of a stalled class meeting and runs the class takoyaki cafe at the school festival (vol. 1-2), tutors Haruka and faces a rich rival, Mitsurugi, in an exam "duel" over her (vol. 3), and finally meets her parents at the Shijoin mansion (vol. 4). Haruka, the daughter of a bookstore-chain owner who loves light novels, has been bullied on and off since elementary school without understanding why (The Outerhaven). The manga ends after four volumes.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could live your teenage years again with everything you learned since, what would you do with them? Shinichiro's first youth was gray and timid; his adult life was swallowed by a job that used him up, and the one girl who brightened it died alone. The series asks whether a person can change what matters by changing himself.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, by small, brave acts rather than one dramatic rescue. A MyAnimeList reader notes that he does not obsess over a single "death flag" but changes many small things: he speaks up, works hard, treats people well. Becoming someone Haruka can talk to (Anime UK News) is the protection the first timeline lacked.

**Characters as arguments.** Shinichiro argues that experience should become kindness and courage. Haruka, gentle and bullied without knowing why, shows how cruelty lands on the innocent. Kanako, his sister, shows that people notice real change. The boss is the case against power without care; Mitsurugi, who thinks a girl belongs to him by rank, is answered by Haruka herself (vol. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The collapse and the waking. The gray of his first youth against the color of the second. The school festival as a test of leadership (vol. 2). Haruka's family bookstores and her love of light novels: stories as the bond between them.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese "regression" and redo stories (ReLIFE, as ANN notes), the American "Peggy Sue" and Back to the Future do-over (a MyAnimeList reviewer's comparison), and the old moral that regret should become amendment of life. Faith readers may see a picture of repentance and redeemed time, though the story offers no source for its grace.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue and practical wisdom win; exploitative work and bullying are condemned; romance is chaste and patient. No lens is mocked.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the rewind, the bully, his family. Vol. 2: the class meeting and festival, and a brief return to the old life. Vol. 3: exams, tutoring and the rival. Vol. 4: the Shijoin family and the close. Readers say the ending is abrupt (MangaUpdates; MyAnimeList).

**Discussion questions.**
1. What did Shinichiro learn at his terrible job that helped him at school?
2. Haruka was bullied without knowing why. What could her classmates have done?
3. Why does it matter so much to Shinichiro to see his mother again?
4. Mitsurugi thinks Haruka belongs to him. How does Haruka answer that?
5. If someone you know seems alone or hopeless, who could you tell?
