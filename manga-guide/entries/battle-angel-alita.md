**Premise.** In the 26th century, in the ruins of the former United States near Kansas City, the Scrapyard is a lawless junk city under Zalem, a floating city whose people never come down. Cyber-doctor Daisuke Ido, an exile from Zalem, finds the living head and chest of a cyborg girl in the dump. He rebuilds her, and since she has no memory he names her Alita after his dead cat (Battle 1). She finds she remembers one thing, the Martian martial art Panzer Kunst, and becomes a bounty hunter ("Hunter-Warrior"). Over nine volumes she falls in love with Hugo, a teenage thief who dreams of reaching Zalem (Battles 8-12); becomes a star of the gladiator sport Motorball (Battles 12-22); kills a monstrous Zapan (Battle 29); spends ten years as an agent of Zalem (TUNED); fights the rebel army Barjack and the mad scientist Desty Nova; and learns Zalem's secret: every adult there has had their brain replaced with a bio-chip (Battle 48) (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**What makes a person human is not the body but the heart: love, grief and the choice to protect others, even in a world where violence is the only law and the paradise above is a lie.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A world of scarcity where status is won by fighting and bodies are traded for parts (Hugo's gang steals cyborg spines, Battles 8-9). Alita is torn between fighting instinct and attachment. The ending regrows her as a human body from the "Tree of Life" (Epilogue). |
| Pragmatic | Survival rules the Scrapyard: bounty hunting, Motorball contracts, black-market parts. Zalem keeps the surface quiet with bounties and violent sport (Wikipedia). Hugo's dream is a scam: no one from the surface can buy passage (Battle 11). |
| Religious | Biblical names frame the world: Zalem (from "Salem," peace), Jeru (Jerusalem), and Zalem's computer Melchizedek, "king of Salem" (Wikipedia, Production). The heaven above is false: its adults have no brains (Battle 48), and Koyomi says the Zalemites are "just normal people... rather than all-knowing gods" (Battle 40). The original ending has Alita become a Tree of Life that saves both cities and then rise again in a human body (fan wiki, Epilogue). Nova's research is into "karma" (fan wiki). |
| Socio-cultural | A class story: an elite city exploits the poor below and arranges violent sport to keep them busy (Wikipedia). Barjack's rebellion is shown with sympathy but led by a split personality (Den) and backed by Nova (Battles 36-41). Alita is a female lead in a seinen action story; reviewers differ on whether the violence has a sexualized edge (ANN guide). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the story is full of loss (Hugo, Ido's memory, Alita's death), but it ends with Alita saving both cities and being reborn to meet the man she loves.
Hope source: P — Alita's growth is measured by whom she chooses to protect, from baby Koyomi (Battle 7) to the whole Scrapyard and Zalem when she turns herself into the Tree of Life (Epilogue; Kodansha's synopsis: she hunts "those who prey on the weak").

**Where traditions disagree.** Most frames find a message worth discussing: the heroine protects the weak, cruelty is shown as cruelty, and the ending is self-sacrifice and rebirth. The divide is over the explicitness: most religious frames rate the constant gore, body horror and nudity as a Significant concern, and pacifist traditions (Anabaptist, Jehovah's Witness) find the violence central to the message, not just the depiction. Religious readers may also differ on the Biblical names: some will read Zalem and Melchizedek as a critique of false paradises; others as sacred names used carelessly.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The story sides with those who choose to protect others and keep feeling, and against those who treat people as parts or experiments, but it lets almost everyone suffer.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yukito Kishiro, born in Tokyo; won Shonen Sunday's Best Newcomer award in 1984 (fan wiki). Alita began as a cyborg police officer, Gally, in an unpublished comic, Rainmaker; Shueisha editors liked her and asked for a series (Wikipedia, Production).
- On-record statements opened: in a Loot Crate interview around the film (date not shown), Kishiro said he was not "working closely" with the filmmakers, had "put [his] full trust in them," and that "the core of the story, its 'soul,' if you will, is quite intact." On the OVA, he said he was too busy with the manga "to review the plan coolly" (Wikipedia). The fan wiki reports that in a 1994 Animerica interview he hoped a director like RoboCop's would make a live-action film. TV Tropes says he ended the original in 1995 because of ill health and later rewrote the ending as Last Order (not confirmed from a primary source; to verify). No statements about religion, politics or the meaning of the Biblical names were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- The Biblical names (Zalem from Hebrew shalem, "peace"; Jeru from Jerusalem; Melchizedek) are Kishiro's; Viz's 1990s edition changed Zalem to Tiphares and Jeru to Ketheres (Kabbalistic terms); newer editions restore them (Wikipedia).
- Publishers: Shueisha (original, 1990-2010); Kodansha (since 2010, when Kishiro moved). English: Viz Media (1990s), Kodansha USA (2017-). James Cameron acquired film rights; the film was released by 20th Century Fox in 2019 (Wikipedia).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone human when everything about the body can be replaced? Alita begins as a head and chest in a dump. Over nine volumes she wears at least five bodies, and the story keeps asking whether she is still herself. Kodansha's own synopsis frames it as whether she can "hold onto her humanity" as she "begins to revel in her own bloodlust."

**The answer the story gives.** Humanity lives in feeling and choice. Alita weeps for Makaku, the brain-eating killer, when she learns he was a burned child Nova saved (Battle 7). She loves Hugo though she fears her body could crush him (Battle 9). The sharpest answer comes in Zalem: its adults, the people who live in "heaven," have had their brains removed and replaced by chips (Battle 48). The city that looked down on cyborgs is less human than the junk-city below. In the original ending, Alita turns her body into a Tree of Life that saves both cities, and is reborn in a human body (Epilogue).

**Characters as arguments.** Ido argues for care, but he also loves the hunt and later erases his memory rather than face the truth (Battle 43). Hugo argues for dreams, and his dream is a con (Battle 11). Nova argues that nothing is forbidden in the study of "karma"; he is both monster and, in the Ouroboros dream, a man who wonders what he was chasing (fan wiki). Zapan argues for pride, and becomes a monster in Alita's stolen body, yet his last act saves her (Battle 29). Den argues for revolution, and turns out to be a split-off part of Nova's son (Battles 40-41).

**Symbols and motifs.** The floating city (heaven out of reach); the tubes Hugo climbs; the dump where Alita is found and the tree she becomes; Alita's changing bodies (Battle 51 title page); Biblical names (Zalem, Jeru, Melchizedek). Butterflies, which Alita fears (Battle 33). The lotus in the original ending (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** Cyberpunk (Blade Runner, Philip K. Dick and Isaac Asimov, per JapanVisitor via Wikipedia); Frankenstein and Pinocchio stories of made beings who want to be real (our framing); the martial-arts tournament manga; the Biblical image of a heavenly Jerusalem, turned upside down.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is shown as ordinary and dangerous. Critical theory finds a clear picture of class rule. Existentialists find a heroine who makes herself. Religious readers find a false heaven and a sacrificial rebirth, with no God. Feminist readers disagree: McNulty praises that Alita is never framed as a sexual object; Loveridge finds the action aesthetic sexualized and dated (ANN guide).

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: bounty hunting, Makaku, and Hugo's tragedy. Vols. 3-4: Motorball and the duel with Jashugan. Vol. 5: Nova, Zapan and Ido's death. Vols. 6-7: ten years later, Alita as a Zalem agent, Figure Four, Barjack. Vols. 8-9: the secret of Zalem, Nova's Granite Inn, Alita's death and the rushed original ending, later replaced by Last Order.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Alita changes bodies many times. What stays the same about her?
2. Why does Alita cry for Makaku after killing him?
3. Hugo was willing to hurt people to reach Zalem. Was his dream worth it?
4. What does the secret of Zalem say about people who think they are better than others?
5. Alita sometimes enjoys fighting. Where is the line between protecting others and loving violence?

**Note on the sequels.** Last Order (2000-2014, 19 volumes) restarts from just before the original ending, revives Alita in Zalem and stages a space martial-arts tournament; Mars Chronicle (2014-2025) tells her childhood on Mars and reveals she was born as a tumor grafted into a machine body (fan wiki). Neither is scored here.
