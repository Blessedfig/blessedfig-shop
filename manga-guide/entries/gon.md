**Premise.** Gon is a tiny, orange, nearly indestructible dinosaur, about a year old in the first stories, living long after the age of the dinosaurs among modern animals. Each wordless, self-contained episode follows him looking for food, sleep or a quiet spot and getting tangled up with whatever creatures live there, from Alaskan bears and African lions to Amazon piranhas, Australian dingoes, penguins and sea turtles. He beats up a grizzly, steals its fish and sleeps on it; bullies a lion into being his steed and then shares a kill with it; floods a forest with an oversized dam; adopts himself into an eagle family and learns to "fly" by flapping his tiny arms (Kodansha vol. 1, The Fandom Post). He often ends up leading or defending young and weak animals against predators, though he is just as often the troublemaker himself (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; reviews). There are no humans, no words and almost no continuity between chapters.

## Life message

**Life in the wild is hungry, rough and funny; strength is at its best when it shares the food and stands up for the small, even in a hero who is mostly out for himself.**

| Pragmatic | Problems are solved physically: bite, kick, eat, sleep. Gon improvises (a hedgehog ball as a weapon, a kangaroo pouch as a springboard; TV Tropes) and almost never loses. |
| Religious | No religion, gods or spirits appear. | Socio-cultural | A universal, wordless comic that critics praise for crossing language and culture (Wikipedia). Bullies (predators who pick on the young) are usually humbled; the comedy also comes from Gon being a bully himself (The Fandom Post; Manga Bookshelf). |

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a creature like when no one can stop it? Gon is the last dinosaur, the size of a toddler and stronger than a grizzly. Each episode drops him into a working ecosystem and watches what happens.

**The answer the story gives.** He eats, sleeps, fights, and very often ends up on the side of the small. The book never states a moral; it simply shows that the same strength that lets Gon steal a bear's fish lets him save an eagle chick or lead orphaned cubs through danger. Readers decide which Gon they see.

**Characters as arguments.** Gon argues for appetite and freedom. The predators argue for the food chain, and the book does not treat them as evil: they hunt because they are hungry (vol. 4 podcast notes). The young animals argue for loyalty and growing up; several do not survive.

**Symbols and motifs.** The last dinosaur out of his time; the nap on a defeated enemy; flight without wings. The wordless page itself: animals understood through faces and movement.

**Traditions it echoes.** Silent comics and animal fables; Chuck Jones-style comedy (Manga Bookshelf); nature documentaries. For faith readers, Psalm 104's lions seeking their food, or the Qur'an's "communities like you" (6:38).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Naturalism is the frame. Strength rules, but the story's sympathy is with the weak. Environmentalism appears only as detail and as one joke about Gon's destructive dam.

**Shifts across the series.** Kodansha vol. 1: forests and Africa (bear, lion, eagles). Vol. 3: the Amazon (piranhas, a tiger, hallucinogenic mushrooms). Vol. 4: sea turtles, a desert crossing, big-cat kittens. Other volumes (placement not confirmed) visit Australia, the Arctic and the underground. The 2012-2013 color revival and the 2013 one-shot with speech balloons are separate from the English volumes.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Gon takes the bear's fish and sleeps on the bear. Is that funny, fair, or both?
2. When does Gon use his strength to help others? When does he use it to help himself?
3. Predators hunt the young animals Gon protects. Are the predators bad, or just hungry?
4. Gon's dam floods the forest. Who pays for what one strong creature wants?
5. Without words, how can you tell what an animal is feeling?
