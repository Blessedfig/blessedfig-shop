**Premise.** In 1969, Kenji Endo and his childhood friends build a secret base in a field, invent a symbol and write a "Book of Prophecy" about villains who try to destroy the world. In 1997 Kenji runs a convenience store and is raising his baby niece Kanna. A friend nicknamed Donkey dies in what looks like suicide. Kenji learns that a fast-growing cult led by a masked figure called "Friend" uses the boys' childhood symbol, and that the cult is carrying out the prophecy. The story moves from 1997-2000 (the attack of "Bloody New Year's Eve") to 2014-2015, when Friend's party rules Japan and a teenage Kanna fights back, to "Year 3 of the Friend Era," when Friend rules the world. Much of the story is told in flashbacks to the 1970s. The question "who is Friend?" runs to the final volumes.

## Life message

**Ordinary people have one thing in life they must not run from. A good life means remembering what you did as a child, owning up to it, keeping faith with your friends and refusing to follow a hidden leader just because the crowd does.** Co-creator Nagasaki: "the spirit of this work is that ordinary people have one thing they absolutely must do in life" (eiga.com, translated).

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame reads the series as a condemnation of a false messiah and the people who follow him, so grades cluster at A and B. The dividing lines are the parody of resurrection and divine claims (Orthodox, Catholic and Pentecostal families weigh how sacred things are handled), psychic powers shown as partly real (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness), the secular stance that treats every messiah, including the heroes, with suspicion (Thomas; Bancha), which secular humanists find closest, and a plague-centered plot, which matters most to Christian Science families. Scientologist families may read the portrayal of a new religion as a cult differently from others.

## Creator and publisher context

- Naoki Urasawa (born 1960, Fuchu, Tokyo) grew up with Tezuka's Astro Boy and took up guitar because of Bob Dylan; he has released rock albums and a "Kenji's song" single tied to the series (Wikipedia, opened).
- Origin, on record: the idea came in the bath while he heard a UN speech on TV saying "without them" humanity would not have reached the 21st century, and he asked "Who's 'them'?" (Japan House LA interview, 2019; Nikkei interview, 2021). He does not plan stories ahead: "If the story of the manga doesn't keep surprising me, I wouldn't be able to continue." About a tenth of the scenes reflect his own childhood (Wikipedia). After the September 11 attacks he dropped a planned scene of giant robots destroying Shinjuku and drew a chapter of Kenji singing instead (Japan House LA). He says when he draws boys "they tend to go in a dark direction" (Nikkei, translated).
- No on-record statement by Urasawa about Aum Shinrikyo, religion or politics was found. Co-creator Takashi Nagasaki, asked about the Aum incident, said he "wasn't thinking about that much," that "new religions were booming back then," that he "proposed making it a religious group," and that cult leaders gain prestige by speaking little (eiga.com, 2008, translated). The Aum link rests on critics and scholars: Jason Thompson ("the biggest inspiration for the Friends is probably Aum Shinrikyo"), Tom Speelman at Polygon (via Wikipedia), and Jolyon Baraka Thomas, who reads Friend's staged levitation as an allusion to Asahara's levitation photos and the Friends' party as a reference to Aum's political party (Japanese Journal of Religious Studies, 2012). Aum Shinrikyo carried out the 1995 sarin attack on the Tokyo subway (Thompson).
- Urasawa was drawing Monster at the same time and injured his shoulder during the run (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shogakukan. Viz Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge). No reputable reports of creator controversy were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why do people follow a leader whose face they never see? And what does an ordinary person owe the world when a lie he helped start grows into a catastrophe? Kenji's childhood "Book of Prophecy" was a game. Someone took it literally and made it come true. The story asks who is responsible for that.

**The answer the story gives.** The cult wins because people are, in the words of one reader, "just looking for something to believe in" (Ogiue Maniax). It falls because ordinary, unremarkable adults decide to do the one thing they must do. Co-creator Nagasaki names that as the spirit of the work. The end does not come from a superpower; Thompson notes the psychic subplot is a red herring. It comes from information, courage, a vaccine and an apology. Bancha argues that even the heroes are "desacralized messiahs": Kenji's long-awaited climax is an apology, and Kanna's role as "last hope" proves misleading. In this reading, no one saves the world alone.

**Characters as arguments.** Kenji is the argument that a man who settled for a convenience store can still answer the call, and that he must also confess his own childhood wrong. Friend is the argument that envy and the hunger to be seen can grow into a will to rule and destroy. EBSCO describes him as "not so much good or evil as uncaring and selfish." His two identities say that "Friend" could be anyone; Thomas writes, "Because Tomodachi could be anyone, he could just as easily represent everyone." Kanna is the next generation, who must reject her father's legacy. The turncoats (Yamane, Sadakiyo, Prisoner 13) are the argument that complicity can end in conscience. The crowd is the quiet subject of the later volumes.

**Symbols and motifs.** The hand-and-eye symbol from the secret base. The Book of Prophecy and the New Book of Prophecy. The mask and the faceless leader. The giant robot that turns out to be a balloon with cloth hung down (Viz v8): a terror that is a fake. Expo '70 and the Tower of the Sun as Showa hope, rebuilt by a tyrant. The T. Rex song and Kenji's guitar, played over the PA in the first and last chapters (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** Apocalyptic literature with a false messiah and a world ruler. Stephen King's It, as Thompson notes, with childhood friends returning as adults. Japanese debate after the 1995 Aum attack about how educated people joined a doomsday cult. Orwellian dystopia: rewritten history, re-education, a manufactured enemy.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarian collectivism is the clear target. Secular reason and science are presented as the way out, and miracles as frauds. Religion is not mocked in every form: the Catholic church and the Pope are sympathetic (to verify), and the psychic "God" says "Don't call me God." Thomas reads the series as a warning about religion causing catastrophe and also as a warning that readers' love of the heroes' "just war" "may be frighteningly similar" to what sustains groups like Aum. Readers from faith traditions often read it instead as a story about false prophets.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 are a mystery in 1997-2000 with flashbacks to 1969-1971. Vols. 6-12 jump to 2014: Friend rules Japan, Kanna grows up, Friend Land re-educates. Vols. 13-16 cover Friend's death, staged return and divinization, and a new pandemic. Vols. 17-22 and 21st Century Boys move to Year 3 of the Friend Era, Kenji's return and the unmasking. The tone moves from mystery to political thriller to a story about memory and apology.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do so many people in the story follow Friend without seeing his face? What makes a leader trustworthy?
2. Kenji wrote the Book of Prophecy as a game. How responsible is he for what Friend did with it?
3. Friend stages a levitation and a resurrection. How would you tell a real sign from a fake one? What does your family's tradition say?
4. Kenji's answer to the villain is an apology. Is that enough? What else is needed for justice?
5. Several of Friend's helpers change sides at the end. Can people who went along with evil still make it right?
