**Premise.** In 1969 a group of boys (Kenji, Otcho, Yoshitsune, Maruo, later Donkey and the tomboy Yukiji) builds a secret base in a field, where they read manga, listen to the radio and share stolen adult magazines, and writes a "Book of Prophecy" in which villains try to destroy the world and the boys save it. In 1997 Kenji runs a convenience store and is raising his baby niece Kanna when Donkey dies in a fall ruled a suicide. A cult led by a masked man called "Friend" uses the boys' secret symbol, and the book's events start coming true: bombings and virus outbreaks, then, on "Bloody New Year's Eve" 2000, a giant robot that spreads a virus over Tokyo. Kenji is presumed dead and his friends are blamed. Fourteen years later Friend's party rules Japan; teenage Kanna unites Thai and Chinese mafia groups, her schoolmate Kyoko survives Friend Land's re-education, Friend is shot, appears to rise from the dead while saving the Pope, and releases a worldwide virus. In the "Friend Era" (3FE) Kanna, revealed as Friend's daughter, leads a resistance, Kenji returns with his guitar, and 21st Century Boys resolves who Friend was (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Ordinary people have to own up to the wrongs of their past and stand together against a lie; a "savior" who demands belief is the real danger, and the friends you made as a child are worth fighting for.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | The pull of the group: crowds rally to a leader who promises belonging, and fear of a plague is used to sort people into vaccinated insiders and exiled outsiders (Wikipedia). The boys' band, and later Kanna's alliance of rival gangs, show the same need for a tribe turned to good. |
| Pragmatic | Detective work and memory are the heroes' tools: they must recall who knew about the Book of Prophecy. Urasawa did not plan the plot in advance (Wikipedia, Production), so the story rewards readers who track clues over decades. |
| Religious | A false messiah is the villain: Friend fakes psychic powers with stage tricks, stages a resurrection and is treated as divine (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). The Pope visits Japan and is a target; a priest who was once a gangster shelters Kanna (TV Tropes). Sonntagsblatt (a Protestant weekly) reads it as a study of how abuse of power works in the name of faith. |
| Socio-cultural | Showa-era nostalgia (Expo '70, 1960s-70s manga and rock) and its dark side. Critics including Thompson and Polygon's Tom Speelman link the Friends to the Aum Shinrikyo cult (Wikipedia). A party wins power through a staged crisis and a vaccine, then exiles the unvaccinated and bans travel (Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bittersweet: Friend is defeated and the old friends are reunited, but a manufactured pandemic has killed a large part of humanity (TV Tropes cites ch. 169 for three billion deaths) and many friends have died.
Hope source: F: the story's way through is the childhood friends and the community they rebuild: Kenji's call brings back those who fled, Kanna unites rival gangs, and Kenji's song becomes a rallying anthem (Wikipedia); owning up to old wrongs (R) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every tradition welcomes the series' warning against a man who claims divine powers and the courage of ordinary people. Differences are of degree: Christian Science (C) weighs a plague-and-vaccine plot against its teaching, and Pentecostal, Adventist and Jehovah's Witness readers (B) note psychic powers presented as partly real and armed resistance. Some Christian readers will watch how the staged resurrection is framed; secular, Atheist and Agnostic frames welcome the debunking of miracles, while the Pope and a kindly priest keep the story from sneering at belief.

## Creator and publisher context

- Naoki Urasawa (born 1960), has drawn manga since age four and usually runs two series at once; many stories co-written with former editor Takashi Nagasaki; cites Osamu Tezuka as a hero; also a musician (Wikipedia).
- On-record origin (Wikipedia, Production): a UN speech line about "them," without whom we would not have reached the 21st century, gave him the title and the T. Rex song; he does not plan stories in advance; about a tenth of the childhood scenes reflect his own; the spoon bending is based on Uri Geller.
- After the September 11 attacks he could not draw a planned chapter of giant robots destroying Shinjuku and replaced it with a chapter of Kenji singing (Wikipedia; J-Generation 2019 interview). In 2019 he said the older, more cynical Kenji is the part of the character most like himself (J-Generation).
- He was briefly hospitalized for exhaustion while drawing Monster and this series together, and a shoulder injury made him consider retiring (Wikipedia).
- Critics (Thompson; Polygon's Tom Speelman) suggest the Aum Shinrikyo doomsday cult as an inspiration (Wikipedia); no statement by Urasawa confirming this was found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's statements about religion and politics.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Big Comic Spirits). VIZ Media publishes it in English under VIZ Signature. No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Are we the adults we dreamed of becoming as children? Thompson puts the question at the heart of the series, and it is literal: a childhood game about saving the world is stolen by someone who wants to end it, and the boys, now tired 30-somethings, must decide whether they are heroes after all.

**The answer the story gives.** Ordinary people can stop a lie, but only if they remember, take responsibility and stand together. The villain is not a monster from nowhere: he grew out of the same playground, from envy, humiliation and being ignored. The way out runs through confession: from general knowledge, Kenji must admit a small wrong he let another boy carry (to verify).

**Characters as arguments.** Kenji argues that an ordinary life is still worth risking for others. Friend argues that people want to be told what to believe, and for a long time he is right. Otcho argues that grief can be turned into protection. Kanna argues that outsiders (Thai and Chinese gangs, the scapegoated) can be united. Sadakiyo and Yamane argue that it is never too late to turn back. Manjome shows how a con man becomes a true believer in power.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Book of Prophecy, a child's story that becomes a script for terror. The Friend symbol: an eye on a pointing hand. Masks. Expo '70 and the Tower of the Sun as national nostalgia. Kenji's guitar and his song. Virtual reality that rewrites memory. The secret base: friendship's first home.

**Traditions it echoes.** Stephen King's It (Thompson), the cult-thriller, the end-of-the-world prophecy, and the Japanese memory of Aum Shinrikyo (Thompson; Polygon). Christian readers hear the warning against false christs who perform signs (Matthew 24:24); Sonntagsblatt reads it as a study of the messiah as manipulator.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarianism is the clear target. Secular humanism wins: miracles are tricks, science saves. Communitarianism is the means: friends and allies. Liberalism is defended in practice, not in speeches. Religion itself is not attacked: the fake faith is the villain's, while the Pope and a priest are shown as good.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (1997-2000, with 1969-73 flashbacks) are a conspiracy mystery ending on Bloody New Year's Eve. Volumes 6-15 (2014-2015) follow Kanna and Kyoko under the Friends' rule, ending with Friend's "resurrection." Volumes 16-22 and 21st Century Boys (3FE) are resistance and resolution. Volume divisions here are from general knowledge (to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do so many people in the story want to believe in Friend?
2. Kenji did something small and wrong as a child. Why does it matter so much by the end?
3. Keroyon and Konchi ran away when Kenji called. What would you have done?
4. How does Friend use fear of a disease to control people?
5. What makes music such a strong weapon against Friend?
