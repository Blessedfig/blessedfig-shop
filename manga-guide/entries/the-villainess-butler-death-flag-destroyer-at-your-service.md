**Premise.** Cyril, a boy who is the son of the Rosenberg family's head butler, remembers his past life and realizes he is a minor character in an otome (romance) game, "Light and Darkness Espressivo": the butler of Sophia Rosenberg, the game's villainess. In the game Sophia loses Prince Alforth to the heroine, Alicia, falls into darkness, harasses her with Cyril's help, and both are sentenced to death (MyAnimeList character page; novel synopsis on AniList). To change that, Cyril becomes Sophia's personal butler earlier than in the game and raises her to be a talented, beautiful young lady who could win the prince; instead she falls for Cyril and ignores the prince (KADOKAWA vol. 1; Manga UP!). At the Royal academy, where Cyril is a junior-high-level student (MyAnimeList), they face faction quarrels, the arrival of the heroine, a student council whose president, Fol, faces a fate she cannot escape, a school play with the game's own title, and another reincarnated person at the school (web novel contents; KADOKAWA vol. 2). In the novel's third part a neighboring prince proposes to Sophia at the urging of a princess who wants Cyril for herself (KADOKAWA vol. 3); how much of this the manga covers before its last chapter is not confirmed. Readers on MangaUpdates disagree whether the manga was cut short or simply rushed its ending.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is a person's fate written? Cyril knows the script: Sophia becomes cruel and is executed. The story asks whether patient care can change the person, and so the ending.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes: change the causes and the outcome changes. Cyril does not fight fate head-on; he raises Sophia so that she has no reason to fall (KADOKAWA vol. 1; novel synopsis). The twist is that the person he formed chooses him rather than the prince.

**Characters as arguments.** Cyril argues for service as love and foresight as duty. Sophia argues that a person formed by kindness becomes kind. Alicia, the heroine, is no longer the victim she was in the game. Fol, the council president, raises the harder case of a fate that seems unavoidable (KADOKAWA vol. 2).

**Symbols and motifs.** "Death flags" (game signs of a coming bad end); the game's title staged as a school play; the butler's uniform; the academy.

**Traditions it echoes.** The faithful-servant story (Jeeves, the Japanese shitsuji), the governess-and-pupil tale, and Proverbs 22:6 on training a child, which some readers will recognize. Its reincarnation is a genre device rather than a religious claim.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Classical virtue ethics and traditionalism are affirmed: virtue is formed by training within a stable order. Existentialism appears as refusing the script. No lens is attacked.

**Shifts across the series.** Early chapters: Cyril's memories and Sophia's training (ch. 1-6, per the Japanese chapter list). Middle: the academy entrance exam and school life (ch. 7 onward), factions and the heroine. Late: the student council, the play and rivals for both leads (novel parts 2-3); the manga's last chapters are reported as rushed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Cyril knows how Sophia's story ends in the game. Why does he change her upbringing instead of fighting the heroine?
2. Sophia falls for Cyril instead of the prince. Is that a happy ending for both of them?
3. What is the difference between serving someone and controlling them?
4. Is there anything in your life that feels like a "death flag"? What could you change early?
5. Fol seems to have a fate she cannot escape. Do you believe some things are fixed?
