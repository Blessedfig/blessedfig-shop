**Premise.** Tokyo, renamed Teito, 2020. Minato Sahashi, 19, bright but timid, has failed his university entrance exams twice. Musubi, a cheerful fighter, falls from the sky onto him: she is a Sekirei, one of 108 super-powered beings (mostly women) grown from alien embryos found by the MBI corporation. A Sekirei is "winged" (bonded and powered up) by a kiss from a human with the right genes, her Ashikabi. MBI's eccentric chairman Hiroto Minaka runs the Sekirei Plan, a city-wide elimination game in which Sekirei fight until one is left; the losers are "terminated" (they lose their bond and are taken away) (ch. 1; Wikipedia). Minato ends up winging six Sekirei (Musubi; Kusano, a child who controls plants; Matsu, a hacker; the proud water-user Tsukiumi; the wind-user Kazehana; and Homura, a fire-user whose body becomes female) and they live at Izumo Inn under the fearsome landlady Miya. He learns that his mother runs the research and that Minaka is his father (ch. 100, 102). In the final battle on Kouten, his Sekirei join their strength so that Musubi can defeat Miya; Musubi stays a year as Kouten's guardian, the terminated Sekirei are revived, and the epilogue reunites everyone (ch. 177-187; v19; Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Bonds that are freely chosen and faithfully kept are stronger than any game the powerful set up; a timid young man finds courage by protecting the people who chose him.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a bond be real if a corporation designed it, genes decided it and a game master set it up as a weapon? Minato and the Sekirei are thrown together by MBI's Plan, and the series asks whether they can turn that bond into a free choice.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, when it is chosen and kept with care. The Sekirei who pick their Ashikabi (Musubi, Kusano, Tsukiumi) thrive; those winged by force are the story's victims. The final battle is won by six Sekirei pooling their strength, and the ending undoes the game's losses (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Minato argues that protecting is stronger than commanding. Minaka argues that life is a contest with one winner, and loses. Higa argues for control through money and blackmail, and loses Uzume's loyalty. Karasuba argues for fighting as an end in itself. Uzume argues for love unto death, and is rewarded with a second life. Homura argues for refusing to be a trophy, even by dying, and is talked down.

**Symbols and motifs.** Wings of light at the winging; the crest on a Sekirei's back; Izumo Inn, a home with rules; Kouten, the sky-island where the game ends; the wagtail that taught the gods to love.

**Traditions it echoes.** The battle royale, the boarding-house harem comedy and Japanese myth. Its stand on consent echoes modern liberal ideas; its harem ending runs against most traditions' teaching on marriage (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right and corporate control are condemned; consent and found family are celebrated; hedonism is the art's mode more than the story's argument.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-7 (ch. 1-65) gather Minato's household and survive the first stages, with the heaviest comedy and fan service. Volumes 8-14 (ch. 66-141) bring the Third Stage, Uzume's death and Minaka's secret. Volumes 15-18 (ch. 142-187) move to Kamikura Island and Kouten; vol. 19 is the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does the story treat forced winging as wrong? What makes a bond freely chosen?
2. Minato wins by protecting, not commanding. Is that what makes him strong?
3. The art shows a lot of nudity. Does that fit the story's message about respecting people?
4. Kusano is a child among adults. How does the story treat her, and how should it?
5. Uzume dies to protect someone. What does the story say about sacrifice?
