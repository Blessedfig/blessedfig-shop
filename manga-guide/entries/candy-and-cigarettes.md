**Premise.** Raizou Hiraga, 65, has just retired from the Tokyo police's VIP bodyguard unit. His grandson has a rare illness whose care costs about a million yen a month; he has sold his house and spent his pension, so he answers an ad for someone with martial arts and marksmanship skills. The ad comes from "Desperado Books," a used-book shop that fronts for a secret government agency, run by a calm young woman, Kinume, and an 11-year-old elementary school girl, Miharu Suzukaze (ANN encyclopedia; Young Magazine page). Miharu is a master assassin: she kills, and Raizou cleans up (Seven Seas). Their targets are criminals out of the law's reach; the first is a corrupt finance minister Raizou once guarded, and when he hesitates, Miharu shoots (AniList review). Miharu wants revenge on Musou Saburou, head of the "Great Chain," who murdered her family (vol. 2). The hunt goes from Japan to Naples (vol. 4), Las Vegas and a terror attack (vol. 5-6), a Middle East war zone (vol. 8-9) and New York, where the two fight a "New World Order" conspiracy to trigger a world depression and meddle in a US presidential election (vol. 10-11) (Penguin Random House volume synopses).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does an adult owe a child who has already been made into a killer? Raizou takes the job for money, to keep his grandson alive. Partnered with a girl the same age as his grandson, he has to decide whether he is her accomplice, her protector, or both.

**The answer the story gives.** Protect her where you can, and stay loyal. Raizou tries once to take the killing on himself so that Miharu does not have to, but, as the AniList reviewer complains, the story returns her to the gun within a few chapters (vol. 6). The answer the series gives in practice is partnership in a violent world rather than escape from it.

**Characters as arguments.** Raizou argues that duty to family justifies dirty work, and that a child should still be treated as a child. Miharu argues for revenge, then for mercy toward other children used as weapons (vol. 8-10). The agency argues that some criminals can only be stopped by killing. The villains, from the finance minister to the "New World Order," argue for money and control, and lose.

**Symbols and motifs.** Candy (the child) and cigarettes (the old man). The used bookstore as a front. The grandson in his hospital bed. "Pulp" as the chapter label, marking the series as pulp crime fiction. My own framing.

**Traditions it echoes.** Hardboiled and neo-noir crime fiction (Wikipedia's genre listing); the "girl assassin with an older partner" pairing familiar from films such as Leon: The Professional (from general knowledge; to verify); Inoue's earlier COPPELION, also about girls sent into danger by the state (from general knowledge; to verify).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Strength rules is the world the pair move through; utilitarian justice (kill the guilty for the greater good) is the agency's premise and is never seriously challenged; pragmatism is Raizou's method; critical theory's suspicion of elites shapes the villains (finance minister, central banker, a world-market conspiracy); authoritarian surveillance is fought in vol. 7.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (Pulp 1-14): Tokyo, the agency, the hunt for Musou. Volumes 4-6 (Pulp 15-29): Naples, Las Vegas, the airport attack and Miharu's revenge. Volumes 7-9 (Pulp 30-44): the Falcone family, the Middle East and Bertha. Volumes 10-11 (Pulp 45-54): New York, the White House and the "New World Order."

**Discussion questions.**
1. Raizou took the job to save his grandson. Was that a good enough reason?
2. Miharu is the same age as Raizou's grandson. How should that change how he treats her?
3. The agency only kills criminals the law cannot reach. Does that make it right?
4. Miharu tells another girl she does not have to be a killer. Why can't she take her own advice?
5. What would a happy ending for Miharu look like?
