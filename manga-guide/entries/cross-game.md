**Premise.** Ko Kitamura, son of a sporting-goods shop owner, grows up next to the Tsukishima family's batting center and café and its four daughters: Ichiyo, Wakaba, Aoba and Momiji. Wakaba, born the same day as Ko, treats him as her boyfriend and dreams of watching him pitch at Koshien, the national high-school championship; Aoba, a year younger and a natural pitcher, resents him for taking her sister's attention. In fifth grade Wakaba drowns at a summer swimming camp (v1). Four years later Ko, who has been secretly training, enters Seishu High, where a new coach, Daimon, has recruited elite transfer students and banished Ko and his friends to a second-string "portable" team. They beat the varsity in a rematch and the coach is removed (v3-v7). Over two more summers, with Aoba (barred from official games as a girl) helping to train him, rivals like Yuhei Azuma turning teammate, and Akane, a girl who looks like Wakaba, moving in next door, Ko chases Wakaba's dream. Seishu beats Ryuo in the 12th inning to reach Koshien (v17), and Ko and Aoba leave the final chapter holding hands (Wikipedia; List of Cross Game chapters; Suitable for Treatment).

## Life message

**Grief does not have to stop you: you honor the people you have lost by keeping your promises to them through quiet, patient effort, and you let yourself love again.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you go on after losing someone you love? Ko loses Wakaba when they are eleven. The series asks what we owe the dead, whether you can love again without betraying them, and how grief and resentment can turn into something good.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep faith quietly. Ko never makes speeches about Wakaba; he trains in secret for years, throwing against a wall until an old man remembers him (v16, Suitable for Treatment), and pitches her team to Koshien (v17). Aoba's resentment of Ko turns out to be her grief (Manga Report). The answer is not to forget Wakaba but to let her dream carry the living forward, and to let Ko and Aoba love each other.

**Characters as arguments.** Ko argues for steady effort and understatement. Aoba argues, at first, that loving someone new betrays the dead, and slowly learns otherwise. Coach Daimon is the case for winning by buying talent, and he loses (v7). Yuhei Azuma, the star recruit who stays, shows talent learning loyalty. Akane, who looks like Wakaba, tests whether the characters love a person or a memory.

**Symbols and motifs.** Seasons and the sisters' names (Ichiyo, Wakaba "young leaf," Aoba "green leaf," Momiji "autumn leaf"; Japanese meanings from general knowledge); Wakaba's birthday wish list; the batting center; the 100 mph fastball; summer rain; wordless panels.

**Traditions it echoes.** Adachi's own Touch (a dead loved one's dream carried by the survivor), the Japanese sports manga of perseverance, and the slice-of-life romance. Its themes of keeping promises and honoring the dead echo teachings across many traditions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and communitarianism are celebrated; duty to a promise is honored; stoic restraint is the story's style; might-makes-right is defeated. No religious frame is offered, so each family can bring its own.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (Part One) is childhood and loss. Volumes 2-7 bring Ko into high school and the fight against Daimon's first string. Volumes 8-14 add Aoba to the team, the first summer loss to Ryuo, Akane and new rivals. Volumes 15-17 (Part Three) are the final summer, Akane's surgery and Koshien.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ko never talks much about Wakaba. How does he show he remembers her?
2. Why does Aoba act like she hates Ko? What is she really feeling?
3. Coach Daimon built a team by recruiting stars. Why does the portable team win?
4. Aoba is the best pitcher in the story but cannot play in official games. Is that fair?
5. Is there someone whose hopes you carry? How do you honor them?
