**Premise.** Five years before My Hero Academia, college student Koichi Haimawari, who never made it into a hero high school, uses his weak "Slide and Glide" Quirk to do small good deeds as the "Nice Guy" without a hero license, which is illegal. When thugs attack him and the street performer Kazuho Haneyama ("Pop☆Step," 14 at first per the fan wiki), the masked, Quirkless brawler Knuckleduster saves them and drafts them into unlicensed street-level heroics in Naruhata (ch. 1-5). Knuckleduster is hunting dealers of Trigger, a drug that boosts Quirks and turns users into violent "instant villains," to find his runaway daughter Tamao (ch. 23-26). Along the way the trio meets the young Stain, the young Eraser Head and his lost friend Oboro (ch. 59-65), and pros such as Captain Celebrity and Endeavor. In the long final arcs the villain Number 6 possesses Kazuho with a queen bee to stage a fake hero's debut (ch. 71-84), then tries to kill her in a city-wide blackout; Koichi holds him off without killing him until All Might and Endeavor arrive (ch. 95-123). Koichi ends up working as "the Skycrawler" in America, under a cloud of lawsuits, while Kazuho recovers (ch. 124-126) (fan wiki).

## Life message

**Doing good for the people around you matters more than an official title, but helping outside the rules has real costs, and a true hero protects others without becoming a killer.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Does it take a license to be a hero? Koichi failed to get into a hero school and has a power people mock as cockroach-like, yet he cannot stop helping. The series asks what heroism is when no one authorizes it.

**The answer the story gives.** Heroism is the habit of helping, and it carries costs. Koichi's small deeds grow into saving a city, but the law does not simply excuse him: he faces investigation and lawsuits, and goes abroad to work as a hero (ch. 124-126). Goodness and legality are both taken seriously.

**Characters as arguments.** Koichi argues for humble kindness. Knuckleduster argues that the system misses real suffering, and his own failure as a father shows the cost of rage (ch. 23-26). Naomasa argues for law with mercy. Number 6 is the dark mirror: a man who wants to be seen as a hero so badly that he stages tragedies (ch. 81), and dies wanting proof he existed (ch. 123). Kazuho, used and possessed, is declared a victim, not a criminal (ch. 124).

**Symbols and motifs.** The All Might hoodie as a homemade costume; the tongue blackened by Trigger; bees and the queen as control; the moon Koichi and Kazuho watch (ch. 95); the scar Number 6 leaves on Koichi (ch. 123).

**Traditions it echoes.** The American street-level vigilante comic (Batman, Spider-Man, Watchmen); the Good Samaritan ideal of helping whoever is in front of you; the Japanese tale of the humble hero who rises through effort.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue and community are celebrated. Libertarian and anarchist instincts (help without permission) are explored and then bounded by real consequences. Pacifism appears as a no-kill rule inside a fighting story. Power for its own sake (Trigger, Number 6) is condemned.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-26): the trio forms, Stain's past and Knuckleduster's daughter. Volumes 4-7: side cases (Osaka, Captain Celebrity, Eraser Head's past, ch. 59-65). Volumes 8-10: Kazuho's possession and the Sky Egg (ch. 67-94). Volumes 11-15: the Naruhata lockdown and the ending (ch. 95-126).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Koichi helps people without a license and breaks the law. Was he right? What would have been a better way?
2. Knuckleduster fought crime to find his daughter. What did he have to change to win her back?
3. Number 6 wanted to be remembered. How is that different from Koichi's wish to help?
4. Kazuho was controlled and hurt people. Why do the police treat her as a victim?
5. Early chapters treat Pop☆Step as someone to be rescued. How does she change by the end?
