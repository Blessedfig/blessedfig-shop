**Premise.** In Victorian Britain, two orphan brothers with a hatred of the cruel nobility are adopted into the Moriarty family because the eldest son, Albert, shares their anger. As children, the three kill Albert's abusive brother William, and the older orphan takes William's name; a gas explosion and fire kill the rest of the household (ch. 1; anime ep. 2-3). As adults, William James Moriarty, a mathematics professor, becomes a "consulting criminal" who arranges the deaths of nobles who prey on commoners: a viscount who let a gardener's child die (ch. 2), a count who hunts commoners for sport (ch. 5-6), a noble who hunts and beheads slum children (ch. 10-11). He draws Sherlock Holmes into his plan, casting him as the hero who will expose the "Lord of Crime." In "The Final Problem" (ch. 48-56) William publicly takes the blame for every crime so that nobles and commoners unite against him, intending to die; Holmes refuses to let him and jumps after him from Tower Bridge. William survives, blind in one eye, and the brothers resolve to live and atone (ch. 66-67). Part 2 (from vol. 20) brings William out of retirement to help investigate an addictive street drug (VIZ vol. 20 synopsis).

## Life message

**Cruel privilege deserves to fall, but the man who kills to bring it down still carries real guilt: death is no atonement, and friendship gives him a reason to live and make amends.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A story of in-groups: three adopted brothers bound tighter than blood (Albert kills his biological brother to protect the orphans, ch. 1), a loyal band (Moran, Fred, Jack, Bonde), and rival elites competing for status. Revenge is framed as defense of the weak against predators who prey on children and the poor. |
| Pragmatic | William's crimes are engineered to change public opinion: each murder is staged to expose a noble's abuse (anime ep. 6), and the final plan makes him a common enemy so the classes cooperate (ch. 48-56). Results are real (the House of Lords loses its superiority, anime ep. 24), but the price is many deaths and Albert's imprisonment. |
| Religious | No faith shapes the heroes. The vocabulary is religious: William speaks of his death as repentance (anime ep. 23), Holmes insists death is no atonement and offers to atone together (ch. 55), and the group vows to live on and atone (ch. 66). A villain kills slum children and collects their heads "in the name of God" (ch. 10-11, fan wiki), shown as monstrous. |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of Victorian class privilege, imperial war profiteering (the Duke of Dunderdale sacrifices Moran's unit to prolong a war, ch. 12-14) and press blackmail (Milverton). The Beat's Santilli reads it as an indictment of the aristocracy; Women Write About Comics finds the noble targets implausibly cartoonish. A gender-nonconforming character (Irene Adler living as James Bonde, ch. 17-23 onward) is accepted by the group. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the class system is reformed and William survives his intended death, but many people are killed along the way, William loses an eye, Albert is arrested for the brothers' crimes, and Part 2 opens on a new drug crisis.
Hope source: R — the story's way through is redemption: William, who planned to die as payment for his crimes, is pulled back by Holmes and resolves with his brothers to live on and atone (ch. 55, 66-67); friendship (F), above all the bond between William and Sherlock, is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every tradition welcomes the defense of the poor and the ending's rejection of suicide in favor of atonement. The divide is over the means: Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist and Bahá'í readings stress that murder cannot be justified by its results (C or D), with peace churches the most cautious (D). Progressive, humanist and Satanist frames welcome the class critique and the accepted gender-nonconforming ally more warmly (B), while still flagging the glamor of political violence. Conservative readers note that reform finally comes through the Crown and the law, which tempers the revolutionary premise.

## Creator and publisher context

- Ryosuke Takeuchi (storyboards and layout; Wikipedia notes he is credited exclusively with these) and Hikaru Miyoshi (art). Takeuchi wrote the manga adaptation of All You Need Is Kill and the series Stars; Miyoshi drew Kanshikan Tsunemori Akane (Wikipedia).
- Origin (Wikipedia, Production): Miyoshi's editor suggested a Sherlock Holmes story; Takeuchi joined after William and Sherlock were designed. The focus moved to Moriarty because Takeuchi, who liked heroes as a child, came to empathize with villains as he grew older. Miyoshi made a "gentleman" criminal and a "rough" detective to stand out; the two leads were designed as near-mirrors.
- After Part 1 ended (December 2022), Takeuchi thanked the team on social media and updated his profile to say his involvement was limited to Part 1 (Wikipedia). Who storyboards Part 2 was not confirmed in sources opened (to verify).
- No creator statements on religion or politics were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Jump Square, shonen). VIZ Media (owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan; from general knowledge) publishes it under Shonen Jump, rated T+.
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If the law protects the powerful, may a brilliant person take justice into his own hands? William James Moriarty grows up watching nobles treat the poor as vermin, and decides that only crime can break the system. The series asks whether that is justice, and what it does to the one who carries it out.

**The answer the story gives.** Two answers, held together. The plan works: London unites against the "Lord of Crime," and the House of Lords loses its superiority (anime ep. 24). But the story will not let William's death settle the account. Holmes tells him death is no atonement (ch. 55), William later thanks him for showing that death is not the answer (ch. 67), and the household toasts its resolve to live on and atone (ch. 66). Reform is welcomed; murder is not erased.

**Characters as arguments.** William argues that a few deaths are worth a fairer nation. Holmes argues for truth and for life: he will expose William, but he will not let him die, and yet he shoots Milverton himself (ch. 47), showing how easily the line is crossed. Albert, the heir who chose the orphans over his blood family, accepts punishment for all of them. Louis decides one person is enough reason to live (ch. 55). Whitely, the honest reformer, shows the lawful path and its fragility (ch. 35-39). The villains (Baskerville, Enders, Milverton, Dunderdale) argue nothing; they show what unchecked privilege does.

**Symbols and motifs.** Scarlet eyes and fire (the burning mansion, ch. 1; burning London, ch. 52-56). The Thames, into which both men fall and from which they come back. William's lost eye: he survives, but marked. The two leads drawn as mirror images (Wikipedia, Production). The "consulting criminal" as a twisted Holmes.

**Traditions it echoes.** Conan Doyle's canon (A Study in Scarlet, The Sign of Four, A Scandal in Bohemia, The Final Problem, The Empty House), the picaresque (Nakano), the gentleman thief, and Death Note's genius vigilante (WWAC compares the two). Its ending sides with the old moral teaching that evil may not be done that good may come, while still honoring the cause.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarianism is explored and then checked: the outcome is good, the man who caused it must atone. Duty ethics speaks through Holmes and Watson. Critical theory's critique of class is affirmed in every case. Patriotism is affirmed in its reforming form and rejected in its jingoist form (Dunderdale). Religion appears mostly as language of repentance, and once as a monster's excuse.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-14) are single cases against cruel nobles. Volumes 5-12 (ch. 17-47) widen to the state (Adler's documents, the Ripper, Parliament, Milverton). Volumes 12-14 (ch. 48-56) are the Final Problem. Volumes 15-19 (ch. 57-76) bring the survivors back from New York and turn to atonement. Part 2 (vol. 20 on) is a new case about an addictive drug, with William coming out of retirement.

**Discussion questions.**
1. William thinks killing a few cruel people will save many. What does Holmes say back, and who convinces you?
2. Albert is arrested for crimes he planned with his brothers. Is that justice, or sacrifice?
3. Holmes kills Milverton. Does that change how we judge William?
4. What could Whitely's lawful path have achieved without murder?
5. Why does the story refuse to let William die, even though he wanted to?
