**Premise.** Sumireko Ogawa, 28, won a youth literary prize at 15 and published a book, then stalled for more than a decade; she now works at a Tokyo bookstore (Japanese Wikipedia). On her birthday she reads a cursed book left in the store, and a poem from the Man'yoshu, Japan's oldest poetry collection, turns her back into a child; the curse nearly kills her until her young-looking coworker, Ren Adashino, intervenes (Wikipedia; aniSearch). Ren is not human: he is a drifter from another world who collects the cursed objects behind Tokyo's urban legends so that he and Oto, a middle-school girl he calls his sister, can buy tickets home (Japanese Wikipedia). Sumireko keeps the power to switch freely between her adult and child bodies and joins them. Each arc is a "mystery": a teacher whose fear of bullying becomes a curse (vol. 1-2), the Girl in Crimson (vol. 3), haunted modern devices and the Princess of Books (vol. 4), the King and Queen of Cats (vols. 5-6), a trip to the beach and a first kiss (vol. 7), a station to the otherworld (vol. 8), and a stretch trapped in child form (vol. 9) (Seven Seas synopses). The series mixes horror with comedy and frequent fan service focused on Sumireko.

**Where traditions disagree.** Most frames welcome the story's core: a burned-out adult recovering her gift, and curses that rebound on those who cast them. The divide has two parts. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to a gift you stopped using? Sumireko peaked at 15 and has been stuck for thirteen years. A curse gives her back her childhood body, and with it a question: was it youth she lost, or courage?

**The answer the story gives.** Courage. ANN's Richard Eisenbeis notes that the change is only in her body, so her renewed drive must come from within. With Ren and Oto believing in her, she writes again; Ren reading Oto's book shows what writing can mean to someone (ANN, ep. 3).

**Characters as arguments.** Sumireko argues that it is never too late. Ren argues for practical bargains with the unseen and teases her constantly. Manami is the warning: her fear of bullying hardens into bullying others, and her curses come back to her (ANN, ep. 3). Shizuku shows loyalty to a lost friend.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Elixir of the Moon (youth restored), the cursed book left behind instead of stolen ("reverse shoplifting," aniSearch), the "!" traffic-sign marks of mysteries (MyAnimeList, baowee), tickets to another world.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese kamikakushi tales, the Man'yoshu's poems of the moon's water of youth (from general knowledge; to verify), Hell Girl's "curses return to the caster" (ANN), and the occult-detective genre (Siliconera's Goosebumps comparison).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Occult power is used for good by the heroes. Self-expression is the goal. Feminist readings split: the story questions ageism while its camera dwells on the heroine's body.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: the curse, the bookstore and the teacher's arc. Vols. 3-6: the Girl in Crimson, Shizuku, the King and Queen of Cats. Vol. 7: the kiss and the beach. Vols. 8-9: the otherworld station, the Adashino family's past and a long stretch in child form; Seven Seas raises the rating to 17+.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sumireko stopped writing for years. What helps her start again?
2. Manami tried to stop bullying and became a bully. How did that happen?
3. Curses come back to the people who cast them. Is that fair?
4. Some readers say the drawings of Sumireko distract from the story. What do you think?
5. Would you want your younger body back? What would you be hoping to regain?
