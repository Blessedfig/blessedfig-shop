**Premise.** Tasuku Kaname is a closeted gay high-school student who has recently moved to Onomichi, Hiroshima Prefecture. When classmates find gay pornography on his phone and taunt him, he denies it, but in despair he comes close to killing himself (ch. 1). Just then he sees a stranger seem to jump from a window unharmed. Following them, he finds a "drop-in center" run by the mysterious person everyone calls Someone (Dareka-san). Its members are LGBTQ+ people of different ages: Haruko Daichi and her partner Saki, a lesbian couple planning a wedding; Utsumi, later revealed as a trans man (vol. 3); Misora, a sixth-grader who dresses as a girl at the center and is unsure of their gender; and Tchaiko, an elderly man whose partner of more than 30 years is dying (vol. 4). The members restore old empty houses for a local nonprofit. Tasuku's crush on classmate Touma Tsubaki, a volleyball player who uses anti-gay slurs and is unsettled by his own feelings, runs through the series (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; Manga Bookshelf vol. 3). The publisher's tagline calls it a "tale of gender, sexuality, and self discovery" (Seven Seas vol. 4 page).

## Life message

**A good life comes from finding a place where you can be who you are, slowly and in your own words, among people who accept you; no one has to fully understand themselves, and community, not labels, is what keeps a person alive.**

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who make room for others and tell the truth in their own time end up at peace and among friends; those who mock, out or hide others are shown the harm they do, and most are given a chance to change.

## Creator and publisher context

- **Yuhki Kamatani, on record:** Kamatani came out publicly as X-gender and asexual on Twitter in May 2012, during Shonen Note's serialization; their profile lists "toX" (Anime Feminist creator spotlight; Wikipedia creator page, citing the tweet of May 7, 2012). Wikipedia also describes them as aromantic, citing sources not opened here. In a BuzzFeed Japan interview (2018) Kamatani described being hurt by being treated as a woman as a teenager, for example having to wear a school skirt (Anime Feminist; Wikipedia). These are Kamatani's own public statements; nothing further about their private life is given here.
- **On the series:** According to Anime Feminist, Kamatani decided after the 2016 US election to give Tasuku's story an ending in which he gains peace of mind, and in a Quarterly S interview hoped the ending would save Tasuku, readers and themself "in the face of a darkening society" (article's paraphrase; interview not opened). Kamatani worked with the LGBT support group Trois Couleurs for experiences not their own (Anime Feminist). Wikipedia says Kamatani's experiences are "somewhat reflected" in the work, and Rachel Thorn called it possibly the first trans-authored portrayal of trans experience outside essay manga (Wikipedia, creator page). No statements on religion or party politics found.
- **Publisher:** Shogakukan (Hibana, then the Manga One app; Big Comics Special). English: Seven Seas Entertainment, whose marketing manager said the series shows "so many different ways that people shape their identities" (Wikipedia). Seven Seas tags it under LGBT+ topics (search result, not opened).
- No controversy involving the creator or publisher was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person who is ashamed of who they are find a reason to live? Tasuku begins the story trying to disappear: classmates have found gay pornography on his phone, he has denied everything, and he is ready to do something he "can't take back" (TV Tropes). The series asks what stands between a hated self and death, and whether identity is something you must name before you can live with it.

**The answer the story gives.** A place and people, more than a label. Tasuku is not saved by an argument but by an image: a stranger who jumps from a window and is unhurt (ch. 1). Following them leads to the drop-in center, where no one asks him to explain himself. Kamatani's own line, "only natural to fail to understand yourself" (Shonen Note interview, via Anime Feminist), fits the series: Dareka-san never tells Tasuku who he is, and is described as a "mirror" rather than a mentor (Anime Feminist). Peace comes slowly, through work done together (restoring old houses) and through other members' stories.

**Characters as arguments.** Tasuku argues that shame can become self-acceptance. Misora argues that pain turned outward hurts the nearest person; their attacks on Tasuku (vol. 2) come from their own fear (Manga Bookshelf vol. 2; Okazu). Utsumi argues that politeness can hide great anger; his patience with constant misgendering breaks in vol. 3 (Manga Bookshelf vol. 3). Shoko argues for a shallow tolerance that still sorts people into "like other homosexuals" or not. Tsubaki argues that casual homophobia can sit next to real affection, and that a person can apologize. Haruko and Saki argue that love wants to be seen; Tchaiko and Seichirou show what thirty years of hiding cost (TV Tropes).

**Symbols and motifs.** Dusk and the title's "who is that?" pun; Dareka-san's name, "Someone," which ANN's Silverman finds "a bit too on the nose"; walls tearing, mirrors breaking and a building collapsing to show Tasuku's inner state (ANN; Manga Bookshelf vol. 2); goldfish for Misora; empty houses being restored, as people are; Tchaikovsky's First Symphony, "Winter Daydreams" (Wikipedia); the Shimanami bridges linking islands.

**Traditions it echoes.** Magical realism in the vein of Latin American fiction; the Japanese "I-novel" honesty about shame; the support-group or consciousness-raising circle of LGBTQ activism. Religious readers may see in the drop-in center the ideal of a community that welcomes the outcast; Buddhist readers may see the loosening of a fixed self. These are readings, not the story's claims.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Progressive and communitarian views are affirmed through the community and the wedding. Expressive individualism is affirmed but softened: the self is discovered slowly and need not be labeled. Traditional family expectations are shown mainly as the source of hiding. Religious and traditional views on sexuality are not voiced by any character in the sources found.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-5): crisis, the drop-in center, Tasuku admits to the members he loves a boy (ch. 5). Vol. 2 (ch. 6-10): Misora's story; it ends with Misora attacking Tasuku in public and his world "collapsing" (Okazu; Manga Bookshelf). Vol. 3 (ch. 11-15): Tsubaki's slur and apology, Utsumi's anger, Tasuku's confrontation with Tsubaki. Vol. 4 (ch. 16-23): Saki outed, Tchaiko and Seichirou's story, Dareka-san's asexuality (ch. 21), and a final chapter that sets the wedding beside Seichirou's death (TV Tropes). The tone moves from isolation to belonging.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tasuku is saved by seeing someone "jump" and survive. Why might an image reach him when words could not?
2. Dareka-san never tells anyone who they are. Is that kinder, or less helpful, than giving advice?
3. Tsubaki uses a slur and later apologizes. What makes an apology real?
4. Saki is outed by someone who did not mean harm. Whose story is it to tell?
5. Your family's tradition has its own teaching about identity, love, suffering and suicide. Where does this story agree with it, and where does it not?
