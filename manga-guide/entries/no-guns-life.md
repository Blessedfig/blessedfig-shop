**Premise.** After a great war, many former soldiers have been rebuilt as cyborgs called Extended, and the megacorporation Berühren that makes their Extensions dominates the city. Juzo Inui, an Extended with no memory of his past and a giant revolver for a head, works as a Resolver who handles trouble involving the Extended. He cannot fire his own head-gun; someone he trusts must pull the trigger, because he refuses to be a mere tool (Wikipedia). A renegade Extended bursts into his office with Tetsuro Arahabaki, a boy of about 12 (The Manga Critic) who is the son of Berühren's CEO; experiments have cut his tendons and vocal cords and implanted Harmony, a device that lets him control Extended bodies remotely (Wikipedia; ch. 1-6). Juzo takes the job and protects Tetsuro from Berühren's agents (Karen, Hugh Cunningham, later Pepper and the Gun Slave Unit Seven), the Kyusei mafia, the Extended Management Squad of Olivier Vandeberme, and the anti-Extended group Spitzbergen led by Andy Wachowski, the scientist who first theorized Extensions. Later arcs follow the murders behind a fugitive first-generation Extended (vols. 2-3), Mary's brother Victor, the serial killer called the Dismantler (vols. 3-5), Juzo's lost wartime past in a prison for Over-Extended (vols. 8-9), and Berühren's board, Wurzel, whose COO Honest seizes control of every Extended in the city to overwrite their minds (vols. 11-13; VIZ blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone a person rather than an instrument? Juzo was built to be a gun, Tetsuro was cut open to host a device, and Berühren treats everyone as parts. The series asks who owns a body, and who decides what it is for.

**The answer the story gives.** Persons own themselves. Juzo's rule that only someone he trusts may pull his trigger turns a weapon into a choice (Wikipedia). Protecting Tetsuro, who did nothing to earn it, is the act that shows Juzo is more than his design. The final arc sets one company's attempt to overwrite every Extended mind against a sacrifice that frees Juzo to stop it (VIZ vol. 13).

**Characters as arguments.** Juzo argues for self-ownership and keeping your word. Tetsuro argues for using power to free others, though he makes rash choices. Hugh Cunningham argues that people are cogs; Honest that minds can be rewritten for the company. Wachowski argues that the technology itself is the sin; the story grants his grievance but not his terror. Kronen argues that the Extended are dangerous machines. Mary and Olivier argue for practical care inside a broken system.

**Symbols and motifs.** The revolver head that cannot fire itself; the trigger as trust; medicated cigarettes as dependence on the company; Harmony as total control; rain and rust (Juzo fears water); the Kyusei Pit as the city's free underside.

**Traditions it echoes.** Hard-boiled detective fiction and noir (The Manga Critic calls it robo noir), cyberpunk from Ghost in the Shell onward, and the soldier-who-wants-out story. Kant's rule never to use a person merely as a means runs through it; religious readers may hear the image of God in persons, and Buddhist readers the question of what self survives a rebuilt body.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is explored, not rejected: Extensions help people, but ownership of the technology corrupts. Duty ethics and existentialism are affirmed in Juzo. Critical theory's critique of corporate power is central. Religion appears mainly as a militant fringe with a point.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-11): Tetsuro, Karen, Hugh and the Gondry case. Vols. 3-5 (ch. 12-31): the Dismantler and Victor; Pepper and Seven. Vols. 6-7 (ch. 32-44): Spitzbergen, Colt, and Io nearly destroys Juzo (anime ends ch. 41). Vols. 8-9 (ch. 45-56): the Over-Extended prison and Juzo's past. Vols. 10-13 (ch. 57-82): Wurzel, Suiso and Honest's Wide-Range Harmony.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Juzo will only let people he trusts pull his trigger. Why does that matter to him?
2. Is it wrong to replace parts of your body with machines? When might it be right?
3. Spitzbergen thinks Extensions offend God. What is fair in their complaint, and what is wrong in their methods?
4. Juzo says he hates kids but protects Tetsuro. Why?
5. Who in the real world gets treated as a tool?
