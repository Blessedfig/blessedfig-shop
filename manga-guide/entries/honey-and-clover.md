**Premise.** Takemoto (19), Mayama (22) and Morita (24, an eccentric eight-year sculpture student who vanishes for weeks and returns with piles of cash) live in a cheap apartment and study at an art college in Tokyo. Professor Hanamoto (Shuji, in his thirties) introduces Hagu (Hagumi, 18), the daughter of his cousin, whom he has raised like a father since her grandmother's death; she is tiny, shy, childlike in looks and a painting prodigy. Takemoto and Morita both fall for her. Ayumi Yamada, a potter, loves Mayama, who loves Rika Harada, an older widowed architect still crippled in body and spirit by the crash that killed her husband (AniList; Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Over five years the friends graduate or fail to, hunt for jobs, and search for who they are: Takemoto's job collapses and he rides off on a bicycle across Japan, finding work with a temple-restoration crew (v6-7); Yamada slowly opens to Nomiya, Mayama's coworker (v8); Morita and his brother take over the company that took their father's lab (v8-9, Japanese Wikipedia); and Hagu's painting hand is badly injured in an accident at the school festival (v9). In the end Hagu chooses art over romance and asks Hanamoto to stay by her side during her recovery, and Takemoto leaves for the restoration work (v10; Soliloquy in Blue).

## Life message

**Not every love is returned and not every dream comes true, but the time you spend loving, working and searching with friends is not wasted; it makes you who you are.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame values the friendship, hard work and self-giving love at the heart of the series, so grades are mostly A and B. Traditions that forbid or caution against alcohol (Muslim, Bahá'í, Latter-day Saint, Adventist, holiness churches) weigh the comic drinking more heavily. Progressive readers split: they welcome the mental-health storylines and women artists but object to the childlike drawing of Hagu and to stalking played for laughs. The ending, with a guardian who loves his ward, unsettles many readers of every background (Soliloquy comments).

## Creator and publisher context

- Chica Umino (pen name; born August 30, 1965, Adachi, Tokyo). Influences include Fusako Kuramochi, Moto Hagio and Western children's books such as Anne of Green Gables, especially in unusual family arrangements. Later work: March Comes In like a Lion (Kodansha Manga Award and Tezuka Osamu Cultural Prize Grand Prize, 2014); character designs for Eden of the East (Wikipedia, Chica Umino).
- On-record intent: the series grew out of her imagining what life would have been like with friends she did not have (Japanese Wikipedia); for the ending she had in mind a development like Moto Hagio's Golden Lilac (Japanese Wikipedia). In 2023 she called the series a spell that kept the fire in her life burning and said it is painful for her to reopen (ANN news, 2023).
- The series moved magazines twice as two closed, and moved from a fashion-oriented "stylish campus comedy" toward romance for adult women (Japanese Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Takarajimasha, then Shueisha; VIZ Media in English (Shojo Beat), rated T+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with a love, or a talent, that does not give back what you put in? Every main character loves someone who loves someone else, and every one must decide what to make of a life that does not go as hoped.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep loving, keep working, and let go when it is time. The time itself was the treasure. Takemoto's final reflection, as he leaves on the train with Hagu's four-leaf-clover sandwiches, is that the friends were all looking for the same thing, and that the memory will stay with him (ch. 64; Soliloquy in Blue). Love that is not returned is not wasted.

**Characters as arguments.** Takemoto argues for ordinary people: no genius, but honest effort and humility, and he finds his place mending old temples. Morita argues for genius and freedom, but his life is shaped by loyalty to his father and brother. Yamada argues that constancy proves love, and must learn that holding on can also be fear (v8, Soliloquy). Mayama shows devotion edging into obsession. Hanamoto embodies love that asks nothing. Hagu, possessed by her art, chooses it over romance, which some readers find heroic and others sad.

**Symbols and motifs.** The four-leaf clover (searched for in v2 and baked into the farewell sandwiches, ch. 64); the bicycle wheel and the departing train that open and close the series (Harvard Independent); the art college as a protected season of life; Hagu's injured hand.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese youth ensemble stories, Moto Hagio's shojo (Golden Lilac), and coming-of-age novels like Anne of Green Gables in its unusual families. Readers from faith traditions will hear echoes of 1 Corinthians 13 in Hanamoto's love and of vocation in Takemoto's journey; Buddhist readers will hear impermanence and non-attachment.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism wins: each character must choose a path. Expressive individualism is honored but tempered by duty and care. Communitarian friendship is the story's comfort. No lens is mocked.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-21) are episodic campus comedy with growing love triangles. Vols. 4-6 (ch. 22-40) turn to graduation, job hunting and Rika's past. Vol. 7 (ch. 41-46) is Takemoto's journey and Hagu's art class. Vols. 8-10 (ch. 47-64) bring Yamada's choice, Mayama and Rika, Morita's family story, Hagu's injury and the ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Takemoto says he did not ride away to "find himself." What did he find?
2. Yamada thinks giving up on Mayama would mean her love was not sincere. Is she right?
3. Is Mayama's following Rika romantic, funny or a problem? Why does the story treat it as a joke?
4. Why does Hagu choose Hanamoto's help over Morita's love? What would you have chosen?
5. Hanamoto gives up a great deal for Hagu without telling her. Is that love, or something else?
