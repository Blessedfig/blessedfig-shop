**Premise.** Wazu, an ordinary village boy, was engaged to his childhood friend Aria, but she broke it off to become engaged to the Hero, who mocked him; rejected by the Hero's party, he left home (Titan blurb via MangaUpdates; Wikipedia). He trains alone for two years on the "Mountain" and comes down without realizing he has become an S-rank adventurer strong enough to knock out a dragon king (BOOK☆WALKER vol. 1). In vol. 1 he saves Sienna, a ten-year-old elf, from a dragon by punching it, defends the elf village and gains a baby dragon companion; he falls for Salona, a beautiful elf warrior who does not return his feelings (The Slings & Arrows; Wikipedia). Heartbroken again, he travels to the fortress city of Rinikku while Salona leaves the village to look for him (vol. 2). There, after an awkward visit to a brothel, he guards Tata, a young woman bound by her father's debt, and uncovers that the city's lord arranged the debt to keep her from him (vol. 3, PRH). In the kingdom of Marnbond he frees Princess Naminissa's brother and sister from cursed items that control them, makes an antidote to the royal family's curse with a necromancer named Neil, and stages a battle to expose the plotter, meeting another S-rank fighter, Flugel Razer (vols. 4-6, BOOK☆WALKER).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with a broken heart? Wazu loses his fiancée to the man who mocked him and walks away from everything (Wikipedia). The series asks whether pain can become strength, and what that strength is for.

**The answer the story gives.** Keep going, get stronger, and use it for others. Wazu's two years alone make him the strongest adventurer without his noticing (BOOK☆WALKER vol. 1), and he spends that power on rescues: a child from a dragon, a woman from a rigged debt (vol. 3), a royal family from curses (vols. 4-5). Romantically, the story refuses him an easy win: love stays the one thing strength cannot fix.

**Characters as arguments.** The Hero argues that status is everything and that the weak deserve mockery; the story sets Wazu up to disprove him. The city lord argues that power entitles you to possess a person and uses debt to do it (vol. 3); he is exposed. The Marnbond plotter argues for control through magic and is unmasked (vol. 6). Salona's leaving her village to find him (vol. 2) argues that love can come late.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Mountain, a place of exile that becomes a place of making; the baby dragon, a defeated threat turned companion; debts and curses as chains that Wazu breaks.

**Traditions it echoes.** The underdog's training journey (the hermit's mountain, the boxing film), the knight-errant who rescues the trapped, and the light-novel harem comedy. For some readers it echoes the moral that strength is for service.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right is explored and bent toward protection: villains who use power to control lose. Hedonism shows in the fan service rather than in any argued philosophy. Virtue is carried by Wazu's decency. Reviewers (The Slings & Arrows) find the romance tropes tired; fans enjoy the comedy (MyAnimeList).

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the elf village and the backstory. Vols. 2-3: the city of Rinikku, rival suitors, the brothel and Tata. Vols. 4-6: the kingdom of Marnbond, cursed royals and the hunt for the plotter. The manga stops around ch. 31 on hiatus (MangaUpdates).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Wazu leaves home after his fiancée chooses the Hero. Was leaving the right choice? What else could he have done?
2. How does Wazu use his strength, and how do the villains use theirs?
3. The story makes jokes about women falling for Wazu, and a young child offers to join his "harem." What does that kind of joke say about how the story sees girls and women?
4. Tata is trapped by her father's debt. What makes that situation unjust?
5. Why does the story keep Wazu unlucky in love even when he is the strongest?
