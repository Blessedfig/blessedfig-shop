**Premise.** Ryu Yamada is a bored second-year delinquent at Suzaku High (TV Tropes). He falls down a staircase onto Urara Shiraishi, the top student, and wakes in her body; they learn that a kiss swaps them back and forth (ch. 1-2). With student council vice-president Toranosuke Miyamura they revive the Supernatural Studies Club and, joined by Miyabi Ito and later Kentaro Tsubaki, hunt for the school's seven "witches," students whose powers (mind reading, charm, precognition, memory erasure and others) work by a kiss on the lips. Gathering all seven lets one wish be made (ch. 86-88); Yamada wishes the powers away and he and Urara become a couple (ch. 89-90, v11). The powers return in new forms, with male witches, a student council war against the Japanese chess (shogi) club, lost memories of Yamada's first year, and finally Urara's disappearance: she turns out to be the original witch, who traded her memories of him for the powers so the two could meet. Yamada restores her memory at graduation. A ten-year epilogue shows the friends as adults; Yamada and Urara marry and later tell the story to their two children (Wikipedia; ch. 243, fan wiki; TV Tropes).

## Creator and publisher context

- Miki Yoshikawa created Flunk Punk Rumble (2006-2011) before this series, both in Weekly Shonen Magazine (Wikipedia).
- On-record statements (as summarized by Wikipedia from a Comic Natalie interview and her Anime Expo 2015 appearance): her editor asked for a one-shot; of two ideas, the other (The Demon's Classroom) was chosen first but was hard to run "because the main character was a grade school girl," and the Yamada prototype became chapter 1. She had the body-swap idea during Flunk Punk Rumble and "researched some medical books on the differences between men and women," saying "I was wondering how a guy finding himself in a girl's body would react, and the reverse." She first considered gender swapping as a power and rejected it. Delinquent heroes come easily because "she grew up in the lower end of Tokyo"; the characters "reflect different aspects of herself." She tries to vary each kiss and draws most from the side; same-gender kisses were "not intended to target any specific demographic but just 'an inevitable outcome.'" She worked by hand with five assistants (Wikipedia, citing the interviews). No statements on religion or politics were found.
- She appeared at Anime Expo 2015 as a guest of honor, hosted by Crunchyroll (Wikipedia; ANN news item found in search, not opened).
- Publisher: Kodansha. English: Kodansha USA, rated 16+. Before that, Crunchyroll Manga carried the English digital chapters under a 2013 partnership with Kodansha (Wikipedia).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to really know another person? The premise answers literally: Yamada lives inside Urara's body, and later inside many classmates' bodies, and learns what their lives are like. The series keeps asking whether that kind of knowledge is enough, or whether being remembered and chosen matters more.

**The answer the story gives.** Knowing someone means taking up their problems as your own. Swapping with Miyabi, Yamada learns of her debts and loneliness (ch. 8-9); each witch's power turns out to mirror a hidden wish, and the club helps her out of it. Memory then becomes the real stake. Witches can erase people from everyone's minds, and the worst fate in the story is to be forgotten (Leona, ch. 85; Nancy, ch. 165). The final mystery is that Urara herself made the bargain that created the powers, giving up her memories so that she and Yamada could meet (Wikipedia). The answer is that love is a choice to remember: Yamada finds a way to bring back her memories at graduation, then, ten years later, marries her (ch. 243).

**Characters as arguments.** Yamada is the case for loyalty: at the first ceremony he wishes the powers away for the whole school rather than for himself (ch. 88). Urara is the case for quiet sacrifice. Toranosuke is the case for cleverness and advantage; he plans, uses powers for the council and becomes president (ch. 92), but stays a friend. The councils and the shogi club argue for control, and lose. Ushio argues that what you lose may be won back at any cost; he is rewarded with a family in the epilogue (TV Tropes).

**Symbols and motifs.** The kiss, a sign of intimacy turned into a key that opens other people's minds and bodies; it is repeated so often that it becomes casual, which is both the joke and the problem the romance must answer (the couple's wedding kiss, ch. 243). Memory and notebooks (the missing witch notes, ch. 97). The old school building and the fire foreseen in vol. 5. The ceremony of the seven witches and the single wish. The book Yamada reads to his children, which frames the whole tale (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** The body-swap comedy (Freaky Friday, Your Name later); the school mystery club; Japanese legends of school "wonders"; Yoshikawa's own delinquent comedy, Flunk Punk Rumble. The ecchi shonen comedy supplies its bath scenes and panty jokes.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian and virtue lenses are shown working: friends save each other, and effort earns the entrance exam (ch. 240). Hedonism appears only as fan-service comedy, which the story enjoys rather than critiques; it shows peeping toms as wrong while letting the hero and his friends look, and TV Tropes notes Yamada mostly does not use his powers for "perverted ends." Progressive readers find routine same-sex kisses and a gay character treated without mockery; the Fandom Post reviewer asked for "equal opportunity fanservice." Religious frames see no religion but weigh the casual kissing and nudity.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-11 are the first witch hunt and end with the wish and the couple's confession (ch. 90); the anime covers only this. Volumes 11-19 bring a new council, male witches and the shogi club war; volumes 19-21 erase and restore Yamada's first-year memories; volumes 22-28 follow third year, Urara's disappearance and the epilogue. Reviewers say the art grows more stylized and the fan service shifts with it (TV Tropes "Art Evolution"), and the last arc feels rushed (AniList; TV Tropes).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yamada learns about Miyabi's life by living in her body. What do you learn about people when you see their lives from the inside?
2. Kisses in this story are tools. Does that change what a kiss means? Who should decide whether a kiss happens?
3. Why is being forgotten the worst thing that can happen to these characters?
4. The story shows peeping toms as wrong, but jokes about its heroes looking. How do you feel about that difference?
5. Urara gives up her memories so she and Yamada could meet. Is that a good sacrifice?
