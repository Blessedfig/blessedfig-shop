**Premise.** Etsuro Kurosu, a shut-in who has failed his university entrance exams twice (Mangapedia), tries to hang himself in his apartment; the attempt fails and leaves a hole in the wall (Japanese Wikipedia; MAL reviews). Through it he can see his neighbor, Rio Miyaichi, a pretty, quiet university student who cooks well and is two years older (Kodansha; AniList). Watching her becomes his reason to live, until he sees her kill a man she brought home with a box cutter. Rio catches him, spares him, and they start dating (v1). She supplies bodies to Reina Enmeiji, an artist who makes furniture from human remains (v2); a policewoman, Jun Schneider, investigates; Rio kills Satoru, a 22-year-old acquaintance of Kurosu, whose high-school sister Haruko starts to dig (v1-3). A surgeon, Sakaki, kidnaps people and alters their faces to build a "family" with Rio (v4). Kurosu asks Rio to stop killing; she agrees to stop after one last murder (v4). They plan a wedding; in a ritual Rio designed, Kurosu cuts her throat, believing that severing their bond makes love eternal (v5-6). He serves a prison term, attempts suicide again, and learns Rio survived with amnesia and now prays with children at a church (v6-7). Wanting the killer he loved back, and to be killed by her, he decides he must kill (v7). In the last volume a care facility run by Suburu, a volunteer-group member obsessed with becoming Rio, becomes the stage for the finale (v8). The pair die in a double suicide, learning at the end that they have a child (Gutternaut; MAL reviews) (Mangapedia; Kodansha volume blurbs).

## Life message

**Two lonely people find in each other the only meaning they have, and the story asks, without answering, whether a love that wants to kill and be killed is wrong if both of them choose it.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Mate attraction stripped to its extremes: Kurosu watches and desires; Rio lures men home and kills them. Rivals (Sakaki, Suburu, the detective) compete to possess her. A child appears only at the end, after the couple has chosen death (essay; MAL review). |
| Pragmatic | Almost nothing practical works: Kurosu's attempts to make Rio stop killing (v4) and to live without her (v6) both fail. The police are always a step behind. The story rewards obsession, not planning. |
| Religious | No faith shapes the plot, but a church appears: the amnesiac Rio prays there with children (v7), and Suburu, linked to Rio's past, belongs to a volunteer group that runs a care facility (v7-8; MangaUpdates tags Cult). | Socio-cultural | A hikikomori (shut-in) romance turned horror-comedy: Japanese Wikipedia calls it a love comedy; reviewers call it black comedy with fourth-wall jokes (MAL). Outlook: Bleak — almost everyone who comes close to the couple dies (essay), Kurosu and Rio end in a double suicide, and the only light, a child they learn of at the last moment, comes too late to change their course.
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: the leads' love for each other is the only thing that keeps either alive, and it ends in death; the late discovery of their child is the one hint that life goes on (L is the close second).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a person with nothing to live for do when he finds one thing to live for, and that thing is a killer? Kurosu's life has already ended in his own eyes when the rope breaks; the hole in the wall gives him something to watch and then someone to love. The series asks whether that love saves him or only gives his despair a new shape.

**The answer the story gives.** It does not give a clear one. Kurosu tries once to pull Rio toward an ordinary life: he asks her to stop killing (v4) and dreams of a plain daily life with her (v3 blurb). But the story keeps returning to her creed that a bond is perfected by cutting it (v6 blurb). When Rio, without her memory, becomes gentle and prays with children (v7), Kurosu chooses the killer over the gentle woman. The last volume asks whether two people who both want to die by each other's hand are wrong, or the world that forbids them (v8 blurb). The double suicide answers in action, and the child they learn of at the end is the only counter-voice.

**Characters as arguments.** Kurosu argues that being needed, even by a killer, is better than being nothing. Rio argues that only death makes things beautiful and lasting. Jun, the police officer, stands for law but is drawn into obsession herself (essay). Sakaki argues for possession: a family made by force. Reina makes death into art. Suburu wants to stop being himself and become Rio (Mangapedia). Each rival is a darker or sillier copy of Kurosu's own obsession.

**Symbols and motifs.** The hole in the wall: a way of seeing that becomes a way of living. The box cutter. The wedding that is also an execution (v5). The church where the gentle Rio prays (v7). The unknown child at the end.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese double love suicide (shinjū) of Chikamatsu's plays (from general knowledge; to verify); the killer-couple romance (the essayist compares it with Dexter and Killing Stalking); the yandere romance of manga and games. Christian, Jewish and Muslim readers will hear the opposite of "choose life"; Buddhist readers will see craving and attachment ending in suffering.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism is the story's mood and Rio's creed, explored rather than refuted. Existentialism appears in the final question about choosing one's end. Duty ethics is raised by name (the golden rule) and set aside. Faith appears only as the quiet life Kurosu turns away from.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 are a dark romantic comedy of a couple hiding murders. Volume 4 turns to horror with the surgeon. Volume 5 brings the wedding and the ritual killing; volume 6 the aftermath, prison and a second suicide attempt; volumes 7-8 Rio's amnesia, the church and the care facility, and the double suicide. Reviewers place the decline in quality somewhere between ch. 40 and ch. 75 (MAL).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kurosu says watching Rio gave him a reason to live. What else could have given him one, and who could have helped?
2. Rio believes cutting a bond makes love last forever. Is that love?
3. When Rio loses her memory she becomes kind and prays with children. Why does Kurosu want the old Rio back?
4. The last volume asks: if two people both want to die, is it wrong? How would you answer, and why?
5. Why do you think the author shows them learning about their child at the very end?
