**Premise.** Ichiro Inuyashiki, 58 but looking far older, is a meek salaryman whose wife and teenage children ignore him; the day his family moves into a modest new house, he learns he has stomach cancer and months to live, and no one answers his calls (ch. 1; Wikipedia). Crying in a park at night, he and a high school boy, Hiro Shishigami, are killed in an alien accident and rebuilt as machines with human appearance, full memories and vast powers. Ichiro discovers he still feels human only when he helps people: he saves a homeless man from teenage attackers, rescues fire victims, heals the terminally ill in hospitals, and smashes a yakuza ring that has abducted a young woman (vols. 1-4). Hiro decides he feels alive only when he kills: he murders whole families at random, including children, then his friend Naoyuki's bullies (vols. 1-2). After police identify him, his mother, hounded online and by the media, takes her own life (ch. 36-37 likely); Hiro kills the online posters through their screens, hides with his classmate Shion, who loves him, and for a time heals people instead (vol. 5). When a police raid shoots Shion and her grandmother (whom he revives), he declares war on Japan, kills crowds in Shinjuku, kills through phones and TVs and crashes airliners into Tokyo (vols. 6-7). Ichiro fights him and revives his daughter Mari (vol. 8), reveals himself to his family, who accept him (vol. 9), and, when a meteor threatens the Earth, flies into space with Hiro: both self-destruct to destroy it, Hiro to save Shion and Naoyuki, Ichiro to save everyone (vol. 10; Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**What makes a person human is compassion: a forgotten old man in a machine body finds his humanity by saving lives, while a boy with the same power who kills to feel alive becomes a monster, though even he ends by giving his life for the people he loves.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Two males handed godlike power: one uses it to protect his kin and strangers, the other to dominate and kill. Hiro still guards a tiny circle (his mother, Naoyuki, Shion), the story's picture of kin loyalty without wider empathy. |
| Pragmatic | Ichiro learns his powers by trial and error, with Naoyuki as technician; Hiro's killing via phones and screens is stopped by a public warning (vol. 7). The final calculation is stark: two self-destructs to stop a meteor (vol. 10). |
| Religious | Chapter titles reach for religious language ("Help Me God," "Divine Punishment," "Savior," "God and the Devil," "If This Was Armageddon"); Ichiro is a healer and savior, Hiro a destroyer (Wikipedia chapter list). No faith or church appears. Faith readers can read the ending as self-giving love, and the machine body as a question about the soul. |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of how modern Japan treats the aged and the overlooked (a salaryman ignored by his family), and of online mobs and media pile-ons that drive Hiro's mother to death; Thompson notes the vigilante shaming of criminals on social media. MangaUpdates tags it Social Commentary. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Ichiro is reconciled with his family and saves countless people, but he dies in the act that saves the Earth, after Hiro's killings have taken hundreds of lives (vol. 10); his son stands up to bullies and Mari's manga is published.
Hope source: P — the way through is protecting others: Ichiro finds his humanity in saving people, and even Hiro's last act is to protect those he loves; family reconciliation (F) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every tradition reads the core message the same way: compassion and self-sacrifice make us human, murder is monstrous, and online cruelty kills. ## Creator and publisher context

- Hiroya Oku (born September 16, 1967, Fukuoka) was influenced by Katsuhiro Otomo and Ryōichi Ikegami; Wikipedia says his manga often contain explicit violence, sexual depictions and taboo subjects, and calls him a pioneer of digital backgrounds. Works: Hen, Gantz, Inuyashiki, Gigant, Thunder 3 (as Yuki Ikeda, revealed September 2026) and The Sweet Sixty Princess (2025-); in an August 2026 interview he said he would retire after it (Wikipedia, Hiroya Oku).
- On-record intent: while finishing Gantz, Oku drew on the Astro Boy film for a dead man revived as a super robot, which became the story of two characters seeking in their own ways to regain their humanity; Evening's editor gave him near-total creative freedom; he chose "Inuyashiki" as a surname that does not exist (Wikipedia, Production). Ichiro learns to fly while singing the Astro Boy theme (Wikipedia ep. 3).
- TV Tropes calls the series a counterpoint to Gantz in its stress that even very bad people can be redeemed. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (no interview opened).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Evening, a seinen magazine). Kodansha USA publishes it in English, rated 16+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found; the series itself was reported blocked on two Russian websites in 2021 (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If your body were replaced by a machine, would you still be human, and what would prove it? Two people receive the same miracle. One is an old man everyone ignores; one is a high school boy everyone admires. The series asks what each does with unlimited power.

**The answer the story gives.** Humanity is not in the body but in what we do for others. Ichiro feels human only when he helps; Hiro feels alive only when he kills. Oku said he built the story around two characters seeking, each in his own way, to regain their humanity (Wikipedia). The ending gives both the same choice: two self-destructs to stop a meteor. Ichiro dies for everyone; Hiro dies for the few he loves.

**Characters as arguments.** Ichiro argues for humble, unthanked service. Hiro argues that only his own circle is real; his mother's suicide after online abuse turns him from killer to terrorist, while Shion's love briefly turns him to healing. Naoyuki argues that friendship cannot excuse murder. The yakuza and the online mob show cruelty without superpowers. Ichiro's family, who ignored him, learn to see him (vol. 9).

**Symbols and motifs.** The finger gun and "bang": killing made casual. Ichiro's hands that heal. The dog Hanako, his one loyal companion (Thompson wanted more of her). The question of the copy: his wife confirms he is still himself by asking about their honeymoon (TV Tropes). Astro Boy, the robot who longs to be human.

**Traditions it echoes.** Astro Boy and the superhero origin story, Oku's own Gantz (more cynical), and the old story of the despised servant who saves others. Christian readers may see self-giving love and a savior figure; Buddhist readers, craving and compassion in the same new body.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism is affirmed: people save people. Transhumanism is a neutral tool. Nihilism and might-makes-right are the villain's creed and are defeated. Existential choice is real: the same power yields saint or murderer.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 set up the two leads; vol. 3 is the yakuza arc (the sexual violence); vols. 4-5 bring healing, Hiro's exposure, his mother's death and Shion; vols. 6-8 are war on Japan and the battle over Tokyo; vols. 9-10 bring the family's acceptance and the meteor.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ichiro feels human when he helps people. What makes you feel most yourself?
2. Hiro cared about his mother, Naoyuki and Shion. Why wasn't that enough to stop him from killing?
3. People online attacked Hiro's mother, who had done nothing wrong. What does the story say about words on the internet?
4. Ichiro's family ignored him. Why do people overlook those who quietly support them?
5. Was Ichiro right to cripple the yakuza? Where is the line between protecting people and punishing them?
