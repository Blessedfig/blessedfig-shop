**Premise.** Haru Nishimiya and Megumi "Hii-chan" Hinoto, college students who have been a couple since before college, are worn down by job hunting. After failing a second-round interview, Haru throws away her applications; Hii-chan quits her internship, and they ride Hii-chan's scooter to a remote island where her aunt has a summer house (Okazu, vol. 1). Their bags and scooter vanish with a local, Naruko, who turns out to be a dive instructor; the aunt lets them stay once Hii-chan explains they are a couple (Okazu, 2022). Haru proposes, they become engaged and fill in a marriage-style contract, though Japan does not recognize same-sex marriage (Okazu, vols. 1-2). In vol. 2 they work part-time at the dive shop and drift through the summer, avoiding the question of what comes next, until the season nears its end and Hii-chan quietly calls her college about returning. In vol. 3 both try for the corporate jobs they fled, fail, stop talking, tear up the marriage contract and go back to Tokyo together; the relationship survives, but the escape does not (Okazu, vol. 3).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens if you simply refuse the life everyone expects of you? Haru and Hii-chan throw their resumes into the sea and take a scooter to an island. The series asks whether love and freedom are enough.

**The answer the story gives.** Not by themselves. The island has rent, seasons and jobs that need degrees; the couple avoid talking about the future until the money runs out, then fall apart in silence and go back (vol. 3). Their love survives; the plan does not. Okazu reads the ending as conformity winning.

**Characters as arguments.** Haru argues for escape and avoids hard questions. Hii-chan argues for loyalty and goes along with everything, which Okazu calls a dangerous pairing. Naruko and the aunt show that island life is real work, and that permission and help come from others.

**Symbols and motifs.** The suit (the job-hunter's uniform) thrown away. The sea they cross and, in the last blurb, stand before to choose. The scooter that carries them and is lost. The marriage contract, signed and torn up.

**Traditions it echoes.** The runaway-lovers story and the island idyll, here turned realistic; Japanese stories about escaping the salaryman path.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is tested and found wanting; existential freedom is shown as frightening; the critique of conformity is real but ends in surrender; the same-sex couple is treated with warmth throughout.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: flight, arrival and engagement. Vol. 2: a drifting summer of part-time work. Vol. 3: job hunting again, conflict and the return to Tokyo.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do Haru and Hii-chan throw away their resumes? Have you ever felt that pressure?
2. What could they have planned before leaving?
3. Why do they stop talking to each other in the last volume?
4. Is going back to Tokyo a failure or growing up?
5. What does a commitment mean if no one else recognizes it?
