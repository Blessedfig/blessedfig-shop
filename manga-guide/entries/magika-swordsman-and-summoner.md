**Premise.** Fifteen years after humanity learned to summon beings from a parallel world, each nation draws power from a mythology (Norse for Germany, Greek for Italy, Japanese and the 72 demons of Solomon for Japan). Kazuki Hayashizaki, 15, an orphan adopted into a famous sword-school family, plans to enter the swordsmanship division of the National Knight Academy, but he becomes the first boy ever marked as a summoner and is forced into the all-girl magic division, over the objection of his adoptive sister Kanae, the school's top swordswoman. His Diva, Lemegeton (Leme), who appears as a small girl, names him the future "King" of the 72 Pillars: his power comes from bonds, so he can use a girl's summoning magic when her affection for him is high enough, and a kiss strengthens the link. He must become a "Harem King" (fan wiki; The Fandom Post). Arcs include a duel with his childhood friend Mio (ch. 6-7), the death and revival of Mio (ch. 13-15), the German refugee Charlotte hunted as a heretic (ch. 19-25), a conspiracy in the school involving Nyarlathotep (ch. 26-38), a student-council election tournament with foreign plots (ch. 41-53), and, near the end, the loss of his mother and a duel with the king of Yamato at Sekigahara (vol. 19), before the final battle in Atlantis (vol. 20) (fan wiki; Seven Seas; Kadokawa).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Where does strength come from: years of training with the sword, or the people who stand with you? Kazuki has trained all his life in the Hayashizaki style, then is told his true power depends on bonds.

**The answer the story gives.** Both, but bonds win. Kazuki keeps his sword skill and his gratitude to his adoptive father, yet every major victory comes through a companion's magic unlocked by trust (and a kiss). The story makes this literal by turning affection into a number.

**Characters as arguments.** Kazuki argues for loyalty and self-sacrifice. Kanae argues for the sword path and family pride. Mio, the proud childhood friend, shows rivalry turned to devotion. Charlotte, a refugee branded a heretic, makes the case for protecting the persecuted. The headmaster's conspiracy and Nyarlathotep stand for control through manipulation.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Enigma mark; the Lemegeton (a real grimoire) as a small girl; the Ring of Solomon; Japan's three regalia; the Witch's House where the girls and Kazuki live.

**Traditions it echoes.** Battle-academy harems such as Infinite Stratos and Zero's Familiar (named by Seven Seas' copy); the Solomonic magic tradition (the Lesser Key of Solomon), here turned into a game system (from general knowledge; to verify).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian loyalty and patriotism are affirmed; theocratic authority is critiqued; occult and mythic powers are used as neutral tools; hedonism is affirmed through the harem and the fan service, which the story never questions.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-15): enrollment, the duel with Mio, her death and revival. Vols. 4-5 (ch. 16-26): Charlotte and Germany. Vols. 6-8 (ch. 27-43): rebellion, the secret facility, the election tournament begins. Vols. 9-17: tournament and foreign plots (chapter detail not found past ch. 53). Vols. 18-19: Kazuki's mother and the duel with Yamato's king. Vol. 20: the final battle in Atlantis (Seven Seas; Kadokawa).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kazuki's power grows when girls like him more. What does that teach about relationships?
2. Kazuki keeps honoring the family that adopted him. Why does that matter to him?
3. Charlotte is hunted as a heretic. How does the story treat religion and power?
4. The fan service is never commented on in the story. How should a reader think about that?
5. Is strength that depends on other people weaker or stronger?
