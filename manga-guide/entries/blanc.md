**Premise.** Three years after high school, Rihito Sajō is finishing a pharmacy degree at Kyoto University and Hikaru Kusakabe is still in their hometown chasing a music career; they promised to marry at twenty. Unsure of his future, and shaken when a classmate's engagement at a drinking party makes him feel the legal gap between his promise and a heterosexual marriage, Sajō asks for a break; Kusakabe returns the ring and throws himself into music. When Sajō's mother's cancer returns while his father works abroad, Kusakabe becomes her caretaker as Sajō finishes his studies; she comes to accept and thank him, then dies of pneumonia. The two reconcile at her funeral. Sajō's father rejects his son's relationship and blames Kusakabe; Sajō stands up to him. The couple choose a wedding ceremony among friends, without legal recognition, where their old teacher Hara officiates and friends from the series bring veils and a cake (Wikipedia; Anime Feminist; fan wiki).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a marriage? Sajō and Kusakabe promised at graduation to marry at twenty, but the law will not recognize them, Sajō's future is unclear and his father objects. blanc asks whether a promise between two people can hold without the law, a career plan or a parent's blessing.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if it is kept in the hard places. Kusakabe keeps faith not by words but by caring for Sajō's dying mother; she thanks him and accepts him. At her funeral the couple find each other again, and they marry among friends as "just two people who love each other" (Anime Feminist's summary).

**Characters as arguments.** Sajō argues for caution and for taking the world's rules seriously, and learns that fear was making his choices. Kusakabe argues for steady, unshowy love. The mother is the argument that a parent can change; the father, that some will not, at least not yet (his own spin-off follows). Hara is the uneasy case: a man who once crossed a line with a student, now a friend who blesses the marriage.

**Symbols and motifs.** White (blanc): a blank page after the break, the wedding veils, and the color of mourning in Japan. Rings returned and given again (the Rings booklet). The guitar and the pharmacy degree as two separate lives that still fit together.

**Traditions it echoes.** The long romance that ends in marriage, here retold for a same-sex couple; the family drama of a dying parent; and the Japanese debate over marriage equality, which the book treats through private life rather than politics.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: the couple's choice is right and the law and father are obstacles. Communitarianism is affirmed in the friends who make the wedding. The Confucian claim of a father's authority over his son's marriage is set aside, while care for the mother is honored.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1: the break, Kusakabe's music, the mother's illness and Kusakabe's caregiving (her talk with him, ch. 6, fan wiki). Volume 2: her death and funeral, the reconciliation and the sex scene, the clash with the father and the wedding (Wikipedia; Anime Feminist; MyAnimeList review). The Rings booklet collects short ring stories set between O.B. and blanc (MangaUpdates).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Sajō ask for a break? Was he right to be worried?
2. What did Kusakabe's care for Sajō's mother show that words could not?
3. Sajō's mother changed her mind; his father did not. What makes the difference?
4. What makes a marriage real to you: the law, a faith community, the two people, or family and friends?
5. How does your family's tradition see the wedding at the end?
