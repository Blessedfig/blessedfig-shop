**Executive summary.** A quiet, Gothic fairy tale about a small human girl, Shiva, and the horned, cursed "Outsider" she calls Teacher, who raises her in an abandoned village on the forbidden side of a divided world, where his touch could curse her. The overall content level is 6 out of 10, set by Fear and intensity: a steady mood of dread, a child abandoned in a cursed forest, body-horror transformations, and soldiers who kill anyone suspected of contact with the curse, including burning a village and, later, a quarantined castle town (TV Tropes). Violence is moderate and stylized (BBFC film advice: "moderate violence, brief bloody images"). There is no sexual content and almost no language. The most discussed points are the religious ones: the human "Inside" follows a church-led faith that TV Tropes calls "essentially identical to Christianity," whose "Holy Father" and god of light drive the purges, and both gods are revealed as manipulative "thieves." The ending is open: Shiva is revealed to be Teacher's own soul in the form of a girl, and the last chapter leaves his final choice to the reader (WeekendNotes; Hidden in Pages). Readers praise the art and the tender guardian bond; some find the ending unresolved. No real-world controversy was found.

**Premise.** Long ago, a god of light stripped a god of darkness and banished him to the Outside; his punishment became a curse (TV Tropes, ch. 2). Now the world is split. Humans live in the walled "Inside." The "Outside" is a dark forest whose inhabitants, black, skull-faced Outsiders, curse humans with a touch and turn them into Outsiders too. Shiva, a small human girl, is left in the Outside by the old woman she calls Auntie. An Outsider finds her. She calls him "Teacher" (Sensei). He was once human, cannot remember his past, never sleeps or eats, and never touches her for fear of passing on the curse. He cannot bring himself to tell her she was abandoned. Other Outsiders call her "a soul" and want to bring her to their "Mother." Soldiers of the Inside, acting on the word of their god, hunt for her too (Wikipedia; MyAnimeList; Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life is gentle: you care for someone who is not your own kind, without being able to hold them, and you keep loving them whatever they turn out to be. Fear of the different, enforced by walls, purges and religious authority, destroys more than the curse it tries to stop.**

Outlook: Bittersweet — Teacher learns that the girl he raised is his own soul and must lose either her or himself, but the last chapter closes on his love and forgiveness for her rather than on despair.

**Where traditions disagree.** Most traditions grade the message well because it centers gentle care, mercy and forgiveness and condemns persecution. The dividing line is the sacred: Christian frames that weigh how God is shown (Orthodox, Reformed, Pentecostal) and monotheist frames (Muslim, Orthodox Jewish) note that a "Heavenly Father"-style god is cast as a schemer and the church as persecutor. Separable souls and spirit "Mother" imagery matter most to Adventist and Jehovah's Witness families; fear of contagion matters most to Christian Science families. Dharmic, Pagan, secular and progressive frames find the closest fit. On depiction, concern is low across the board, since the content is mostly mood and stylized violence.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

## Creator and publisher context

- **Nagabe** has shared little personal information publicly (from general knowledge; to verify); available facts are limited to work history and interviews. Nagabe began by posting illustrations on pixiv before being offered a manga job (pixivision, January 2, 2017). No statements on religion or politics were found. Other English-licensed works include the short-story collection "Love on the Other Side" (ANN review found, not opened). Nagabe announced the final manuscript on Twitter in January 2021 (ANN, opened).
- **Stated intent (pixivision, 2017):** "The most important theme behind Totsukuni no Shoujo is gentleness." The story began from the idea of "not touching." The leads came from "the idea of a pure white character and a pure black character"; the light-dark duality was meant to make the setting clear, and Nagabe "didn't overthink" it. Nagabe avoided real-world references because "the theme behind this manga is what's most important." Influences: Tove Jansson and Komako Sakai. Nagabe likes animals for their appearance and because they are "not as versatile as humans."
- **Manga-News interview (Livre Paris, March 2018; published October 10, 2018; French, summarized):** compares the story to Beauty and the Beast, two beings who should never have met; wanted a world like "engravings and illustrations from old tales" (Grimm); names Jansson, Edward Gorey, Norman Rockwell, Mucha and Tasha Tudor. The editor gave Nagabe a CD of the Irish song "Siúil a Rún," whose melody Nagabe found sad but hopeful, and it became the subtitle. Nagabe wanted a darker opening but the editor advised a gentler start, so the story darkens later.
- **Publishers:** Mag Garden (Japan); Seven Seas Entertainment (English). Wit Studio's feature film was its first crowdfunding project, via Kickstarter (Japanese Wikipedia).
- No controversies involving the creator were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.** (No statements on the religious elements of the story were found; afterwords not checked.)

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you love someone you cannot touch? Nagabe says the story began from that idea of "not touching" (pixivision). Behind it sits a second question: what makes the people we fear into monsters, and are they?

**The answer the story gives.** Love can be real without contact, and the monster may be the one who stays kind. Teacher never touches Shiva because he might curse her. He cooks, reads, keeps a home in a dead village and keeps a painful secret: she was abandoned (Wikipedia). The Inside, by contrast, answers fear with walls, purges and fire. By the end, the story's bleakest truth is personal: Shiva is Teacher's own soul, which had taken the form of a little girl (WeekendNotes). He must choose between letting her appear one last time or keeping his memories until he dies. The story does not say which he chooses. It does show that he keeps his promise to love her whatever she turned out to be, and forgives her for the pain she caused him (WeekendNotes).

**Characters as arguments.** Teacher argues that gentleness is strength: the one Outsider who keeps his sense of self (TV Tropes) and the one who refuses to harm anyone, even to save the child he loves (v8 blurb). Shiva argues for trust; her small deception has large consequences (v4 blurb), and her innocence draws both sides. Auntie argues that love sometimes looks like abandonment: she left Shiva Outside so soldiers would not kill her (TV Tropes). The King argues the hard case for the greater good: he burns a cursed town, then, in an "agonizing decision," betrays his kingdom so a child can escape (v10 blurb). The cursed soldiers, who want to be saved (v7 blurb), argue that the hunters can become the hunted.

**Symbols and motifs.** White and black: Shiva in white, Teacher in black (Japanese Wikipedia); Nagabe "tried to avoid grey" (Manga-News). Touch as danger and as longing. The locket with a scratched-out face (TV Tropes) as lost memory. The walled Inside and the forest Outside. The Mother at the bottom of the lake. Trees that the cursed become. A cottage that "crumbles to dust" (v11 blurb).

**Traditions it echoes.** Beauty and the Beast, which Nagabe names (Manga-News). Grimm fairy tales and old engravings. Irish folk song and what SLJ calls "Irish fairy-faith." Gnostic-style or Zoroastrian-style dualism of a light god and a dark god (my own framing). Plague-era and witch-hunt persecution (Beneath the Tangles). Buddhist teaching on impermanence and the loss of self (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Care ethics and virtue are shown as true in Teacher. Authority, religious or royal, is shown as fear turned into violence, though the King gets a tragic redemption. Religion appears mostly as institution and prophecy; the gods themselves are thieves and schemers, so the story offers no good divine figure in the sources opened. Utilitarian calculation (burn the town) is shown at its full cost. Existential questions about the self are left open.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 are a gentle, slice-of-life fable with "low-level dread" (SLJ) and a calm start chosen on the editor's advice (Manga-News). From vol. 3, outbreak, purges and Auntie's story darken it (Comics Beat; v4 blurb). Vols. 5-7 bring the curse onto Shiva and the cursed soldiers' pursuit. Vols. 8-9 are the darkest: Teacher gives up his soul and forgets her. Vols. 10-11 reveal his past as Albert and Shiva's nature and end openly.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Teacher never touches Shiva. How else does he show love? What does that teach about love?
2. Auntie left Shiva in the forest to save her from soldiers. Was that love or abandonment, or both?
3. The Inside burns towns to stop the curse. When does protecting people turn into harming them?
4. The story's church and god act out of fear and self-interest. How is that like or unlike what your family believes about God?
5. At the end Teacher must choose between seeing Shiva one last time and keeping his memories. What would you choose, and why might the author leave it open?
