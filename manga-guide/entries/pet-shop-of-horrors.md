**Premise.** In a smoky alley of Los Angeles's Chinatown, the androgynous "Count D" runs a pet shop that sells, he says, love, dreams and hope. Each rare animal (rabbits, birds, a mermaid-like fish, a lizard called Medusa, a kirin, a nine-tailed fox) appears in human form to its buyer and comes with a three-term contract; when the buyer breaks the contract, disaster follows, and the shop takes no responsibility (Wikipedia; Seven Seas). Most chapters are stand-alone tales of grief, greed, vanity and obsession: parents who see their dead daughter in a rabbit (ch. 3), a man whose new "fish" looks like his drowned wife (ch. 7), a congressman who makes a pact with a kirin (ch. 16) (Wikipedia). Detective Leon Orcot, a brash womanizer, is sure D is a drug dealer or worse and keeps coming back, bribed with sweets into an odd friendship; Leon's young brother Chris, mute after his cousin blamed him for their mother's death, comes to live at the shop and sees the animals as people (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Japanese Wikipedia says the stories focus on people's hidden darkness and include many quite cruel depictions. The final volume (ch. 34-37) reveals that D's family descends from an ancient Chinese people close to animals, massacred after a priestess refused a prince, whose survivors vowed revenge on humanity; D's father, a genetic researcher who hates humans for destroying nature, is killed and reborn as a baby, Leon is badly hurt and later searches the world for D, and Chris, healed and able to speak, grows up to visit a new D twenty years later (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**What you desire can destroy you if you will not respect its limits: people who use animals, nature and one another for their own wants pay the price, while honesty, grief faced truthfully and love for the vulnerable can heal.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when you get exactly what you want? Each customer walks into D's shop with a hole in their life (a dead child, a lost wife, ambition, loneliness) and walks out with a creature that fills it, on conditions. The series asks whether the danger lies in the desire, in the broken promise, or in the people who sell us what we crave.

**The answer the story gives.** Desire is not evil, but desire that will not accept limits destroys. The contracts are small (show her to no one, feed him only vegetables), and the customers break them because they want more. Those who keep faith (a blind girl's guard dog, ch. 4) or face grief honestly come through. Underneath runs a second answer: humanity's appetite is destroying the living world, and the animals' patience is not endless (ch. 8; the final arc).

**Characters as arguments.** D argues for the animals and against human greed, but grows fond of two humans despite himself. Leon argues for reason and the law, and keeps losing the argument while winning D's friendship. Chris argues for innocence: the child who sees things as they are, and who must give that up to speak again. D's father argues that humanity deserves punishment, and the story shows where that leads.

**Symbols and motifs.** The contract of three terms. The animals' human faces, revealed as animal in the last panels (IGN). Sweets, D's weakness and Leon's bribe. The letter D at the start of every title. The child who leaves paradise when he learns to speak (MyAnimeList user's reading of the ending).

**Traditions it echoes.** The fairy-tale bargain and the monkey's paw; Chinese myth of the kirin and the fox spirit; the Faustian merchant; and 1990s environmental fables. Some readers see Eden in the ending; Buddhist readers will see craving and its fruits, and a family that is reborn.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Environmentalism is affirmed, though its revenge form is condemned. Pagan/occult wonder is the setting, never judged. Classical virtue ethics frames each tale as a lesson in temperance. Hedonism and unbounded desire are critiqued. Naturalism, through Leon, is the view the story keeps disproving.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-12) are mostly stand-alone fables. Volumes 4-7 (ch. 13-26) deepen Leon and D's bond, bring Chris to the shop and add Leon's grief (ch. 23). Volumes 8-10 (ch. 27-37) bring D's family, D's father and the final confrontation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do D's customers break their contracts, even when they know the rules?
2. The grieving parents in "Daughter" loved their child. Were they punished fairly?
3. D cares more for animals than people. Is he right about humans, wrong, or both?
4. Leon keeps refusing to believe in the supernatural. When is skepticism wise, and when is it stubborn?
5. Chris loses the ability to see the animals as people when he learns to speak again. What did he gain, and what did he lose?
