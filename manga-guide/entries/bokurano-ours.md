**Premise.** At a seaside summer camp, fifteen children, all middle schoolers except fourth-grader Kana, find a cave where a man calling himself Kokopelli invites them to test a "game": piloting a giant robot to defend Earth from fifteen invaders. Fourteen sign a contract. That night a black robot appears; Kokopelli wins the first battle, a small sarcastic creature named Koyemshi becomes their guide, and the children learn the rules one at a time: each battle has one pilot chosen in turn; the robot, which they name Zearth, runs on the pilot's life, so every pilot dies after the battle; the enemies are people of parallel Earths, and the losing Earth is erased (Wikipedia; VIZ vol. 11 rules). Each arc follows one child: their family, secrets and choice of how to spend their last days, while the Japanese government and military try to control the situation and civilians die in the fighting. The final pilot, Jun Ushiro, must fight on an enemy world where the enemy pilot hides among the population, and ends the battle by killing everyone on that Earth before he dies (ch. 60-64). The robot then passes to another Earth, with Koyemshi's successor as the new Kokopelli (ch. 65; TV Tropes; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Everyone dies and goodness does not spare anyone; what matters is what you do with the time you have, and whether you will give your life for people you will never meet.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Each child's last days are spent on kin: Maki fights so her unborn brother can be born (ch. 25-28), Daiichi for his younger siblings (ch. 8-9), Kana to reunite her brother with his birth mother (ch. 48-52). Survival of one's own group requires the extinction of another's. |
| Pragmatic | The rules are fixed and the children must work within them: Daiichi delays fighting until civilians evacuate, a practice all later pilots adopt (fan wiki); Jun's final choice is forced by the 48-hour rule (ch. 60-64). |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot; the names Kokopelli and Koyemshi come from Hopi tradition (from general knowledge; to verify). Mako wishes to be reborn as someone like her mother (ch. 10-12). Beneath the Tangles reads the children's facing of death beside the Apostles' and Christ's self-sacrifice (anime). |
| Socio-cultural | A deconstruction of the giant-robot genre and of child soldiers: Comic Book Bin reads it as anti-war and a critique of militarized childhood (vol. 11). Adult institutions are often corrupt: a teacher who abuses a student is promoted to a ministry post (TV Tropes), a construction firm hides shoddy work (Kanji, ch. 43-47), and the government manipulates the media. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — every child dies and whole parallel worlds are destroyed, but Earth survives, several children find peace or purpose before the end, and readers argue it is a kind of happy ending (TV Tropes: Esoteric Happy Ending).
Hope source: P — the hope is protecting others: the children give their lives so families and strangers can live; family love (F) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame honors the children's self-sacrifice and the condemnation of abusers and corrupt adults. ## Creator and publisher context

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you knew you would die in a few days, and your death could save everyone, what would you do with the time? TV Tropes names the central theme directly: what do you do with the little time left in your life?

**The answer the story gives.** There is no escape and no reward; the only answer is how each child chooses. Some choose love (Maki, Kana), some duty (Daiichi, Mako), some revenge (Chizuru), some collapse (Kako, Kodama). Kitoh's refusal of "magical solutions" means the answer has to be found in the choice itself (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Kodama argues that some lives matter more; the story breaks him. Daiichi argues that strangers' lives matter as much as his. Mako argues for duty and honesty, and hopes to be reborn as her mother. Chizuru shows how abuse can turn into revenge that harms innocents. Kana argues for enduring love. Jun is forced to carry utilitarian logic to its end and is broken by it (ch. 60-64).

**Symbols and motifs.** Each pilot's chair, taken from their life (Maki's is a baby cot, fan wiki); the contract; Koyemshi's mocking guidance; the 48-hour clock; parallel Earths with slightly different aircraft (Maki's discovery, fan wiki).

**Traditions it echoes.** Neon Genesis Evangelion and the super-robot genre it inverts; Ikigami and other death-countdown stories (Michelle Smith compares the two, Manga Bookshelf tag page); the death-game genre; Hopi figures as names.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is the frame; utilitarian reasoning is shown as necessary and horrifying; nihilism is the threat the children resist; pacifist hopes are raised and denied by the rules. Christian readers find an echo of sacrificial love but no resurrection; secular readers find an honest look at mortality.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-12): the game revealed, Waku, Kodama, Daiichi, Mako. Vols. 3-5 (ch. 13-29): Kako, Chizuru, Moji, Maki; parallel worlds revealed. Vols. 6-8 (ch. 30-47): Kirie, Komo, Aiko, Kanji; politics grows. Vols. 9-11 (ch. 48-65): the Ushiro family, Tanaka, Machi, Jun's final battle and the cycle passed on.

**Discussion questions.**
1. If you knew you had only a few days, what would you do with them?
2. Daiichi refuses to fight until people are evacuated. Why does that matter if he is going to die anyway?
3. Is it right to destroy another world to save your own? Is there any other choice in the story?
4. Chizuru's revenge kills people who did nothing to her. How do the other children respond?
5. Some adults in the story abuse their power and are never punished. Why might the author leave that unresolved?
