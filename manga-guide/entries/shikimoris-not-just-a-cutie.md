**Premise.** Yuu Izumi, a second-year high school student, has had terrible luck all his life: falling objects, runaway balls, cars and animals find him, and his mother is accident-prone too (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). His girlfriend, classmate Miyako Shikimori, is sweet, pretty and a little clumsy at cooking and singing, but when Izumi is in danger she turns "cool": her eyes sharpen, she becomes intimidating and athletic, and she saves him (Wikipedia; But Why Tho). The couple is already together on page one. The early chapters are short gags; later chapters grow longer and more character-driven (TV Tropes), adding their friends Inuzuka, Nekozaki and Hachimitsu, a volleyball star (Ai Kamiya) who also likes Izumi and whom Shikimori helps to "reconcile her feelings" (Wikipedia), a class trip to Kyoto (Wikipedia; Epicstream), and, in the last volumes, studying for university entrance exams (Kodansha vols. 18 and 19). Izumi kisses Shikimori, on the cheek and off-panel, on the bullet train home from the school trip in chapter 100 (Epicstream; TV Tropes). The story ends with an epilogue after graduation (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life is a partnership in which each person protects, supports and openly accepts the other as they are: love does not erase weakness or bad luck, but shares it, and frees both people from narrow ideas of what a boy or a girl should be.**

| Evolutionary biology | Pair-bonding as mutual protection, with the usual roles swapped: the girl is the physical protector, the boy the nurturer who cooks and cleans (TV Tropes; Wikipedia). ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does each person owe the one they love, and who gets to be strong? Izumi's life is a string of accidents he cannot prevent. Shikimori, sweet in every other moment, becomes formidable when he is in danger. The series asks whether a relationship can rest on one person needing protection, and whether a girl who is "cool" can also be "cute," and a boy who is "cute" can also be worth admiring.

**The answer the story gives.** Strength is shared, not owned. Izumi never stops being unlucky, and the story never treats that as shameful. He gives back what he can: cooking, care, praise, honesty, good grades (TV Tropes). Shikimori protects him, but she also wants to be seen as cute, and the story honors that wish. Reviewers note that she is "pretty feminine overall" (Moore, ANN) and that neither lead is pushed into the other gender's coding (Silverman, ANN). The answer is a partnership in which, as Eisenbeis puts it, the couple's communication makes "such a healthy relationship."

**Characters as arguments.** Shikimori argues that strength and softness can live in one person. Izumi argues that gentleness and needing help are compatible with dignity; Rebecca Silverman calls him "the damsel in distress to Shikimori's prince charming." Kamiya, who also likes Izumi, argues for competition, and the story answers with Shikimori helping her come to terms with her feelings (Wikipedia). Inuzuka argues that a male best friend can be kind and loyal (THEM). Izumi's parents argue that adults can be warm and present (Silverman, ANN).

**Symbols and motifs.** Shikimori's changing eyes, drawn like a villainess's (Eisenbeis, ANN; But Why Tho), mark the shift from cute to cool. Izumi's bad luck stands for the misfortunes no one can fix, only share. Cooking stands for his way of giving. The chapter 100 cheek kiss, kept off the page (Epicstream), stands for a private intimacy the story chooses not to show.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese romcom of a dominant or teasing girl (*Nagatoro*, *Takagi-san*), and the "tomboy who wants to be feminine" story of *Tomo-chan Is a Girl!* (ANN ep. 11). More broadly it echoes the old ideal of love as mutual help in hardship, and modern arguments that strength and care belong to both sexes.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Feminist and progressive readers find the role reversal affirmed (Anime Feminist). Virtue and community lenses are quietly rewarded. Traditional readers find a faithful, chaste-seeming courtship and caring families, with gender roles as the point of difference. Faith traditions find little to object to beyond mild flirtation, teen dating and, once confirmed, the epilogue's view of the couple's future.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-16) is short gags about accidents and rescues. Vols. 2-9 widen the cast with friends, families, festivals and sports; Kamiya's crush and its resolution fall in this stretch (exact chapters to verify; adapted in the anime's later episodes). Vols. 10-11 cover the Kyoto class trip, with the shy classmate Saruogi joining the group (Wikipedia) and the cheek kiss in ch. 100. Vols. 16-19 turn to third year and entrance exams, with Shikimori's "secret ambition for the future" revealed (vol. 18 blurb) and a winter beach trip on New Year's Day (vol. 19 blurb). Vol. 20 (ch. 175-178 and three bonus stories) ends the series with a post-graduation epilogue (TV Tropes; to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shikimori protects Izumi, and Izumi cooks for Shikimori. Do roles in a relationship have to follow gender? What does your family believe?
2. Izumi's bad luck never goes away. How do people live well with problems that cannot be fixed?
3. Shikimori wants to be "cute" as well as "cool." Why might she want both?
4. How does Shikimori treat Kamiya, who likes Izumi too? What would you have done?
5. What makes Izumi and Shikimori's relationship healthy, and what would you want in your own future relationships?
