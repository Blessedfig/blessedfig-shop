**Premise.** In 2075, Earth orbit is cluttered with dangerous junk, and the Technora Corporation's Debris Section clears it in the ship DS-12 "Toy Box": hot-headed Hachirota "Hachimaki" Hoshino, who dreams of owning his own ship; quiet Yuri Mihairokov, whose wife died when a stray bolt pierced a spaceplane window; and Fee Carmichael, the American captain, a mother and a desperate smoker (Wikipedia; Gaffney). Hachimaki survives several near-death accidents, is diagnosed with Deep Space Disorder, and fights his way onto the crew of the Von Braun, the first crewed ship to Jupiter, built by the cold visionary Werner Locksmith (v1-2). Along the way he learns that a friend, Hakim, is a terrorist, and only the new recruit Ai Tanabe, who believes love is the answer to everything, stops him from killing Hakim (Wikipedia). Hachimaki's ambition hollows him out until a breakdown and a lonely vision on the Moon turn him back toward Tanabe, whom he marries before leaving for seven years (v3; Gaffney, Wrong Every Time). Volume 4 follows those left behind: Tanabe's new co-worker "the Baron," Locksmith at the grave of an engineer his project killed, Fee's failed campaign against orbital mines as the US and an unnamed republic slide into a space war, and memories of her uncle Roy, a Black recluse driven out by racist neighbors. It ends with Hachimaki's speech from Jupiter: he is content with what he already has and no longer needs a ship of his own (Wikipedia; Gaffney; Wrong Every Time).

## Life message

**Reaching for the stars is worth it only if you do not lose the people beside you: meaning is found in love, honest work and ordinary happiness, not in conquering the universe alone.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every tradition welcomes the story's message: love and humble work over solitary glory, mercy over revenge, and peace over war. The grades differ mainly in how much a tradition wants faith named: Christian, Jewish and Muslim frames read a deeply humane story that finds meaning in love without God (A), while secular, Buddhist, Lutheran, mainline and Agnostic frames find it an unusually close fit (S). Depiction concerns are small: Fee's smoking matters most to Latter-day Saint, Adventist, Sikh and holiness families, and one scene of adult nudity matters to modesty-minded families.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

No one wins outright. Those who keep love and humility (Tanabe, Yuri, a changed Hachimaki) end at peace; those who put ambition, ideology or national glory above people (Locksmith, the terrorists, the war party) get what they want at a heavy human cost, and the story does not admire them for it.

## Creator and publisher context

- Makoto Yukimura, born May 8, 1976, in Yokohama; worked as an assistant to Shin Morimura before debuting with Planetes; later created Vinland Saga, which won the 2009 Japan Media Arts Festival Grand Prize (Wikipedia).
- On record (Wikipedia): the idea came from a science book on orbital debris he found in a library; his main influence was Arthur C. Clarke, and he was moved by the writer Koshu Tani; his only goal was to show characters' daily lives in a future setting, and he ended the story once it felt complete. JAXA consulted on the anime.
- Yukimura has said he hates violence, and he drew on the Cold War and the September 11 attacks for Vinland Saga's turn to peace (Wikipedia); Wrong Every Time sees the same move in Planetes vol. 4. No statements on religion were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (interviews on Planetes itself not opened).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Morning, a seinen magazine). English: Tokyopop (2003-2005), then Dark Horse (2015-2016 omnibuses, rated 14+, and deluxe editions). No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a human life matter in a universe that does not notice us? Hachimaki wants to be remembered: his own ship, the first crew to Jupiter, a name in history. The series sets that wish against the vastness of space and the plain work of picking up other people's trash.

**The answer the story gives.** Meaning is close at hand: love, family, friends and work done well. Hachimaki's ambition nearly destroys him (v2-3); a voice in his vision tells him he is human, he is Hachimaki, and he loves Tanabe (ch. 12, likely; Wrong Every Time). At Jupiter he says he is satisfied with what he already has (ch. 26). Exploration is still worth doing, Gaffney notes, as long as it does not lose sight of humanity.

**Characters as arguments.** Tanabe argues for love, sometimes too loudly (The Manga Curmudgeon). Locksmith argues for progress at any cost and is left alone at a grave (ch. 18). Yuri argues for faithful grief and then letting go (v1-2). Fee argues for rebellion and loses, but keeps her dignity (v4). Hakim argues, with terror, for the nations left behind. Colonel Sanders argues that armies need cautious men (v4).

**Symbols and motifs.** Debris: the mess every ambition leaves behind. The Toy Box: a humble ship that is also a home. The wounded cat dragging itself toward the moon (v3, Wrong Every Time). Dogs in vol. 4: strays, pets and a "trained dog" Fee refuses to be. Shiritori, the word game used for the proposal.

**Traditions it echoes.** Arthur C. Clarke's grounded space fiction (Yukimura's own influence); Miyazawa Kenji's Guskou Budori, the scientist who serves the people; Ecclesiastes and the Buddhist teaching on craving, for readers who see them in Hachimaki's turn to contentment. Yukimura would make the same turn from violence to peace the spine of Vinland Saga.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is the frame and communitarian love the answer. Environmentalism is the premise. Utilitarian progress, embodied in Locksmith, is respected for its results and rejected for its coldness. Nationalism is the villain of vol. 4. Faith is absent rather than attacked; the mystery of the vision is left open.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-5) is episodic: the crew, an old astronaut's death, Fee's smoke, Yuri's grief. Vol. 2 (ch. 6-11) is Hachimaki's ambition, Tanabe's arrival and the terrorists. Vol. 3 (ch. 12-16) is his breakdown, recovery and marriage. Vol. 4 (ch. 17-26) shifts to Tanabe, Locksmith and Fee, war and racism, and ends at Jupiter.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hachimaki wanted to be remembered. What did he find instead, and was it a loss?
2. Tanabe says love is the answer to everything. Where is she right, and where is it harder than that?
3. Locksmith built the ship that reached Jupiter. Does that make him a hero?
4. Why does Tanabe stop Hachimaki from hurting Hakim?
5. Debris collection is looked down on. What jobs in our town are like that, and who would we miss most?
