**Premise.** On the first day of their third and final year of high school, Taichi Ichinose finds himself in the same class as Toma Mita, a popular baseball player and childhood friend he has drifted away from, and Futaba Kuze, a shy girl he dislikes. Futaba has a crush on Toma and asks Taichi to help her. Her protective friend Masumi Itachi tells Taichi that Toma is in love with Taichi, and that she herself loves Futaba (ch. 5, TV Tropes). The friends spend the year on a sports festival, college plans and their tangled feelings; Mami Yagihara joins the group. Taichi and Futaba start dating (ch. 27). Toma is outed by a classmate and confesses to Taichi. The final chapter jumps years ahead: Taichi and Futaba broke up two years after high school, and Taichi is shown, by implication, as Toma's partner (ANN vol. 8; Mary Sue; Wikipedia says "living together as a married couple").

## Life message

**A good life is honest about who you are and whom you love, even when others' expectations and prejudice push you to hide; the choices you make at eighteen do not have to be final.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it cost to hide who you are, and what does it cost to stop hiding? Blue Flag asks this of five students in their last year of high school. Each is waiting for a future that others have planned: college, a normal marriage, a place in the class order. Toma, the popular baseball player, has decided that loving a boy makes him "abnormal" (v3). Masumi tries to cure herself by dating boys (v4). Taichi has spent years at the bottom of the class and does not think he deserves much at all.

**The answer the story gives.** Hiding costs more. The story does not pretend that coming out is safe: Toma does not choose to be known; a classmate outs him (ch. 41), rumors spread (ch. 42), he fights, is suspended and stops attending school (v7-8). Yet the characters who face the truth end up in lives they chose. The epilogue, years later, shows Futaba at her wedding, Masumi married to a man who knows her old feelings, and Taichi with a ring, implied to be Toma's partner. ANN's reviewer reads the final message as "high school is not where most people settle who they are."

**Characters as arguments.** Toma argues that love can be both a burden and a gift: he saves Taichi from a car at the cost of his own sports future (ch. 12) and still apologizes when he confesses ("I love you. I'm sorry," TV Tropes). Masumi argues, through her pain, that conformity cannot change the heart. Mami argues that girls and boys can be friends and that the gossip says more about the gossips (ch. 32-33). Futaba, whom ANN calls "more catalyst than individual," argues that wanting to change yourself is not the same as knowing yourself. Taichi, in v8, realizes he dated Futaba partly hoping that helping her change would change him. The homophobic classmate in v7 is given a reason, past trauma, rather than being a cartoon.

**Symbols and motifs.** The title's flag suggests a signal raised for others to see, or a banner of youth (blue as in "seishun"; my own framing). Kaito's art uses subjective point of view, and ANN praises its "expressive faces and body language." Conversations are the main action. The car accident marks the point where Toma's planned future ends and the real question begins. Taichi's wrecked bedroom (v8) shows feelings he cannot name.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese school-life drama and shojo-style inner monologue; the Western coming-out story; and the ensemble "love quadrangle" (Viz). The Mary Sue compares its mood to *Kids on the Slope*. The Gita's call to live one's own dharma rather than another's, and Socrates' "know thyself," are echoes a reader might draw (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and a progressive critique of prejudice are shown as true. Traditional expectations about gender and marriage are mostly voiced by side characters and treated as pressure rather than wisdom, though the homophobic classmate is treated with some understanding. Religion is absent rather than argued against. Feminist readers find a clear critique of double standards in Mami's thread, and some find Futaba underwritten.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (likely ch. 1-12) set up the quadrangle and end with the accident. Vols. 3-5 turn inward: Toma's fear, Masumi's pretend dating, Futaba's feelings, Taichi and Futaba's dating (ch. 27). Vol. 6 brings Mami's thread and Masumi's coming out to Akiko (ch. 39) and ends with rumors. Vol. 7 is the outing and the long conversations ANN calls heavy-handed. Vol. 8 resolves the confession and jumps years ahead.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Toma calls himself "abnormal" in vol. 3. Where do ideas of "normal" come from, and who should get to decide?
2. Kensuke tells others about Toma's feelings (ch. 41). Why is sharing someone's secret harmful even if it is true?
3. Masumi dates boys to try to change herself. What does the story say about pretending to be someone else?
4. Taichi realizes he started dating Futaba partly to change himself. What is a good reason to start a relationship?
5. The story ends years later with different couples than in school. What does that say about the choices people make at eighteen?
