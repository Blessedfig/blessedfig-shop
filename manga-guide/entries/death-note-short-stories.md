**Premise.** A collection of every short Death Note story by its creators. In "C-Kira," set three years after Light Yagami's defeat, Near now works as L when a new Kira starts killing elderly people who say they want to die, claiming to relieve their pain. Near refuses to chase a killer he finds purposeless, tells the public on TV that this Kira is only a murderer, and the user writes his own name in the notebook (fan wiki). In "a-Kira," set in 2017-2019, Ryuk gives the notebook to Minoru Tanaka, a middle-schooler with a very high IQ and poor grades. Minoru realizes modern surveillance would catch any killer, has Ryuk take the notebook away for two years, then announces on hijacked live TV that the notebook is for sale. The US and China bid; the US wins with a sum that sets every Japanese citizen under 60 up for life. The King of Death then makes a new rule that anyone who buys or sells a Death Note dies, and Ryuk writes Minoru's name (Wikipedia; fan wiki). The book also has comic four-panel strips, two short comic pieces about L's childhood and daily life with Watari, and the 2003 pilot, in which a bullied 13-year-old, Taro Kagami, mistakes the notebook for a diary and accidentally kills his bullies (fan wiki).

## Life message

**Power over life and death ruins whoever holds it, whether they call its use mercy, justice or business; the only safe choice is to refuse it, and even the cleverest person cannot outwit death.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Status and survival drive every user: C-Kira seeks to matter, Minoru to secure money and safety, governments to hold the ultimate weapon. The pilot turns on a bullied boy's fear and anger. Death comes to those who reach for the notebook. |
| Pragmatic | Minoru is a pure problem-solver: he studies Kira in ethics and history class, tests the rules (Ryuk can go 14 km from the owner) and designs a sale that avoids tracing (fan wiki). The story shows that clever planning cannot beat rules made by a higher power. |
| Religious | Shinigami, a King of Death and apples are fantasy furniture, not a faith. Kira is called a god by his supporters; after a-Kira the public praise Kira as a god again (fan wiki, Minoru Tanaka). The stories treat self-made gods as frauds or murderers. Readers of many faiths will see the euthanasia theme in C-Kira, where the elderly are killed at their own request, and the suicide that follows. |
| Socio-cultural | Written for a Japan with an aging population and heavy digital surveillance. The Mary Sue reads C-Kira's talk of the elderly as a burden against COVID-era rhetoric; a-Kira satirizes governments and a US president whose likeness to Donald Trump is noted by CBR (via Wikipedia) and by every outlet opened. But Why Tho praises its critique of politics, greed and power. |

Outlook: Bleak — both main stories end with the notebook's user dead (C-Kira by his own hand, Minoru by Ryuk's), Near admits defeat in a-Kira, and Kira is worshipped again; the L pieces and gag strips are light, and the pilot ends with confession and the dead restored.
Hope source: N — the stories find little hope in human hands: power over death destroys its holders and the death god stays bored and amused; forgiveness and confession in the pilot (R) are the lighter counterweight.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the stories' refusal to call killing mercy, justice or business. The divide is over the death gods and over hope. Pentecostal, Adventist and Jehovah's Witness readings weigh shinigami and a King of Death that rules human fate as the message (D), and several Christian, Jewish and Muslim readings note that suicide appears with no help or hope. Secular, Agnostic and Buddhist readings take the gods as fantasy and find a sharp moral fable (A or B). Readers on euthanasia may split: religious frames read Near's verdict of murder as clear affirmation of life; secular readers note that C-Kira is an unaccountable killer, not a lawful assisted-dying system.

## Creator and publisher context

- Tsugumi Ohba (story; a pen name, identity not public) wrote Death Note (Weekly Shōnen Jump, 2003-2006; Eagle Award 2008) and, with Obata, Bakuman (2008-2012) and Platinum End (Jump SQ, 2015-2021). Ohba cites Shotaro Ishinomori, Fujiko Fujio and Fujio Akatsuka as influences and is fond of Akira Kurosawa and Charlie Chaplin films (Wikipedia, Tsugumi Ohba).
- Takeshi Obata (art) drew Hikaru no Go and All You Need Is Kill ((from general knowledge; to verify)). Parts of the a-Kira manuscript were first shown at his 30th-anniversary exhibition, "Never Complete," in Tokyo, July-August 2019 (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: Ohba said of the pilot that he "didn't really care" for the eraser device that revives the dead (Wikipedia). No interview about C-Kira or a-Kira was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Jump Comics); VIZ Media, rated T+. C-Kira was timed to promote the film L: Change the WorLd (fan wiki).
- Controversy: the 2020 one-shot drew wide news coverage because its US president resembles Donald Trump (Wikipedia, citing CBR; many news headlines in search). The main series has been banned in some Chinese schools and cities (Wikipedia). No controversies involving the creators personally were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to use the Death Note, or to refuse it? Each story hands the notebook to a different kind of person: a lonely fan who wants to be Kira, a lazy genius who wants money and safety, a bullied 13-year-old who wanted a diary. The book asks which of them can hold power over death without being destroyed.

**The answer the story gives.** None of them. C-Kira collapses the moment L calls him a murderer. Minoru never kills, but by turning death into a product he invites a higher rule that kills him. Only the pilot, where the boys confess, undo the killings and give the notebook up, ends well. The Daily Bruin sums up the moral as "one should not fear the Death Note itself, but rather those who wield it."

**Characters as arguments.** C-Kira argues that killing the suffering is mercy; Near answers that he is simply a murderer and that he will not chase a killer with no purpose (fan wiki). Minoru argues that a clever person can profit from power without blood on his hands; the King of Death answers by banning sale and purchase. The US president argues that might will win the weapon, and must give it back to stay alive. Ryuk argues for nothing: he wants apples and entertainment.

**Symbols and motifs.** The notebook and its rules; apples, Ryuk's price; a live TV broadcast as the public square (C-Kira's debate, Minoru's hijack); the eraser of the pilot, which can undo death and so allows confession and forgiveness; Ryuk's pen writing a second name beside Light's.

**Traditions it echoes.** The bargain with a demon or death figure (Faust; the monkey's paw), the ring of power that ruins its holder, and debates over euthanasia and the weapons trade. Religious readers will hear echoes of "vengeance is mine" and of the rule that life belongs to God; Buddhist readers, of the first precept.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarian calculation is the stories' main target: every "greater good" (relieving pain, enriching a nation) ends in death. Consumerism is satirized through the auction. Secular reason (Near) is respected but not all-powerful, since a fantasy rule settles a-Kira. The public's return to worshipping Kira is shown as folly.

**Shifts across the series.** The book runs from serious to light: C-Kira (2008) and a-Kira (2020) are the moral core; the four-panel strips and L vignettes are comedy and character sketches; the 2003 pilot, placed last, is a simpler, gentler story of a bullied boy whose mistake can still be undone. Anime UK News warns that the first pages spoil the ending of the main series.

**Discussion questions.**
1. C-Kira only killed old people who said they wanted to die. Why does Near still call him a murderer?
2. Minoru never killed anyone with the notebook. Was selling it to a government right or wrong?
3. Why do people in the story start calling Kira a god again after a-Kira? What does that say about crowds?
4. In the pilot, Taro confesses and is forgiven. What made his story end differently from the others?
5. If someone you knew was being bullied, what better "notebook" could they have than one that kills?
