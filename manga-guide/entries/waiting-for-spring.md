**Premise.** Mitsuki Haruno has always struggled to make friends, so she starts high school at Seiryo, where no one knows her, hoping to change and make one true friend. She works part-time at a café run by the family of Nanase ("Nana"), who becomes her mentor. The "four heavenly kings" of the boys' basketball team start dropping by: her classmate Towa Asakura, the prankster Rui Miyamoto, and second-years Kyosuke Wakamiya and Ryuji Tada (who is in love with Nanase). Mitsuki becomes their friend and also makes girl friends: Reina, a fan who imagines the boys as couples, and Maki, a girls' basketball player who secretly likes Towa. The team has a no-dating rule. Then Aya Kamiyama returns: the childhood friend Mitsuki thought was a girl, now the star of rival Hōjō High, who has loved her for years. Across tournaments, a school festival, an amusement park, a hot-spring trip and a lost game that keeps the dating ban in place (vol. 12), Towa and Aya both confess (vol. 9); Mitsuki and Towa become a couple (vol. 13); the last volume follows the friends' own romances and Aya's heartbreak (Wikipedia; Kodansha USA; Penguin Random House).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How does a person who freezes around others learn to belong? Mitsuki picks a new school to start over, with one modest goal: to talk more easily and make one good friend (ANN).

**The answer the story gives.** By small acts of honesty, kindness and courage, and by letting others help. The boys are thoughtless at first and come to understand her (ANN); Reina and Maki become friends; Nanase gives advice. Love comes after friendship and is made to wait: the team's ban stays after a lost game, and Mitsuki chooses to wait (vol. 12) before she and Towa become a couple (vol. 13).

**Characters as arguments.** Mitsuki argues that honesty is braver than charm. Towa, indifferent to fans, finds her easy to be with because she is real (Wikipedia). Aya argues for constancy, but his refusal to accept her no is the story's questioned note (Soliloquy in Blue). Reina's fandom shows another way to be an outsider who is happy anyway.

**Symbols and motifs.** Spring as the season of beginnings; the Ferris wheel (vol. 5); the basketball court where rivalries are settled; the café as a safe place to grow.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo friendship-and-romance story (*Kimi ni Todoke*, *Say I Love You*), the sports manga's team bond, and the old idea that friendship is a school of virtue.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and expressive individualism work together: Mitsuki becomes more herself by belonging to a group. Virtue ethics shows in rewarded honesty and patience. No religious or political lens appears.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: friends, Aya's return, the first tournament. Vols. 4-8: preliminaries, the amusement park, the festival, Towa's feelings. Vols. 9-12: two confessions, injuries, a hot-spring trip, the tournament that keeps the ban. Vols. 13-14: Towa and Mitsuki as a couple, the friends' own romances and side stories.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mitsuki chose a school where nobody knew her. Would you ever want a fresh start? Why?
2. What small things did Mitsuki do that made her braver?
3. Mitsuki told Aya no. Did he respect that? What should someone do after hearing no?
4. Why might a team ban dating? Was it fair that Mitsuki and Towa had to wait?
5. Who is the best friend in the story, and what makes them a good friend?
