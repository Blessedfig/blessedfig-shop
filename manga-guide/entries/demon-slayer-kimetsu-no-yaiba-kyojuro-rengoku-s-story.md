**Premise.** Taisho-era Japan, before the main story. Kyojuro Rengoku, a Kinoe (top-rank) Demon Slayer not yet a Hashira, trains his student Mitsuri Kanroji, who has just passed Final Selection. His father Shinjuro, the Flame Hashira, has drunk heavily and refused his duties since his wife's death; when a Hashira meeting is called he throws a pot of alcohol at his son and says Kyojuro will never become a Hashira. Kyojuro attends in his place. The Wind Hashira Sanemi beats him to test him; Kyojuro refuses to strike back because swordsmen may not strike each other, and the Corps leader Kagaya sends him to Tokyo to hunt a suspected Twelve Kizuki demon. The demon is Hairo, Lower Rank Two, a former swordsman humiliated by soldiers with guns, who now fights with summoned rifles, a Gatling gun, dynamite and shadow wolves, and who mistakes Kyojuro for Shinjuro, the drunken Hashira who once cut off his hands again and again and spared him. While Mitsuri finds her own fighting style protecting a mother and child who had mocked her, Kyojuro takes every bullet and blast, recalls his mother's teaching that the strong must protect the weak, and beheads Hairo with the Ninth Form, Rengoku. He is made Flame Hashira; a closing flash-forward shows his death against Akaza and the cherry blossoms falling at the Rengoku home (fan wiki, Part 1 and Part 2).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a son owe a father who has given up? Shinjuro has stopped serving, drinks, and tells Kyojuro his family's art is weak. Kyojuro must decide whether to resent him, abandon the family calling, or carry it himself.

**The answer the story gives.** Carry it, and carry it without bitterness. Kyojuro will not speak against his father and believes he will recover; he takes the post to motivate him (Part 1). His compass is his mother's teaching that strength exists to protect the weak.

**Characters as arguments.** Kyojuro argues for duty and kindness held together. Shinjuro is the cost of despair, and his cruelty to a defeated enemy created the villain, so wrongs done in weakness come back. Hairo is rage given form: a swordsman humiliated by guns who became a gunman who hates guns, finally choosing the sword and conceding defeat with respect. Sanemi argues that worth must be proven by force, and is rebuked. Mitsuri argues that the way to be strong is to be yourself.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Flame Hashira's cloak that Kyojuro looks at but must earn, and that hangs empty in the last panel; the Ninth Form, which bears the family name; guns against swords; cherry blossoms, linked with Mitsuri and with remembrance.

**Traditions it echoes.** Filial piety and the eldest son's duty; the warrior's code; and, for faith readers, honoring father and mother and refusing to return evil for evil.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue, duty and Confucian family ethics win. Might makes right is rebuked. Bushido is explored through a villain who betrayed his own sword and returns to it at the end. Expressive individualism appears gently, as Mitsuri's self-acceptance inside a duty-centered story.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 is family drama and the Hashira meeting, ending as the battle begins; Part 2 is the battle, Mitsuri's breakthrough, the homecoming and the coda that points to the main series.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kyojuro's father treats him badly. Why doesn't Kyojuro speak against him? Would you?
2. Sanemi beats Kyojuro to test him, and Kyojuro will not hit back. Was that weakness or strength?
3. Hairo's grudge began when a drunk Hashira was cruel to him. Does that make Hairo less responsible for what he does?
4. Mitsuri saves a woman who had insulted her. Why does that matter to the story?
5. Kyojuro's mother said the strong must protect the weak. Where do you see that in your own life?
