**Premise.** Haru Tachibana dies of cancer at 19 after chemotherapy fails; her last word is the name of her fiancé, Togo Hiiragi, the heir of a wealthy banking family to whom she was engaged by family arrangement (Otaku USA; Now Read This!; ciatr). Her older sister Natsumi, who works a part-time job and had planned to live with Haru one day, had leaned on Haru as her only real family: their mother left when Natsumi was in fifth grade, and their father, who remarried and had a son, Kaito, disliked Natsumi for resembling her mother (Renote; Japanese Wikipedia). Natsumi had resented Togo so much for taking Haru that she had wanted to kill him (Japanese Wikipedia). After the funeral, Togo's family proposes that he date Natsumi instead, and she agrees on one condition: he must take her only to the places he went with Haru (ch. 1). Over the seasons (ch. 2-8) the two fall for each other, and guilt, family pressure and Haru's memory hang over them like a curse.

## Life message

**Grief and guilt cannot be outrun by living inside a dead loved one's memory; the living honor the dead by being honest about their own love and by choosing their own lives.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you love someone your dead sister loved without betraying her? Natsumi's condition, that Togo take her only where he took Haru, is a way of keeping Haru alive and of punishing herself at the same time.

**The answer the story gives.** The dead do not need us to stay frozen. Haru's diary shows that she wanted Togo to be happy even without her, and that she envied and admired Natsumi (Renote; ciatr). The curse is not Haru's; it is the guilt the living lay on themselves. The way out is honesty: Natsumi and Togo say what they feel and choose their own life (ch. 8).

**Characters as arguments.** Natsumi argues that loyalty means never moving on; her near-suicide shows where that leads. Togo argues for doing what the family expects, until losing Natsumi shows him that his life has been empty. Haru, through her diary, argues that love wants the other's good. Togo's mother argues for lineage and duty, and is left behind but not condemned.

**Symbols and motifs.** Seasons (from "Spring is gone" to "and winter will come"); the places Togo and Haru visited, retraced; Haru's dying word; Haru's online diary; railway crossings as places of danger and choice; a photo of the sisters placed in the coffin.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese josei grief drama and the literary use of the seasons; the old story of a sibling's betrothed passing to the surviving sibling, here questioned rather than obeyed. For Christian readers, lament and the call to choose life; for Buddhist readers, clinging and letting go; for secular readers, self-determination after loss.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and expressive individualism carry the ending; traditionalism and Confucian family duty are the weight the leads must shed, shown as rigid rather than evil. Religion is absent; healing comes from honesty and love.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-3 (spring to August) set up the arrangement and Natsumi's hostility; ch. 4-5 (September) bring Haru's diary and Natsumi's darkest moment; ch. 6-7 (November) bring the break-up, family upheaval, Togo's accident and the confession; ch. 8 is the ending and leaving home; bonus comics explain the characters' changes.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Natsumi ask Togo to take her only where he had taken Haru?
2. What does Haru's diary change about how Natsumi sees her sister?
3. Togo stops Natsumi at the crossing. What should someone do if a friend talks about wanting to die?
4. Were Togo's family and Natsumi's family wrong, or only too rigid?
5. The couple leave home together at the end. What do they gain, and what do they give up?
