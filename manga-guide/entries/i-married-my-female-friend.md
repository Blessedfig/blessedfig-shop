**Premise.** At their Coming of Age Day ceremony (age 20), Ruriko Aizawa tells her free-spirited best friend Kurumi Sera, who fears no one will ever want to marry her, that Japan will soon legalize same-sex marriage, and suggests that if neither is married in five years they marry each other. Five years later Ruriko comes home from pastry training in France and keeps the promise (TV Tropes). In this story's Japan same-sex marriage is newly legal (AniList review); in real-world Japan it was not legal nationwide during the series' run (from general knowledge; to verify). Kurumi, a writer, is assigned a column about being married to a woman (Wikipedia; Okazu). They sleep in separate rooms, split costs 50-50 and write house rules. Ruriko has loved Kurumi from the start; Kurumi slowly falls in love. Along the way Ruriko is hospitalized with appendicitis (ch. 7-8), the two nearly fall apart over a broken rule and agree on "bonding days" (ch. 11-13, vol. 2), Ruriko takes in Rio, a younger friend estranged from her parents (ch. 20-21, vol. 3), and Kurumi grows jealous and then lonely when Rio moves out to work at a bakery (vol. 4). The final chapter is their wedding ceremony (TV Tropes; Penguin Random House).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a marriage, if it does not start with romance? Kurumi and Ruriko marry because they promised to, not because they fell in love, and the series asks whether friendship, fairness and care can be enough, and what happens when one partner wants more.

**The answer the story gives.** Partnership is something you build: you write rules, break them, and rewrite them (vol. 2); you take care of each other when sick (ch. 7-8); you make time for each other ("bonding days," ch. 13). Friedman reads its core message as love needing to be expressed clearly to be returned (Okazu). In the end love does grow, and the series closes on a wedding (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Ruriko argues for patient, faithful love that waits. Kurumi argues for freedom and honesty, and slowly learns that commitment does not mean losing herself. Ruriko's parents argue that marriage without romance is empty; the story answers that romance can come later. Rio, estranged from her own parents, shows the household as a place of refuge.

**Symbols and motifs.** The five-year promise; the house rules; the washer-dryer they buy together (ch. 11-13); a photo of a lone daikon that Ruriko knows Kurumi will love (Okazu); separate bedrooms; the wedding.

**Traditions it echoes.** The marriage-of-convenience romance and the Japanese slice-of-life household story. Friedman links it to Usui's Doughnuts Under a Crescent Moon. For some readers it echoes older ideas that love follows commitment, as in arranged marriage, while its couple is one that many faiths do not recognize. My own framing.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Progressivism is the setting, not the argument. Pragmatism carries the plot. Communitarianism appears in chosen family. Traditional marriage values (fidelity, permanence, a wedding) are honored and applied to a same-sex couple.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the pact, settling in, the hospital. Vol. 2: a near-breakup and new rules; an extra chapter on their school days. Vol. 3: Rio and jealousy. Vol. 4: loneliness, Kurumi's realization, the wedding.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kurumi and Ruriko made a promise at 20. Should promises like that be kept?
2. They wrote house rules. Which rule would matter most in your home?
3. Ruriko hid that she was sick. Why, and what changed afterward?
4. Ruriko's parents worried about a marriage without romantic love. What do you think makes a marriage?
5. Different families and faiths see this couple's marriage differently. How does your family see it, and why?
