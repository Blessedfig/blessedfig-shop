**Premise.** Ryo Saeba survived a plane crash at age three in Central America, was raised as a guerrilla fighter in a civil war, and was used as the test subject for a combat drug, Angel Dust, by his adoptive father Shin Kaibara (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). Now in Tokyo, he is a "sweeper," a gun for hire, bodyguard and troubleshooter, contacted by writing XYZ on the message board at Shinjuku Station. His partner, ex-detective Hideyuki Makimura, is murdered by a drugged killer (vol. 1), and Ryo keeps his promise to look after Hideyuki's adoptive sister, Kaori, who becomes his new partner and pounds him with a giant hammer whenever he chases women (Wikipedia). Each story features a new client, most of them beautiful women: Ryo is a crack shot and a protector with a heart of gold, and also a relentless lecher. Recurring figures include the mercenary Umibozu and his partner Miki, police detective Saeko Nogami, the American sweeper Mick Angel, and finally Kaibara, now a drug lord of the syndicate Union Teope. Ryo comes to admit he loves Kaori (TV Tropes; ending details to verify).

**Behind the clown's mask, a good man protects people who cannot protect themselves; keeping your word to a dead friend and the steady love of a partner can redeem even a violent past.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a good man also be a fool, and can a killer be a protector? Ryo Saeba was made into a weapon as a child and now sells his gun in Shinjuku. The series asks what keeps such a man human.

**The answer the story gives.** Promises and people. Ryo keeps Hideyuki's last wish and guards Kaori; he risks his life for each client, often for little pay. His lechery is a mask he wears to keep people, especially Kaori, at a distance from a dangerous life (TV Tropes says he claims he holds back because of his enemies). The final arc, against the adoptive father who drugged him as a child, ends with Ryo admitting his love (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Ryo argues for loyalty and protection under a cloak of comedy. Kaori argues for steady, faithful love and moral limits, enforced with a hammer. Umibozu argues for warrior honor. Saeko represents the law that needs the outlaw. Kaibara argues that power is made by drugs and child soldiers, and loses.

**Symbols and motifs.** XYZ on the station board (the last letters, a last resort); the Colt Python and the "one-hole shot"; Kaori's ever-heavier hammer; the rigged gun that keeps Kaori from killing; the Cat's Eye café as home base.

**Traditions it echoes.** Hard-boiled detective fiction and the James Bond and Lupin III mix that Kana advertises; the gun-for-hire with a code. Christian readers may see a man whose real self is shown in sacrifice (John 15:13); many traditions will also see a man whose desires are never disciplined, only interrupted.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue, duty and honor are affirmed in action. Hedonism is played for laughs and never rewarded: Ryo almost never "succeeds." The use of strength to prey on others is defeated. Women are both strong partners and gag targets.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: hard-boiled stories, Angel Dust and Hideyuki's death, Kaori joins (ch. 1-5). Middle volumes: episodic heroine-of-the-week comedy-action with Umibozu, Miki and Saeko. Late volumes: Mick Angel, Kaori's birth family, and the Kaibara arc; the last volume returns to a darker tone (TV Tropes) and ends with Umibozu and Miki's wedding.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ryo jokes and chases women, but turns serious when someone is in danger. Which is the real Ryo?
2. Kaori hits Ryo with a hammer when he harasses women. Is that a good answer to bad behavior?
3. Why does Ryo make Kaori's gun unable to hit anyone?
4. Is it right for a private person to punish criminals when the police cannot?
