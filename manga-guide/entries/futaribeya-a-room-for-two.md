**Premise.** Sakurako Kawawa, a responsible, studious girl starting high school, moves into lodgings tied to her school and meets her roommate Kasumi Yamabuki, a beautiful, sleepy, easygoing girl who works part-time and does some magazine modeling. The opposites get along at once: they share matching mugs and a single bed. In four-panel gag strips the series follows their high-school years (vols. 1-4), college (vols. 5-8, now in a one-room apartment), graduation and a trip to Hawaii (vol. 9), and their first jobs as adults (vol. 10), still living together and sharing one bed. Friends include the landlord Natsuki Hyuuga, 19, classmates Azusa and Yukari, Sakurako's younger sister (who has a crush on Kasumi), and the college friends Moka and Koruri, whose own relationship "takes an exciting new turn" (Tokyopop). Whether Sakurako and Kasumi are a couple is left mostly unspoken: Okazu called the early volumes yuri mostly in readers' imaginations and found the ending still relies on readers seeing it for themselves (Wikipedia; Tokyopop; Okazu; Manga Bookshelf).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes two people a household? Futaribeya asks it without drama: two very different girls are put in one room and discover that they fit.

**The answer the story gives.** Small things done every day: cooking, waking each other, matching mugs, a microwave oven given on White Day. The story never names the relationship, but it ends with the two still sharing one room and one bed as working adults (Tokyopop, vol. 10).

**Characters as arguments.** Sakurako argues for responsibility and care; Kasumi for ease and acceptance. Each softens the other. Sakurako's sister and the college pair Moka and Koruri show other kinds of attraction, treated lightly and kindly.

**Symbols and motifs.** The one bed; the matching mugs; the room itself, which moves from school lodgings to a one-room apartment; Kasumi's naps.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese 4-koma "cute girls doing cute things" tradition (Hidamari Sketch, which Manga Bookshelf invokes) and older yuri that lived largely in subtext (Okazu compares it to Aria and Lucky Star in how fans read it).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian and virtue readings are affirmed. The progressive reading (an LGBT couple) is invited by the publisher's marketing and the ending but never argued; critics on that side wanted more. Traditional readers can read most of it as friendship, but the direction of the last volumes is clear to most readers.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4: high school in supervised lodgings. Vols. 5-8: college, a one-room apartment, new friends, job hunting. Vol. 9: graduation and Hawaii. Vol. 10: first jobs and adult routine (Tokyopop blurbs).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Sakurako and Kasumi are very different. Why do they get along so well?
2. What small things show that they care for each other?
3. Is this a story about friendship, romance or both? What makes you think so?
4. How does the story handle growing up and leaving school?
5. What would you want in a roommate?
