**Premise.** In an alternate 19th-century Europe, five years after the Fosanian Conflict, a war fought over "Agartha" water that turns blood into steam, cities are rebuilding and the crowd sport of Mechanical Martial Arts (metal boxing) pits cyborg fighters against each other. Levius Cromwell, a quiet teenager, lost his father in the war, his right arm to a steam cannon, and his mother to a coma when she shielded him (backstory, ch. 2). He lives with his uncle Zacks, a former boxer who becomes his trainer, and his grandmother Masha, and fights with an arm built by the brilliant mechanic Bill Weinberg. He beats the aging Malcolm Eden (ch. 4), then watches a mysterious fighter, A.J. Langdon, destroy the champion Hugo Stratus (ch. 5-6). A.J. is the girl Levius saw kidnapped during the war: the military corporation Amethyst made her a child soldier at 11 and her guardian, Dr. Clown Jack Pudding, controls her with surgery, drugs and threats against her brother (ch. 9, 15). Clown offers a deal: if Levius wins their promotion bout, A.J. goes free; if he loses, he belongs to Amethyst (ch. 10). The final match (ch. 11-18) is fought to the edge of death (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**People are not tools: fight for those you love and to free those who are used and controlled, even at great cost, and the people who stand by you are what make you strong.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a person a person when bodies can be rebuilt as machines, and who has the right to own someone? Levius has a metal arm; A.J. has been rebuilt almost entirely as a weapon (ch. 16). Clown treats both as instruments.

**The answer the story gives.** A person is whoever is loved and chooses for themselves. Levius will not be bought (ch. 10) and wins the last exchange with his own flesh-and-blood arm (anime ep. 12), the part of him no one built. A.J. still has a buried will (she whispers a warning in ch. 10).

**Characters as arguments.** Levius argues for fighting to protect. Zacks argues for love that sees limits and still risks itself. Bill argues that technology should heal. Clown argues that people are material for art and power, and the story mocks him: his body in the ring is a puppet. Malcolm and Hugo show the arena's other side: men who gave themselves to it and died.

**Symbols and motifs.** Steam from blood: fighting literally burns life. The two arms, metal and human. The comatose mother. Clown's mask and his automaton doubles. The tank where A.J. is kept (ch. 9).

**Traditions it echoes.** Boxing stories and gladiator tales; steampunk and cyberpunk worries about the body as machine (ComicBookBin compares it to Ghost in the Shell); the rescue of a captive princess, turned so that the captive is also the strongest fighter.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism and strength-rules are the targets: the people who most want to turn humans into weapons are the villains. Classical virtue and community carry the hero. Critical theory's anti-corporate note is real but stays in the background. Pacifism is not tried.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-6) sets up Levius's past, the Malcolm fight and A.J.'s destruction of Hugo. Vol. 2 (ch. 7-12) is the visit to Clown's mansion and the run-up to the bout. Vol. 3 (ch. 13-18) is the single title fight, Clown's backstory of A.J.'s captivity (ch. 15) and its end. Reviewers find vol. 3 weaker (ANN; AIPT). *Levius/est* continues the story separately.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Levius won't replace his remaining human arm. Why might that matter to him?
2. Clown says A.J. is his. What makes someone free?
3. Is it right for crowds to enjoy watching fighters risk death? Why do they?
4. Zacks risks his eye to help Levius. What do we owe the people we love?
5. A.J. hurt people while she was controlled. Is she to blame?
