**Premise.** Makoto "Mako" Nasuno is an easygoing, plain high school boy who likes to spend his afternoons lazing in the empty classroom. His classmate and best friend Sakuya Sakurai keeps sitting very close, asking odd questions and teasing him with double meanings. He cannot tell whether she is flirting or just being friendly; the reader can see she has fallen for him first (MangaUpdates; ANN). Vol. 1 runs through familiar romcom set pieces: sharing an umbrella in the rain, a beach trip with a bikini reveal, and a confession drowned out by a passing train (Honey's Anime). In vol. 2 she blushes more than he does; in vol. 3 they are alone in her room and he wonders if she wants to kiss (publisher blurbs). In vol. 4 Mako decides to stop playing defense and make his move; the two confess, become a couple and end with a date and promises to stay together (publisher blurb; Goodreads reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you know whether someone likes you? Mako's whole problem is doubt: a pretty, popular girl keeps sitting close and teasing him, and he decides it cannot mean what it seems to mean.

**The answer the story gives.** You find out by saying it. Four volumes of hints, near-misses and a confession lost under a passing train (vol. 1) end when Mako stops waiting and acts (vol. 4).

**Characters as arguments.** Sakurai argues for showing feelings without the risk of saying them; the story gently shows that hints are not enough. Mako argues for caution and self-doubt; the story rewards him when he lets them go.

**Symbols and motifs.** The empty classroom after school as a private world; the shared umbrella; the train that drowns out a confession; the close-up distance between two desks.

**Traditions it echoes.** The teasing-girl romantic comedy of recent manga, and the older Japanese romance of the unspoken confession. For many religious readers it raises truthfulness in speech, but the book's comedy is built on innuendo and fan service.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: honesty about feelings is the payoff. Hedonism appears only in the camera's interest in the heroine.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 sets up the teasing and the near-misses; vol. 2 shows Sakurai flustered too; vol. 3 brings them close to a kiss; vol. 4 brings the confession and a date.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think Sakurai teases Mako instead of telling him how she feels?
2. Mako assumes she cannot like him. Where does that idea come from?
3. Is teasing a good way to show you like someone? When can it hurt?
4. What changed for Mako by the last volume?
5. The publisher sells the book on its fan service. Does that change how you see the characters?
