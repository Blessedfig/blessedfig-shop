**Premise.** Momo Ayase is a high school girl who believes in ghosts but not aliens; her grandmother Seiko is a working spirit medium. Ken Takakura, nicknamed Okarun, is a bullied classmate who believes in aliens but not ghosts. They make a bet: each visits a place tied to the other's belief. Both turn out to be right. Aliens (the Serpoians) abduct Momo, and a yokai called Turbo Granny curses Okarun and steals his genitals. Momo awakens psychic powers, and Okarun can borrow Turbo Granny's speed. The rest of the series is a run of monster-of-the-week arcs (yokai, urban legends, cursed objects, alien invaders, a kaiju), a growing group of misfit friends, and a slow romance between the two leads. Okarun's long quest to recover his "kintama" (testicles, literally "golden balls") is the running thread of the first 19 volumes. In the latest volumes a curse shrinks Momo and erases her from others' memory.

## Life message

**A good life is lived with kindness toward people, spirits and outsiders alike: stand by your friends, protect the vulnerable, eat well after hard work, and do not let fear or other people's mockery decide who you are.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | Reproduction is literally the monsters' target: the Serpoians want human reproductive organs to restore their species (ch. 1, 20), and the curse that drives the plot takes a boy's testicles. Read this way, the comedy and horror both play on adolescent anxiety about sex, fertility and the body. Mate choice is slow and mutual: Momo leaves a boyfriend who pressures her for sex (ch. 1) and grows toward a loyal, kind boy. Kin bonds are strong (Seiko raising Momo; Silky's loss of her daughter). |
| Pragmatic | Problems are solved by teamwork, improvisation and borrowed expertise (Seiko's barriers, Kinta's robot, alien and yokai allies). Rules about the spirit world are practical: Seiko's barriers only work within the region of the god whose power she borrows, and she must thank that god at temples for three days (fan wiki). Tatsu's stated message is that "It's pointless if you work hard and get nothing" (Gamepressure, reporting an interview). Meals after battles reward effort. |
| Religious | Japanese folk religion is treated as real: yokai, ghosts, curses, talismans on a torii gate, exorcisms, and a grandmother who is "an honest-to-God medium" (Plugged In). Momo trains as a medium. Spirits of the dead are pitied and helped to "pass on" (Silky, ch. 17; Rin's friend Mai, ch. 124-125). Religion used to harm is condemned: a family that sacrifices children to a serpent god (Cursed House arc, ch. 28-50), a fake priest (v24), a curse-using secret society (v25). Aliens and ancient Sumerian figures (Vamola, the Kur) share the same world. |
| Socio-cultural | A friend-group of misfits (an occult nerd, a gyaru girl, a vain "chosen one," a rich boy, an otaku with a robot, a delinquent) forms a found family. Bullying, debt, poverty, child sacrifice, a teacher blackmailing a student over lewd selfies (v20-22) and predatory men are the human evils. Gender roles are loose: Momo is the strongest fighter early on. Sexual threat aimed at teens recurs and is framed as villainy, but critics say it is used too often and sometimes for laughs (see How readers took it). |

**Where traditions disagree.** The main dividing line is the spirit world. Shinto and Wiccan / Pagan frames find a close fit, because Seiko's medium practice, talismans and pacifying of the dead are shown as good and respectful. Jehovah's Witness, Seventh-day Adventist, Pentecostal and Orthodox Jewish frames see the same material as spiritism or necromancy endorsed, and grade lowest. Most other Christian frames, Muslim, Sikh and Bahá'í land at C for the same reason. On depiction, almost every frame raises the repeated sexual threats to teen characters and the nudity, though they differ in why: modesty traditions (Orthodox Jewish, Muslim, Adventist, Anabaptist) point to the images themselves, while secular progressive and feminist readers point to sexualization of minors and assault used as a plot device. Secular, Reform, Mainline and UU frames grade the message well for its kindness and inclusion.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yukinobu Tatsu worked as an assistant on *Chainsaw Man* and *Hell's Paradise*. The series was approved in mid-2020, but he delayed its start until both assistant jobs ended so he could finish them (Wikipedia).
- On-record statements: on the meal scenes after battles, he cites *Jarinko Chie*, shared meals in Miyazaki films, and his mentor giving him money for a meal; the scenes are meant to give readers hope (Wikipedia). In an interview reported by Gamepressure (October 29, 2024): "It's pointless if you work hard and get nothing. I think kindness is important." In a Manga Plus 6th-anniversary interview (reported by Anime Corner, February 13, 2025) he named Momo his favorite character, said he redraws her when she is not cute enough, and compared her to Superman, whose "presence inspires people, giving them the courage to stand up against such evil."
- No public statement by Tatsu about the sexual-assault scenes, religion or politics was found. No statements about his private life were found; this entry does not speculate. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Shonen Jump+). Viz Media publishes in English (Viz is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan; from general knowledge). Science Saru animates.
- Controversy (anime, not the manga's creator): Season 2 episode 6's song "Hunting Soul" was noted to resemble X Japan's 1989 single "Kurenai." Yoshiki said lawyers had contacted him about possible infringement; the production committee apologized on August 22, 2025, saying it had not explained the homage to him in advance, and Yoshiki then thanked them and mentioned possible collaboration (AniTrendz, opened).

**Profile notes for parents:** The series is built on care and loyalty, with a heroine raised by a spirit-medium grandmother and a spirit world treated as real. Its sharpest content is sexual: repeated attempted assaults on a teen girl, constant genital humor and frequent nudity, which is why Viz rates it Mature and Common Sense Media says 15+. The suicide backstories (Silky, the Kito parents, Zuma's mother) are the other scenes most families will want to talk through.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is real, and how should you treat what you do not believe in? Momo and Okarun each scoff at the other's belief, and each is proven wrong within hours. The story then asks a more practical question: once you know the world is full of strange beings, some cruel and some suffering, how do you live with them?

**The answer the story gives.** With kindness, rules and friends. Seiko's craft is built on coexistence: she may use force against spirits that attack the living, but she does not disrespect the dead (fan wiki). The heroes defeat monsters, then often help them. Acrobatic Silky, the ghost of a mother who lost her daughter, gives her power to revive a girl and passes on (ch. 17). The Evil Eye, a sacrificed child who became a curse, ends up living with the friends as someone who only wanted to play (ch. 49-61). The creator puts it simply: "I think kindness is important" (Gamepressure).

**Characters as arguments.** Momo is the argument that toughness and tenderness go together; Tatsu compares her to Superman, whose presence gives others courage. Okarun is the argument that the bullied nerd deserves respect, and that a good man keeps his promises. Seiko is the argument that inherited wisdom, even unusual wisdom, protects the young. Turbo Granny is vulgarity with a hidden heart: she comforts the spirits of murdered girls. The Serpoians, the Kito men and the gym teacher are arguments against using others' bodies as resources. The Kito family stands for tradition turned into atrocity.

**Symbols and motifs.** The "kintama" quest turns adolescent fear about the body into a long running joke and a journey toward manhood. Food after battle stands for reward and hope. Torii gates and talismans mark the line between the human and spirit worlds. The red dress Silky bought for her daughter is the series' most quoted image of love and loss.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese folk religion and yokai lore, Showa-era occult boom and UFO magazines, the urban legends of the internet age, Ultraman-style kaiju battles, Western UFO lore (Flatwoods Monster, Serpo, Nessie), Buddhist ideas of attachment and release, and the shonen formula of friendship and training.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Folk religion is affirmed when it serves the living and the dead, and condemned when it demands sacrifice or deceives (fake priest, v24). Feminist readings divide: Momo is the strongest character early on, but critics see her sexual victimization as overused (ANN; The Mary Sue), while others argue the villains reflect real dangers of adolescence and the story frames consent around respect (Reactor). Expressive individualism is affirmed for outsiders. Might makes right fails every time.

**Shifts across the series.** The opening arcs (ch. 1-27) are frantic horror-comedy with the heaviest sexual menace. The Cursed House and Evil Eye arcs (ch. 28-62) add real-world horror: child sacrifice, suicide, predatory men. The middle (v9-14) turns to kaiju and alien-invasion spectacle. Later arcs (v15-25) add grief stories (Rin and Mai, Zuma's family), the romance's confession (v19), and a slower, sadder curse that erases Momo from others' lives. The series is still running.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Momo and Okarun each mocked something the other believed. What changed when they took each other seriously?
2. Seiko helps spirits "pass on" and refuses payment from Okarun. What does your family's tradition say about the dead and about people who claim to deal with them?
3. The story shows several adults who try to exploit teens. How do the heroes respond, and what would you do in their place?
4. Silky did terrible things because she loved her lost daughter. Does understanding someone's pain change how we judge what they did?
5. Some reviewers say the story uses sexual threats too often; others say it shows real dangers honestly. What do you think, and why?
