**Premise.** Momo Ayase, a high school girl raised by her grandmother Seiko, a spirit medium, believes in ghosts but not aliens; her classmate Ken Takakura, a bullied occult fan she nicknames "Okarun," believes in aliens but not ghosts. On a bet, each visits the other's haunt: Momo is abducted by aliens from Serpo, who unlock her psychic powers, and Okarun is cursed by the Turbo Granny, a tunnel ghost who takes his genitals ("kintama") (ch. 1-8). Hunting for them draws in Aira Shiratori, who has one of them and is stalked by the grieving ghost Acrobatic Silky (ch. 9-17); Momo's childhood friend Jiji and his family's haunted house, run by the Kito family who have sacrificed children to a creature for 200 years (ch. 28-50); the Evil Eye, the ghost of one such sacrificed boy (ch. 51-62); a kaiju, an alien invasion and new friends (ch. 63-120); a cursed diorama (ch. 129-165); a teacher who blackmails a student into attacking Momo (ch. 166-191); and a curse that shrinks Momo until it is lifted in Shimane, leaving her without her memories of Okarun and the others (ch. 192-254) (Wikipedia; fan wiki Story Arcs).

## Life message

**A good life is lived with kindness toward people, spirits and outsiders alike: stand by your friends, protect the vulnerable, eat well after hard work, and do not let fear or other people's mockery decide who you are.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame welcomes the series' kindness to outsiders, its loyalty and its condemnation of predators and child sacrifice. Two things divide them. First, the medium's practice: most Christian, Jewish and Muslim readings treat contacting and commanding spirits, shown as good, as the core concern (C or D), while Shinto and Pagan readings see familiar, respectful folk practice (A), and Atheist and Agnostic readings treat it as fiction. Second, nearly every frame, secular ones included, raises the repeated sexual menace and nudity involving teenage characters as a depiction concern.

Those who show kindness and stick together (Momo, Okarun, Seiko and their growing family of friends, human and otherwise) survive and help even former enemies, while those who exploit the vulnerable (the Serpo, the Kito family, the blackmailing teacher) are defeated or exposed.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yukinobu Tatsu, born in Saitama Prefecture around 1990, never attended art school; as a junior high student he loved Berserk, Project ARMS, Spriggan, Akira and To Love Ru (Wikipedia, Yukinobu Tatsu). He assisted Tatsuki Fujimoto (Fire Punch, Chainsaw Man) and Yuji Kaku (Hell's Paradise) and delayed Dandadan until both series ended so he could finish his assistant duties (Wikipedia, series article).
- On-record intent: Tatsu adds a meal after each major event because "hard work merits good food," citing Jarinko Chie and the shared meals in Miyazaki's My Neighbor Totoro and Porco Rosso, and a mentor who once gave him 1,000 yen for dinner; he includes them to give readers hope (Wikipedia, Production).
- No interview in sources opened addresses the series' sexual content or its use of folk religion. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Shonen Jump+, a web platform). VIZ Media publishes it in English under its Shonen Jump line, rated Mature.
- No controversies involving the creator personally were found. The anime's handling of the hot spring cliffhanger drew criticism (The Mary Sue, December 2024).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you owe to people, and beings, that everyone else mocks or fears? Momo and Okarun are outsiders who each believe in one kind of strange thing; the story proves them both right and asks how to treat the strange once you know it is real.

**The answer the story gives.** With kindness, and with your friends beside you. The monsters are often grieving people (Silky, ch. 17; the Evil Eye, ch. 51-62), and the friends win as much by understanding them as by beating them. Tatsu ends each arc with a shared meal as a promise of hope (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Momo argues for courage and plain speech; Okarun for loyalty and keeping going when mocked. Seiko argues that the living and the dead can coexist if treated with respect. Silky argues, tragically, that love can become possession. The Kito family argues that a village's safety justifies sacrificing outsiders' children, and is destroyed by the creature it fed. The teacher argues that power over the young is a resource, and is arrested.

**Symbols and motifs.** The missing "golden balls" (a crude quest object that is also a joke about masculinity), the haunted tunnel, the torii gate with talismans, the shared dinner spread, and Momo's lost memories in the latest arc, which turn the romance into a second courtship.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese yokai stories and internet urban legends, UFO lore (the Serpo, the Dover Demon, Nessie), tokusatsu and kaiju films (AniList tags), and the grindhouse horror-comedy of Tatsu's mentors (Chainsaw Man).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan / occult practice is affirmed as protective and ethical when used to help; Buddhist-style release of the dead is shown with compassion; communitarianism is the found family. Feminism is contested: a strong heroine who is also repeatedly threatened with sexual violence, which critics say the story uses too often.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-27) are the kintama hunt with the most sexual menace and crude humor. Volumes 4-8 (ch. 28-62) are the darkest horror (the Kito sacrifice house, the Evil Eye). Volumes 8-14 (ch. 63-120) widen into kaiju and alien-invasion action. Volumes 15-22 (ch. 121-191) bring the cursed diorama and the teacher's blackmail plot. Volumes 22-26 (ch. 192-254) follow Momo's curse, the trip to Shimane and her amnesia.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Momo believed in ghosts and Okarun in aliens, and both were right. What does that say about being sure you are right?
2. Several monsters turn out to be grieving people. Does knowing why someone is hurting change how you treat them?
3. The series often puts Momo in sexual danger. Why do some critics object to that, and what do you think?
4. Seiko is a spirit medium. What does your family believe about contacting the dead?
5. Why does every arc end with a big meal together?
