**Premise.** In a sooty factory town, nine middle-school boys meet in a secret clubhouse in an abandoned factory: the Light Club. Their leader, Zera, a beautiful and paranoid boy who addresses the members in German and models himself on the Roman emperor Elagabalus, wants to stay young forever and purge the town of ugliness. The club builds Lychee, a thinking machine fueled by lychee fruit, to abduct beautiful girls for them to idolize. Lychee brings back Kanon, a Christian girl the boys' age, whom the club imprisons, worships and later means to sacrifice. Lychee, programmed to believe it is human, grows more human through Kanon, who teaches it that being human means not killing. Meanwhile the club turns on itself: the founder Tamiya tries to take back the club, Jaibo betrays Zera, and murders multiply until nearly everyone is dead and Kanon alone survives (Kodansha blurb; Wikipedia; The Comics Journal; Manga Bookshelf; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A group that worships beauty, youth and its leader's will destroys itself, and the machine it built to serve that will learns what its makers never did: that being human means not killing.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Usamaru Furuya (born 1968, Tokyo) studied oil painting at Tama Art University and took an interest in sculpture and Butoh dance; he debuted in Garo (1994), then worked across mainstream seinen and shonen magazines and alternative venues such as Manga Erotics F, where he was a regular from 2001 (Wikipedia).
- Style: wide-ranging, from photorealism to cute mascots; Masanao Amano says he turns everyday situations into surreal, mysterious worlds; he is influenced by 1980s New Wave manga (Wikipedia). Christianity is a recurring theme in his work, often in stories of adolescents struggling toward adulthood (Wikipedia, citing Sean Patrick Webb).
- Lychee Light Club is loosely based on a 1985 play by Tokyo Grand Guignol (Wikipedia); by high school Furuya had turned to a darker, more underground style (Wikipedia). Other recognition: Genkaku Picasso was nominated for the ALA's Great Graphic Novels for Teens (2012); his No Longer Human was a Japan Media Arts Festival jury selection (2011) (Wikipedia).

- Publisher: Ohta Publishing (Manga Erotics F). English: Vertical (now part of Kodansha), whose current page rates it 16+.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when children build a world without adults, ruled by beauty and a leader's will? The Light Club begins as a secret game (a chess club, TCJ) and becomes a small fascist state. The book asks where cruelty comes from, and whether a made thing can be more human than the people who made it.

**The answer the story gives.** Cruelty grows from fear: of aging, of ugliness, of betrayal, of becoming ordinary adults. The club's rituals, purges and worship of Zera end in mutual slaughter. Against this, a captive girl teaches a machine that being human means not killing, and the machine chooses to stop. Graphic Novel Reporter reads the book as saying that being human does not guarantee humanity.

**Characters as arguments.** Zera is the case for rule by beauty and will, and its collapse. Niko is blind loyalty, faithful to the end and destructive. Jaibo is love turned to obsession and betrayal. Tamiya remembers a gentler club and cannot get it back. Lychee is the open question: built as a tool, it becomes a person. Kanon is mercy and courage, and she alone walks out.

**Symbols and motifs.** The lychee fruit as fuel and as the club's name; light in a sooty town; the black-and-white vision Lychee has before it is told it is human (Experiments in Manga); roses at the planned execution; Elagabalus, the decadent boy emperor; German as the club's language; the stage frame of Tokyo Grand Guignol.

**Traditions it echoes.** Lord of the Flies (Vertical's own comparison, per Manga Bookshelf), Frankenstein (Otaku USA; MangaUpdates tag), Beauty and the Beast and Pinocchio (The Japan Times on the film, via Wikipedia), Suehiro Maruo's ero-guro art (Otaku USA), and Furuya's recurring Christian themes.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarianism and might makes right are the satire's targets and are shown failing. Nihilism and hedonistic aestheticism set the book's tone for most of its length. Pacifism and a Christian-tinged mercy carry the moral, late, through Kanon and Lychee. Transhumanist questions about what makes a person are left open.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-2: the clubhouse, the murder of a teacher and a trespasser, the blood pact. Ch. 3-4: Kanon's capture and the abuse; Tamiya's cruel choice. Middle chapters: Lychee's growing humanity and the club's fracturing. Final chapter: the planned execution, Kanon's escape, Lychee's refusal, Zera's death (TV Tropes; chapter numbers to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. The Light Club starts as a secret club for friends. What changes it?
2. Why do the members obey Zera even when he is cruel?
3. Lychee is told it is human. What makes it human in the end?
4. What does Kanon teach Lychee, and why does it matter that she is a prisoner when she teaches it?
5. Can a story about terrible things help readers understand them, or does showing them do harm? Where would your family draw the line?
