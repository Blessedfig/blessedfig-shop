**Premise.** Makoto Kowata, a polite, easily lost witch in training (about 15; from general knowledge; to verify), leaves her parents in Yokohama with her black cat familiar Chito to live with second cousins on a farm outside Hirosaki, Aomori, as part of her training, and enrolls at the local high school (Wikipedia; Kodansha blurb). Her cousin Chinatsu (about ten, per The Manga Critic) is wary until Makoto flies her home on a new broom; Chinatsu later becomes the apprentice of Makoto's globe-trotting older sister Akane. Episodes are small: a screaming mandrake pulled from a field, a courtesy visit from the black-clad Harbinger of Spring, summoning crows with a hair, Inukai the fortune-teller witch stuck with a dog's face after eating Akane's chocolates during a night of heavy drinking, a café run by witches with a shy ghost waitress from the Meiji era, a sky whale, cat spirits at a Yokohama temple, and later Makoto learning she is a rare "darkness" (creation) witch (v8) and a contest over where to hold the witches' Great Sabbath festival (v14) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; Penguin Random House).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if magic were ordinary? Flying Witch puts a witch in a farmhouse in Aomori and asks what changes. Very little, it turns out, except that everyone starts noticing the world more closely.

**The answer the story gives.** Wonder is a way of paying attention. Makoto's magic mostly involves plants, weather, animals and courtesy; the spirits she meets are neighbors to be greeted, thanked and given gifts. ANN's line on the jacket says it plainly: real magic is nice, but there is magic in everything.

**Characters as arguments.** Makoto argues for patience and politeness; Akane for freedom and travel; Chinatsu for a child's delight and the wish to learn; Kei for calm acceptance. No character argues against magic; the only friction is surprise.

**Symbols and motifs.** The broom bought at a supermarket. The mandrake pulled from a roadside field. The Harbinger of Spring, dressed in black but bringing flowers. Chito the cat, older than the children. The Sabbath festival (v14) as a witches' community gathering.

**Traditions it echoes.** Kiki's Delivery Service and the European witch tale made gentle; Japanese iyashikei; Shinto courtesy to the spirits of places and seasons. For Christian, Jewish and Muslim readers it raises the old question of how far fantasy magic differs from the practices their scriptures forbid.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan and occult practice is affirmed as kind craft; environmental care and community are affirmed; nothing is critiqued.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-21, the anime) settle Makoto in and introduce Akane, Inukai, Anzu and the café. Later volumes widen the witches' world: Chinatsu's apprenticeship and water magic, Makoto's rare darkness/creation type (v8), spirit visitors (ch. 35-36, 63) and the Great Sabbath contest (v14). The tone stays the same.

**Discussion questions.**
1. What everyday things in the book feel magical even without spells?
2. The Harbinger of Spring scares Chinatsu but brings flowers. Why does the book make spirits polite neighbors?
3. Makoto prefers potions to spells. What does that say about her?
4. Chinatsu wants to become a witch. How would our family think about that, and why?
5. Where do you see "everyday magic" in our town or home?
