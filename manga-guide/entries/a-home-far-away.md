**Premise.** In the American winter of 1990, Alan, a sickly young man of 17 (StoryGraph review), lives with intensely devout parents who pray constantly, blame every trouble on sin and the devil, and tell him his chronic illness is punishment for a sin he committed; he half-believes that prayer and repentance will heal him. As a child he was raped by a priest, which has broken his trust in God and in his parents (Boys Love Library). One night, staying out past curfew, he sits on a restaurant's steps; Hayden, a cook there and a drifter with a violent family past (his mother died of an overdose after killing his father, per the earlier quick read; to verify), takes him in after a nosebleed. Their nightly talks become love; when Hayden is due to move on, Alan confesses and the two run away together. The road trip is shadowed by Alan's worsening illness, crime and the law. In a chapel Alan shoots through a crucifix (MyAnimeList review), and the story ends with both young men dying at the sea, which one reviewer reads as baptismal (MyAnimeList) and another as the only "happy" ending open to them (Boys Love Library). An end-of-book note states that the characters were of legal age (StoryGraph review).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Where is home for someone whose home is where the harm came from? Alan has a house, parents and a church, and none of them is a home: his parents call his illness a punishment and a priest abused him. The title answers in advance: the home he wants is "far away."

**The answer the story gives.** Home is a person. Hayden, who has no home either, gives Alan shelter, then love, then the road. The story gives that love its full sympathy and gives it no future: illness, crime and the law close in, and the two die together at the sea. Boys Love Library reads this as the gentlest ending available to them; a StoryGraph reader reads it as a familiar tragic shortcut for gay characters.

**Characters as arguments.** The parents argue that suffering is deserved; the story answers that this belief is itself a cruelty. The priest is the case of authority turned predator. Hayden argues for unconditional acceptance. Alan is the argument's ground: a young man who still half-believes that his prayers could heal him, until he shoots through the crucifix (MyAnimeList).

**Symbols and motifs.** Winter and the road; nosebleeds as the body's constant reminder; the chapel and the crucifix; the sea, which one reviewer reads as a baptism that washes away the guilt Alan was taught (MyAnimeList).

**Traditions it echoes.** The lovers-on-the-run road story, the boys' love tragedy, and the American story of the abused child of a religious home. For Christian readers it also echoes John 9, where Jesus rejects the idea that a man's illness is punishment for sin, the very idea that crushes Alan.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Christianity is critiqued through its abusers; no believer is shown living it well in sources opened. Expressive individualism and the existential choice of love are affirmed. Nihilism hovers over the ending but the story is not cynical about love itself.

**Shifts across the series.** Likely ch. 1-2: the home, the meeting and the nightly talks. Likely ch. 3-4: the confession and the escape. Likely ch. 5-7: the road, crime, the chapel and the sea (order from Boys Love Library; chapter numbers not confirmed).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Alan's parents believed his illness was a punishment. What does your family believe about why people get sick?
2. What could an adult have done to help Alan before he ran away?
3. Why does Alan shoot the crucifix? Is he angry at God or at the people who used God's name?
4. Is the ending a happy one, a sad one, or both? Why do some readers object to it?
5. Where would you tell a friend in Alan's place to go for help?
