**Premise.** In 1938, fifty years after Part 1, Jonathan's grandson Joseph Joestar lives in New York with his grandmother Erina. He is brash, funny and a natural Hamon (Ripple) user who fights by tricks, often predicting his enemy's next line. In the first chapter he saves Smokey Brown, a young Black pickpocket, from two corrupt policemen who beat and try to extort him (ch. 1, jojowiki). A Hamon master, Straizo, uses a stone mask to become a vampire; Joseph defeats him on the Brooklyn Bridge and Straizo kills himself with his own Ripple (ch. 7-8). Learning that Nazi soldiers in Mexico hold the elderly Speedwagon, Joseph goes there and, with the eventual help of the SS officer Rudol von Stroheim, defeats Santana, an ancient "Pillar Man" the Nazis had revived for study on Hitler's orders (ch. 9-17). The Pillar Men are an ancient race that fed on humans; humans once "regarded them as either gods to be worshiped or demons to be feared," and their leader Kars invented the stone mask (jojowiki, Pillar Men). In Rome, Joseph meets Caesar Zeppeli, grandson of Part 1's Will Zeppeli (ch. 18). Three more Pillar Men, Wamuu, Esidisi and Kars, wake under Rome and plant poisoned rings in Joseph, giving him 33 days. Joseph and Caesar train under the Hamon master Lisa Lisa in Venice. Esidisi dies; Caesar dies fighting Wamuu and leaves Joseph the antidote (ch. 48); Joseph defeats Wamuu in a chariot duel (ch. 50-60). Kars gains the Red Stone of Aja and becomes the "Ultimate Thing," but a volcanic eruption throws him into space, where he freezes and "eventually stops thinking" (ch. 65-69). In the epilogue Joseph turns up alive at his own funeral, missing a hand, having married Lisa Lisa's assistant Suzi Q; Lisa Lisa is revealed as his mother; Stroheim dies at Stalingrad in 1943; Smokey becomes "the first black mayor" of his Georgia hometown (ch. 69, jojowiki).

## Life message

**A good life is lived with wit, courage and loyalty to friends and family, carrying on what those before you gave. Cleverness and humor can beat raw power, and a friend's sacrifice is honored by finishing the fight.**

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, most frames see a familiar adventure moral: friends and family overcome a pride that would make itself a god. Three lines divide them. First, the Nazi officer: Jewish and progressive frames weigh most heavily a story that shows Stroheim's cruelty and then makes him a hero, while leaving out the regime's crimes; reviewers from ANN, Anime Feminist and ComicsAlliance raise this, and TV Tropes records fans split on him; others read Stroheim as a period-adventure foil. Second, the supernatural: beings once worshipped as gods, a prophecy and vampire masks weigh most for Pentecostal, Adventist and Witness families. Third, the treatment of ethnic characters and women weighs for Wiccan/Pagan, progressive and Reform frames, while traditionalist frames focus on lineage and also note the hero's comic peeping.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987; Battle Tendency is Part 2 (Wikipedia).
- On Joseph (JoJonium commentary, 2014, via jojowiki's transcript): "he has the personality of a swindler," made to contrast with "the gentlemanly Jonathan" and because "I wanted the focus to shift from the physical battles of Part 1 to more cerebral confrontations."
- On Caesar (same): added because "Weekly Shonen Jump is a shonen magazine after all, so I wanted to include something that portrayed friendship in a positive light." He likes weapons "with faults" covered by strategy. Caesar's pose was meant "to convey a crucifixion--in other words, his future," the Zeppeli fate "to forge a path for the Joestars."
- On Lisa Lisa and women (same): shonen heroines then were "the stereotype of 'a man's ideal girl'," so a "sadistic" warrior teacher "felt fresh"; she was inspired by an intimidating female tutor from his childhood; supernatural powers let "a woman fight on even footing with a strong male opponent," the idea that led to Part 3's Stands.
- On Kars and the Pillar Men (same): taken "up a notch to the level of gods," designed from Roman statues, Egyptian sphinxes and Nio statues; Kars's quest "to become the epitome of biological evolution"; he never meant Kars to return, and his fate "may be the harshest punishment possible."
- On his outlook in this period (bunkoban vol. 4 afterword, 2002, via jojowiki): "both righteousness and darkness are part of a true 'ode to humanity,' and I decided to depict them both."
- Weekly author's notes for every Part 2 chapter (jojowiki archive) concern work, travel, music and films; none mentions the Nazis, Stroheim, Mexico or race. One asks readers for a tape of the TV series *Roots* (note for ch. 21).
- On the Nazis and Stroheim: no statement by Araki was found in his author's notes, the JoJonium commentary or the bunkoban afterword. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** for this point.
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found.
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media publishes the English edition; Viz is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). The 2012 anime is by David Production (Wikipedia); it renames the Nazis "German soldiers" and censors the salute (jojowiki). The English release of the game *Eyes of Heaven* removed Stroheim's Nazi uniform (jojowiki).
- Controversies: no single controversy, but the Nazi characters are a standing criticism. A 2023 JTA article, "Does anime have a Nazi problem? Some Jewish fans think so," names "the Nazi characters in 'Hellsing' and 'Jojo's Bizarre Adventure'" among its examples. Anime Feminist's "My Fave is Problematic" (2023) and ANN's vol. 4 review make the same point.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What beats overwhelming power? The Pillar Men are older, stronger and smarter than humans, and see them as food (jojowiki). Araki says he raised them "to the level of gods." Joseph has none of their strength. The question is whether wit, training and other people can make up the difference.

**The answer the story gives.** They can. Araki built Joseph as a "swindler" for "more cerebral confrontations," and said he prefers weapons "with faults" so that strategy must fill the gap (JoJonium commentary). Joseph traps, bluffs, cheats at cards and taunts. But he does not win alone. He lives because Caesar leaves him the antidote as he dies (ch. 48), because Lisa Lisa trains him (ch. 27-32), and because Smokey, Speedwagon and even a Nazi officer come to his aid (ch. 62, 67). Araki says he added Caesar to portray "friendship in a positive light." The Japanese subtitle names the theme: "His Proud Lineage."

**Characters as arguments.** Joseph argues for cleverness and humor over solemn virtue; he is the opposite of his grandfather. Caesar argues for honor and legacy: he carries his grandfather's name into a fight he cannot win, and Araki drew his last pose as a crucifixion, the Zeppeli fate "to forge a path for the Joestars." Lisa Lisa argues for discipline and, once revealed as Joseph's mother, for a love that hid itself; critics note she is put out of the final fight early. Wamuu argues that an enemy can have honor: he spares Caesar's last gift, and Joseph gives him his blood as he dies (ch. 60). Kars argues for the will to perfect oneself at any cost, and the story answers by giving him a perfect body in a place where it is useless, "the harshest punishment possible" in Araki's words. Stroheim is the hardest to read. His first scenes show cruelty: he threatens a servant's tongue with a razor (ch. 8) and orders captives killed while sparing one brave boy because "only the great should live" (ch. 9). Then his courage wins Joseph's respect, and he returns as a cyborg proud to be "above every other human being" and later rescues the heroes. The story never argues for Nazism, but it never weighs it either; ComicsAlliance calls the result "morally empty," and a Jewish critic at Anime Feminist objects to a Nazi shown as "just a regular, friendly guy." Others, such as TV Tropes editors, read him as "A Lighter Shade of Black."

**Symbols and motifs.** Breath and sunlight again: Hamon is life made into a weapon. The poisoned rings and the 33-day countdown: life on borrowed time. Caesar's bubbles, which Araki chose because they pop easily, "a perfect representation of his fate," and his last bubble of blood holding the antidote and his headband (ch. 48). The Red Stone of Aja, a jewel that turns ambition into godhood. Kars drifting in space, unable to die, "stops thinking": immortality as punishment. The story itself explains the swastika as an old symbol of "destiny and life" bent to Hitler's use (ch. 21).

**Traditions it echoes.** The 1930s pulp and adventure film: Nazis chasing ancient relics in Latin America and Europe; ComicsAlliance notes "an Indiana Jones vibe." The trickster of folklore, who beats giants by cunning (my own framing). The Faustian or Promethean overreach of a being who wants to be more than he is. Kung fu training films, with a demanding master. Aztec myth, where gods needed human blood; a 2022 essay in JOJO magazine by novelist Kiwamu Sato reads the Pillar Men against Aztec sculpture and ritual. Victorian melodrama, which ANN sees in the lost-mother reveal.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics, pragmatism and community are affirmed: honor, cleverness and friendship win. Might makes right and the drive to transcend limits are critiqued through Kars, and in passing through Stroheim's "only the great should live." Religion is mostly backdrop: beings once called gods, a prophecy, a crucifixion pose. Politics enters through the 1938 setting, but the Nazis are a plot engine rather than a subject.

**Shifts across the series.** Chapters 1-18 (Viz vol. 1): New York, Smokey and the corrupt police, Straizo, and the Nazi lab in Mexico with Santana. Chapters 19-36 (vol. 2): Rome, Caesar, the three Pillar Men, the poison rings, Lisa Lisa's training and Esidisi. Chapters 37-49 (vol. 3): the bath scene and Suzi Q's possession, Stroheim's cyborg return in Switzerland, and Caesar's death. Chapters 50-69 (vol. 4): Wamuu's chariot duel, Kars's betrayal of Lisa Lisa, the Nazi rescue, Kars's transformation, the volcano and the epilogue. The tone moves from comedy toward grief at Caesar's death and back to a light epilogue that jumps fifty years.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Joseph wins by lying, bluffing and tricks. Is that different from cheating? When is it fair?
2. Caesar dies to give Joseph a chance (ch. 48). What do we owe to people who sacrifice for us?
3. Stroheim orders captives killed in his first chapters, then becomes a hero who saves Joseph. Does one act of courage cancel what came before? What does the story leave out about the regime he served?
4. Kars gets the perfect body he wanted and ends unable to die (ch. 69). What is the story saying about wanting to be perfect?
5. Joseph defends Smokey against police and a racist in the first chapters, yet reviewers say Smokey and others are drawn as caricatures. Can a story mean well and still draw people unfairly?
