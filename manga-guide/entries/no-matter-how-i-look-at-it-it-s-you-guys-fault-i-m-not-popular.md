**Premise.** Tomoko Kuroki, 15, has spent middle school playing dating sims and is sure she will be popular in high school. Instead she cannot speak to classmates, eats lunch alone and schemes from the sidelines, while her inner voice judges everyone, herself included. Her only friend is Yuu Naruse from middle school; at home she pesters her younger brother Tomoki, who finds her embarrassing (ch. 1-38, Wikipedia; fan wiki). From her second year the cast widens: the quiet Yuri Tamura, the delinquent-looking Masaki Yoshida, the baseball-loving rival Kotomi Komiyama, the gyaru Asuka Katou, Emiri Uchi, the kind former student-council president Megumi Imae and many more, especially after the Kyoto field trip (ch. 71-81). In her third year Tomoko has a crowded circle of friends, plans to study literature at Aoyama Gakuin University to become a novelist, and leads her class's film for the culture festival (ch. 192-236) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; ANN 2023).

## Life message

**Even a lonely, self-sabotaging person can grow into real friendship, one awkward step at a time; other people are kinder than the voice in your head says.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if the person keeping you lonely is you? Tomoko blames everyone else (the title says so) and has a rulebook from dating sims. Every chapter title begins "Because I'm not popular," and every early plan collapses.

**The answer the story gives.** Loneliness ends not when Tomoko becomes someone else but when other people decide to stay. The Kyoto field trip (ch. 71-81) puts her in a group by accident; Yuri keeps inviting her to lunch; Kotomi becomes a rival she cannot shake; by her third year she has more plans than time (ANN 2023). She is still awkward, still crude in her head, but no longer alone.

**Characters as arguments.** Tomoko argues that the world is unfair to the unpopular, and the story answers that she has been unfair to the world too. Yuu argues for loyalty through change. Tomoki argues for ordinary responsibility, and Tomoko's tearful apology to him (ch. 35) is one of her first real steps. Kii, the cousin, is a mirror: her admiration turns to pity and back to respect (Wikipedia; ANN 2023).

**Symbols and motifs.** The "Fail" chapter titles. Tomoko's tired eyes. Phones and games as walls. The class film for the festival (v20 onward), which turns her otaku imagination into something shared.

**Traditions it echoes.** The cringe comedy of Welcome to the NHK and Genshiken (Theron Martin's comparison, via Wikipedia) and the later "socially anxious girl" stories it helped inspire (ANN 2023). For faith readers it can echo teaching on rash judgment and on the neighbor: Tomoko's inner voice is a long study in judging others badly, and the plot keeps proving her wrong.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism wins: belonging is the cure. Expressive individualism is affirmed in that friends accept her as she is. Postmodern parody targets the fantasies otaku culture sells. No religious or political lens is argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Year 1 (ch. 1-38, v1-5) is almost pure cringe comedy, the source of the 2013 anime and of the crudest jokes. Year 2 (ch. 39-121, v5-12) builds the cast and turns at the Kyoto trip. Year 3 (ch. 122 on) is an ensemble story about friendship, college plans and the culture festival, with more romantic undertones and a "tamer" tone (The Outer Haven, vol. 20).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tomoko blames "you guys" in the title. Whose fault was her loneliness?
2. Which of Tomoko's plans worked, and which friendships came by accident?
3. Tomoko's inner voice is often unkind. Is it ever right about anyone?
4. Some reviewers thought the early humor laughed at a girl with anxiety. Did you laugh with her or at her?
5. Who is the friend who changed Tomoko most, and what did that friend do?
