**Premise.** Shun Hashimoto, a gay aspiring novelist from Hokkaido, came out to his family at his own arranged wedding to his childhood friend Sakurako; his parents disowned him, and he moved to his aunt's (or grandmother's) home on a small island off Okinawa (Wikipedia; ANN; Seven Seas). There he notices Mio Chibana, a quiet high school student who sits by the sea every night after his mother's death; Mio lives with relatives who want him to "get over" his grief (ANN). Shun's first approach offends Mio, but they become close until Mio is sent to a group home on the mainland (ch. 1). Three years later Mio, now 20, returns and tells Shun he loves him, even though Shun is a man. Shun, 27, holds back, guilty that he would rob Mio of a "normal" life. When Sakurako arrives to bring Shun home because his father is ill, Mio urges him to go and reconcile, and Shun asks Mio to come with him (Wikipedia). The sequel follows them to Hokkaido and Shun's family.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can someone who was rejected for who he is believe that he deserves to be loved? Shun was disowned for being gay; years later, when a young man he cares about says "I love you," he cannot say yes.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, but only by facing his shame. Mio's patient, plain love is what breaks it down, and the story's last move is not escape but return: Mio urges Shun to go home to his ill father, and Shun asks Mio to come with him (Wikipedia). Love here is meant to heal old wounds, not just start a new life.

**Characters as arguments.** Shun argues, against himself, that he would ruin Mio's chance at a "normal" life; the story treats this as the voice of internalized stigma (ANN). Mio argues that love is simple, that he loves Shun as a person. Sakurako argues for loyalty and family duty, and helps bring Shun home. Eri and Suzu show a same-sex couple living openly.

**Symbols and motifs.** The sea and the bench where Mio sat in grief; the stranger (étranger) on the shore, an outsider in a small community; the island as exile and refuge; the flight north to Hokkaido as return.

**Traditions it echoes.** The quiet slice-of-life BL romance; the homecoming story and, for some readers, the parable of the prodigal son told from the son's side. Kono Manga ga Sugoi! praised its film-like camera work, which reflects Kii's background in animation (Wikipedia).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and an affirming progressive view are the story's center. Traditional family duty is critiqued where it demands self-denial (the arranged marriage) and honored where it means care (the father's illness). There is no religious voice.

**Shifts across the series.** This volume (4 chapters) covers the first meeting, the three-year gap and the return. The sequel, Harukaze no Étranger (6 volumes), moves to Hokkaido, where Shun and Mio live with his parents and adopted younger brother, who struggle to accept them; Shun's first novel is labeled "gay" by the media (Wikipedia).
