**Premise.** Akira Fudo is a gentle, timid high-school student living with the family of his friend Miki Makimura while his parents work abroad. His childhood friend Ryo Asuka tells him that demons who once ruled the Earth are waking to take it back, and that the only way to fight them is to take their power. At a drug-fueled nightclub party staged as a "Black Sabbath," Ryo provokes the demons to possess the revelers; Akira merges with the demon warrior Amon, but his pure heart keeps control, and he becomes Devilman (early chapters; Wikipedia; Thompson). He fights demons hidden in society, including Sirene, who loved Amon, and Jinmen, who carries his living victims' faces on his shell (v1-2; fan wiki). When the demons attack openly, Ryo learns that he is Satan, a fallen angel who sided with the demons against God, and on television he exposes Devilmen without telling them apart from demons. Mass hysteria follows: governments torture and kill suspects, and a mob of neighbors kills Miki, her brother and their friend (v5). Akira loses faith in humanity and leads the surviving Devilmen against Satan's army. Twenty years later humanity is extinct; Satan kills Akira, realizes he has done to humanity what God did to the demons, and weeps beside his friend's body as God's angels descend (ch. 53; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Fear and hatred of the other can make people more monstrous than demons; a loving heart is what makes someone human, and war, any war, has no justice.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A war of species for the Earth: Thompson links the demons to a Darwinian struggle for survival. Demons absorb or merge with whatever they meet; humans turn on their own kind once they feel threatened (v5). |
| Pragmatic | Ryo's method is ruthless efficiency: he shoots revelers to trigger possession (Thompson; TCJ) and later breaks humanity with one broadcast. Every practical measure the humans take (the Demon Busters, mass arrests) makes things worse. |
| Religious | Built on inverted Christian mythology: Satan is the fallen angel who loved the demons and Akira, and Nagai said he saw God as the more fitting villain because of what God did to the demons (Wikipedia). The ending brings God's angels to a dead world. Mania's Thomas Zoth noted its "casual blasphemy" (via Wikipedia). |
| Socio-cultural | Nagai designed it as an anti-war work: merging with demons stands for the draft, and Miki's death for the death of peace (Wikipedia). Manga Design compares the turn of humans on humans to witch-hunting (via Wikipedia); the mob calls Miki a witch (fan wiki). |

Outlook: Bleak — Miki and her family are murdered by their neighbors, humanity is wiped out, Akira dies, and Satan grieves alone as the angels descend (ch. 53); love survives only as a memory and as Satan's too-late regret.
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: Akira's love for Miki and Satan's love for Akira are real but cannot stop the end, and Nagai frames the whole as a warning (Wikipedia); love between two (L) is the only light, and it comes too late.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every tradition recognizes the force of the mob scenes and the anti-war warning. The divide is theological: most Christian, Muslim and Latter-day Saint frames grade the message D or F because Satan is made sympathetic, God is (in the author's words) the villain, and the hero's strength comes from a demon. Anglican, mainline, Reform Jewish and Buddhist frames read it as a dark parable worth discussing with older readers (C). Secular, Unitarian Universalist, Satanist, Atheist and Agnostic frames find its critique of fear and scapegoating strongest (B), while all note its despair. Jewish frames also note the demon-banker in the young-Hitler chapter of Seven Seas vol. 1.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

No one wins. Akira, Miki, the Devilmen and nearly all of humanity die; Satan wins his war and grieves it. The story's sympathy goes to those who love (Akira, Miki, the demons Sirene and Kaim), and its harshest judgment falls on the human mob.

## Creator and publisher context

- Go Nagai, born September 6, 1945, in Wajima, Ishikawa; lost his father young; began as an assistant to Shotaro Ishinomori in 1965 and debuted in 1967. As a child he was struck by Gustave Doré's illustrations for the Divine Comedy (Wikipedia, Go Nagai).
- Harenchi Gakuen (1968) brought open eroticism into boys' manga and drew heavy public criticism; Nagai is known for pushing limits on sex and violence (Wikipedia, Go Nagai).
- On record (Wikipedia): Devilman grew out of his earlier Demon Lord Dante when Toei asked for a more human antihero; he made the manga darker than the anime for older readers; he designed it as an anti-war work, writing that there is no justice in any war; he said Satan is not a stereotypical villain and that God fits that role better because of what he did to the demons. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (his own afterwords and interviews not opened).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Shonen Magazine, a boys' magazine; Gaffney notes its readers were teens). English: Seven Seas (2018), translated by Zack Davisson and Adrienne Beck. Controversy: PTA and other groups protested its violence in the 1970s (Wikipedia). No controversies involving the creator personally were found beyond reactions to his work.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone human: a human body, or a human heart? Akira gains a demon's body and keeps a loving heart; the ordinary people around him keep their bodies and lose their hearts.

**The answer the story gives.** Love makes us human, and fear can unmake us. When Ryo's broadcast spreads panic, neighbors murder a family they have known for years (v5). Nagai wrote that there is no justice in any war (Wikipedia); the story ends with everyone dead and Satan grieving the friend he killed (ch. 53).

**Characters as arguments.** Akira argues for love and loyalty, then for revenge. Ryo/Satan argues that humans are the true monsters, and then learns he is one too. Miki argues for trust; she is killed for it. Sirene and Kaim, demons who love each other, argue that monsters can be faithful (Wikipedia). The mob argues nothing; it only fears.

**Symbols and motifs.** The demon mask that shows Akira the ancient past; Jinmen's shell of living faces; the television broadcast; the word "witch"; the angels' light over a dead world.

**Traditions it echoes.** Milton's Paradise Lost and Dante (through Doré), the war in heaven, the witch-hunts of Europe, and Japanese wartime memory. Critics see its influence in Evangelion and CLAMP's X (Wikipedia). Many Christian readers will see in it a retelling of the fall that takes Satan's side; others read it as a warning that uses that myth to condemn human cruelty.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism has the last word. Christian myth is used and inverted. The anti-war conviction is clear in intent but expressed through catastrophe rather than through anyone choosing peace. Might makes right is shown winning and inheriting nothing.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-2: the Sabbath, Sirene, Jinmen and demon-of-the-week horror, with a lot of nudity. The middle volumes widen to open war and the government crackdown. Vol. 5: the reveal of Satan, the broadcast, Miki's death, the twenty-year jump and the end (Wikipedia). Seven Seas vol. 1 also includes later time-travel chapters from Shin Devilman (Gaffney).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Akira has a demon's body and a human heart. The mob has human bodies. Who is more human, and why?
2. How does one television broadcast turn neighbors into killers? Could that happen with news or rumors today?
3. Satan realizes at the end that he did what he blamed God for. What does that say about revenge?
4. Nagai said there is no justice in any war. Does the story prove that, or only show it?
5. Many traditions describe God and Satan very differently from this story. What would your family's tradition say back to it?
