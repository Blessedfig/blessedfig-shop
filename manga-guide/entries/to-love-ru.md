**Premise.** Rito Yuuki, 15, is a shy, kind high-school boy who cannot confess to his crush, classmate Haruna Sairenji. One night a naked girl with a devil's tail appears in his bath: Lala, runaway crown princess of the planet Deviluke, which rules much of the galaxy (ch. 1). When Rito shouts that marriage is only for the person you love, Lala and her bodyguard Zastin take it as a proposal; Lala falls in love with him, and her father, King Gid, agrees that Rito may marry her if he protects her from rival suitors, or he will destroy the Earth (fan wiki). Rito must fend off alien suitors (Ghi Bree, ch. 7; Lacospo, who hires the assassin Golden Darkness, ch. 35-38) while more girls enter his life: the strict class officer Yui Kotegawa, the queen bee Saki Tenjouin, the sex-switching alien Run/Ren, the school nurse Mikado, the ghost Oshizu, and Lala's twin sisters Momo and Nana (ch. 108). His sister Mikan, 12, keeps house because their parents are away (Wikipedia). Nearly every chapter turns on accidental nudity and groping. In the last chapter Rito tells Lala he loves her and that he has long loved Haruna; Lala, delighted, tells him to confess to Haruna and marry both of them, and the series ends without his choosing (ch. 162).

## Creator and publisher context

- Saki Hasemi (story) worked in video games in the 1980s-1990s and wrote anime scripts (Black Cat, Powerpuff Girls Z; series composition on Moetan and To Love Ru) before this, his first manga; later wrote GT-Girl (2020) (Wikipedia, Saki Hasemi).
- Kentaro Yabuki (art) learned manga from Dragon Ball, assisted Takeshi Obata on Hikaru no Go, created Black Cat (12 million copies), and later Ayakashi Triangle (2020-) and the Darling in the Franxx manga (Wikipedia, Kentaro Yabuki).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, Production, from volume interviews): Yabuki wanted a love triangle and insisted Rito not be a pervert; once Peke became an undressing robot the direction was set; lewd scenes became the focus and each chapter tested Jump's limits; a reader survey asked for more eroticism; the editor called each week a battle with the editorial department; Yabuki said by mid-series simple touching was no longer enough for readers. The ending without a choice was Yabuki's idea. Darkness ended partly because authors and readers had grown desensitized to the sexiness.
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Shōnen Jump). English: Seven Seas, Ghost Ship imprint, rated Older Teen.
- Controversies concern the sequel Darkness (Tokyo Bill 156 review, Fukushima harmful designation, Australian refusal of classification) (Wikipedia). No controversies about the creators personally were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can kindness carry a love story when everything else is chaos? Rito is shy, clumsy and decent. Aliens, assassins and suitors crash into his life, along with a weekly run of accidents. The series asks, lightly, what makes someone worth loving.

**The answer the story gives.** Sincerity and kindness. Rito's first speech, that marriage is only for the one you love, makes Lala love him (ch. 1); his taiyaki for a hungry stranger begins the assassin's change (ch. 35); his last speech is an honest confession (ch. 162). Love, in the end, is plural and unresolved: Lala offers to share him (ch. 162). Loveridge (ANN) finds it "earnestly pure underneath its perviness."

**Characters as arguments.** Rito argues for gentleness over strength. Lala argues for love that is free, cheerful and unpossessive. Haruna stands for shy, steady affection. Yui argues for decency and rules, and the story mocks her even as it softens toward her. The suitors argue for possession by force and lose. Golden Darkness shows that a weapon can become a person. The principal is lechery made a clown.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bathtub where it all starts. Peke, the dress that comes undone. Lala's D-Dial of inventions. Taiyaki. The pool where the series ends.

**Traditions it echoes.** Rumiko Takahashi's Urusei Yatsura (alien princess moves in with a hapless boy), Ranma 1/2, Tenchi Muyo! and Love Hina, as reviewers note (ANN, Price; THEM via Wikipedia); Jump's romantic-comedy line.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism wins in form: the fan service is the draw, by the creators' own account. Communitarianism and expressive individualism are affirmed in Rito's found family and Lala's free choice. Might makes right loses every time.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4 (ch. 1-34): Lala's arrival, suitors and school chaos. Vols. 5-9 (ch. 35-79): Golden Darkness, Yui and a bigger cast; the creators say the fan service grew more daring mid-series. Vols. 10-16 (ch. 80-142): Momo and Nana arrive (ch. 108), alien adventures (ch. 125-132). Vols. 17-18 (ch. 143-162): toward confessions, ending at the pool (ch. 162). Darkness continues it, more explicitly.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rito never means to do the things that happen to him. Does that make them all right?
2. Why does Lala fall in love with Rito after his speech about marriage?
3. Golden Darkness came to kill Rito. What changed her mind?
4. Yui is mocked for wanting rules about decency. Is she wrong?
5. The story ends with Lala suggesting Rito marry two girls. What do you think of that ending?
