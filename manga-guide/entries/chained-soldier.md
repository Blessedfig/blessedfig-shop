**Premise.** Gates to Mato, a dangerous other dimension, opened across Japan. Mato holds monsters called Shuuki and magical Peaches that give superpowers only to women. Women became the dominant sex, and an all-female Mato (Demon) Defense Force guards the gates. High school student Yuuki Wakura wanders into Mato and is saved by Kyouka Uzen, commander of the 7th Squad. Her power, "Slave," turns him into a transformed combat beast under her collar and chain. After each fight, she must give him a "reward," which for Yuuki is kissing and intimate physical contact. Yuuki takes a job as the squad's dorm caretaker, hoping to become a hero and find his missing sister. Kyouka seeks revenge on the one-horned Shuuki that destroyed her village. Later arcs bring in the Eight Thunder Gods who rule Mato, humanoid Shuuki who were once human, and a secret government bureau that experimented on them (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A good life is lived in service to your team and the people you love: be diligent, loyal and brave, protect the weak, and keep your promises. Physical pleasure is shown as a fair and fun payoff for hard work.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a person owed for their work, and who decides? Chained Soldier builds a world on a contract. A master gives a slave power; the slave fights; the master must pay. The question underneath is how power, service and desire fit together when one person's body is bound to another's will.

**The answer the story gives.** The story treats the bargain as fair and fun. Yuuki agrees to serve Kyouka (TV Tropes notes he "expressly agreed"), works hard, and receives what he "subconsciously wants" (Japanese Wikipedia). The master, in turn, cannot refuse: her body moves to pay the reward whether she wishes it or not. The story plays this double compulsion as comedy and fan service. Takahiro calls the rewards "very worth seeing" and "definitely necessary" (interview). Readers from many traditions will see an unresolved tension here: the story values Yuuki's selfless service, yet pays it in a currency that turns service into a transaction.

**Characters as arguments.** Yuuki is the argument for humble diligence: a boy with no powers who cooks, cleans and fights for others. Kyouka is duty driven by grief; her revenge on the one-horned Shuuki gives her purpose until it is done. Himari is pride tamed by closeness. Shushu and Tenka are desire spoken out loud. Nei, the 11-year-old scout, is the child the squad protects. Aoba, Yuuki's sister, is the argument that monsters may be victims: she ate a Peach to survive and now leads others like her (Wikipedia). The secret bureau is the argument against treating such people as specimens.

**Symbols and motifs.** The collar and chain stand for the bond: in this world, being bound makes you strong. Peaches recall the Kojiki, where peaches drive back the forces of the underworld (from general knowledge; to verify). The gates to Mato mark the line between the ordered human world and a dangerous one. Shangri-la, a space where time stretches, becomes a place of training and of repeated rewards (Yen Press vol. 14 blurb).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese underworld myth (Yomi, thunder deities). Shonen battle and team conventions. The harem and ecchi genre. A gender-reversed social premise that recalls speculative fiction about matriarchy, though reviewers say it is not developed.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism is affirmed as the reward for virtue. Communal loyalty and virtue ethics are affirmed through Yuuki and the squad. Might-makes-right is the world's rule rather than its moral. Feminist readers find a female-led world whose camera still serves male desire: Anime Feminist calls the premiere "a vehicle for an awkward, adolescent version of a dominatrix fantasy." State power over the vulnerable is critiqued.

**Shifts across the series.** Early chapters (about ch. 1-44, the first anime season) set up the reward system, the squad and the gender-reversed society. Humanoid Shuuki appear from ch. 6 (TV Tropes) and grow into a plot about Yuuki's sister and the bureau's experiments. Later arcs (the Azuma banquet, the Yokohama battle, joint training in Shangri-la) widen the cast of women who borrow Yuuki and raise the scale of the war with the Thunder Gods. The gender theme fades as the action takes over (TV Tropes; Anime UK News).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kyouka's body must give the reward even if she does not want to. What does that say about consent, for her and for Yuuki?
2. Yuuki is diligent and kind. Does the reward system change how you see his goodness?
3. Only women can use Peach powers, and men are second-class. Does the story say anything about that, or just use it as a setting?
4. Aoba and the humanoid Shuuki were once human. Who are the real monsters in the bureau storyline?
5. The creators say the revealing scenes are necessary. What does your family think a story needs, and what does it add?
