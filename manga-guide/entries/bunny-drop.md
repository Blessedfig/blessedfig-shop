**Premise.** Daikichi Kawachi, 30 and single, goes home for his grandfather Souichi's funeral and finds a silent six-year-old girl, Rin, said to be Souichi's illegitimate daughter. The relatives treat her as an embarrassment and plan to give her up; angered, Daikichi takes her in himself (vol. 1). He changes his life for her: quits smoking, transfers to a lower-pressure job, learns to cook, and leans on Yukari Nitani, the divorced mother of Rin's nursery-school friend Kouki (vols. 1-4). He tracks down Rin's mother, Masako, a manga artist who gave Rin up for her career; Rin refuses adoption and keeps her own surname (vol. 2). Part two jumps ten years: Daikichi is 40 and Rin 15. Rin turns down Kouki, whose middle-school girlfriend Akari harassed her by text and later fakes a pregnancy to get money from him (vols. 5-6); Nitani turns down Daikichi's proposal and marries someone else (vols. 6-8); Rin meets Masako (vol. 7). Rin realizes she is in love with Daikichi (vol. 8). In vol. 9 Kouki tells Daikichi; Masako tells Rin she is not Souichi's child and has no blood tie to Daikichi; Daikichi asks her to wait two years until she graduates, then agrees that they will marry, and Rin hopes to have his child. Vol. 10 is side stories, ending with Rin and Daikichi as a couple some months on (Wikipedia; Yen Press; Gaffney; Carlson).

## Life message

**Family is made by choosing to care for someone day after day, not by blood or by what relatives think proper; the series carries that idea to an ending in which the girl and the man who raised her choose to marry, which most readers rejected.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Kin obligation tested: blood relatives refuse a child; an unrelated-in-practice guardian invests years of care. The ending turns on a revealed lack of blood ties (vol. 9), as if kinship rules, not upbringing, decided what is allowed. Rin hopes to bear a child (vol. 9). |
| Pragmatic | Daikichi solves real problems: day care, work hours, school registration, illness, a sore back (vols. 1-7). Practical advice from Nitani and co-workers carries him. The ending is less practical: reviewers say no one tells a 16-year-old to wait and see life first (Carlson, vol. 9). |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot. A funeral, grave visits and a tree planted for a birth (vols. 1, 3) are family customs. Faith readers welcome the care for an unwanted child (the "fatherless," orphans) and reject the ending, in which a guardian accepts his ward as a future wife. |
| Socio-cultural | Japanese work culture versus fatherhood: Daikichi takes a demotion to raise Rin (vol. 1); single mothers (Nitani, Masako) and family "face" over an illegitimate child. Carlson links the ending to an old literary pattern of men raising girls to marry, going back to The Tale of Genji. |

Outlook: Hopeful — every main character ends settled and content: Rin and Daikichi together, Masako with a new family, Nitani remarried, Kouki moving on, though reviewers found the hopeful ending itself troubling.
Hope source: F — the way through is family built by daily care: Daikichi's choice to raise Rin is the series' heart, and the ending keeps the two of them together as a new kind of family; love between two (L) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame welcomes the first half: a man choosing to raise a child no one else wanted is praised as mercy, seva, chesed, care for the orphan, or plain decency. The divide is how much the ending weighs. Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and Sikh readings see the turn from father to husband as a breach of family order and land at C. Secular progressive readers weigh the power imbalance most heavily (D), since the story endorses it and no adult questions it. Shinto readers, for whom family order here is less a matter of ritual purity, read it a little more warmly. Every frame agrees that the anime and vols. 1-4 can be enjoyed on their own.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** Readers and the story split sharply. Critics loved part one (Aoki, Leigh and Carlson via Wikipedia; Carlson's best-of-2010 pick), and the anime is remembered as a gentle family story. The ending was received negatively as incestuous in spirit (Wikipedia, citing CBR), and many fans ignore everything after the anime's endpoint (CBR). Gaffney (vol. 8) found Kouki's growth the best character work and wished for Daikichi and Nitani to marry; Carlson called the twist "repellant". The anime's director admitted mixed feelings about it (ANN interview). The fan favorite is the Daikichi of vols. 1-4.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yumi Unita, manga artist; other works include Sukimasuki (Wikipedia) and Yoningurashi (Takeshobo), a family series about a couple raising two children (Gaffney, citing the vol. 10 interview).
- On-record intent: in an interview printed at the end of the English vol. 10, Unita says she planned the story's course from the start and wanted it to contrast with Yoningurashi (Gaffney, vol. 10 review; the interview itself was not opened). Wikipedia says some of Daikichi's child-raising experiences came from her own. She avoided Rin's inner monologue before the time skip and Daikichi's after it (Gaffney).
- Anime director Kanta Kamei told ANN (2012) that the team decided from the start to adapt only the first four volumes, because the manga was still running and the child and young-adult parts have very different themes and viewpoints; Kamei said a happy life together for the two would be wonderful but admitted mixed feelings about the ending (ANN interview, read through a fetch-tool summary; also cited by CBR).
- No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shodensha (Feel Young, a josei magazine for adult women). Yen Press published it in English, rated T (Teen).
- No controversies involving the creator were found; the controversy is about the ending.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone family? Rin's blood relatives will not take her; a man who barely knew she existed does. For four volumes the series answers that family is what you do: packing lunches, finding a day care, comforting a child who is afraid you will die.

**The answer the story gives.** Care makes family, and Daikichi's choice is the best thing in his life (vol. 4). Part two pushes the answer further: when Rin learns she is not related to Daikichi by blood, the story treats the years of parenting as the ground for a marriage (vol. 9). Most readers and critics think the story undid its own answer here (Gaffney; Carlson; ANN; CBR).

**Characters as arguments.** Daikichi argues that an ordinary, selfish-seeming man can become a good father by choosing to. Masako argues the opposite case, a mother who chose her work, and the story slowly forgives her (vol. 7). Nitani argues for quiet self-sacrifice: she turns Daikichi down so they can both move on (vol. 6). Kouki argues that people can grow out of their worst years. Rin, who rarely shows her thoughts, is the hardest to read; reviewers say her love is told rather than explained (Carlson; Gaffney).

**Symbols and motifs.** Trees: the loquat planted for Rin's school entry and the fragrant olive Souichi planted at her birth, dug up and moved to Daikichi's garden (vol. 3), are roots chosen and transplanted. The rindo (gentian) flower Rin loved at the funeral. The pinky swear. The title's "bunny," a small creature dropped into Daikichi's life.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese iyashikei (healing) slice-of-life story; the single-father comedy; Yotsuba&! (Carlson). The ending echoes the old pattern in Japanese literature of a man raising a girl to be his wife, from The Tale of Genji (Carlson), and the "not related by blood after all" turn of many romance manga (Gaffney).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and duty carry part one. Expressive individualism wins the ending: Rin's feelings override every role. Confucian ethics is honored in the care for elders and broken in the end. Traditionalism appears only as the relatives' coldness.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4 (ch. 1-24) are Daikichi's first year with six-year-old Rin, the part adapted by the anime and film. Vols. 5-8 jump ten years: Rin at 15-16, Kouki's past, Nitani's remarriage, Rin meeting her mother. Vol. 9 is the romance and its resolution. Vol. 10 returns to side stories from all periods and ends with the couple.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Daikichi take Rin when no one else would? What did it cost him?
2. Rin keeps her own surname (vol. 2). Why does that matter to her?
3. Nitani says no to Daikichi so they can both move on. Was she right?
4. Many fans stop at volume 4. If you read on, what do you think about how it ends, and who should have talked to Rin?
5. Is a family made by blood, by law, or by years of care?
