**Premise.** Mirai Kakehashi lost his parents and little brother in a car crash and has been worked and beaten by the aunt and uncle who took him in. On the day of his middle school graduation he jumps from a tower block and is caught by Nasse, an angel who gives him wings, red arrows (anyone hit falls in love with the shooter for 33 days) and white arrows (instant painless death). Nasse tells him his relatives arranged the crash; when Mirai tests it with a red arrow, his aunt confesses and stabs herself (ch. 1). God will retire in 999 days, and Mirai is one of thirteen candidates chosen by angels to replace him. Kanade Uryu, a rich student calling himself Metropoliman, sets out to kill every rival (v1-8). Mirai, his childhood friend Saki and Nanato Mukaido, a father dying of cancer, ally to win without killing. After Metropoliman's death a second arc (v9-14) brings a media contest among the remaining candidates, including 13-year-old Shuji Nakaumi, who helped his own family die, and Nobel laureate Gaku Yoneda, who argues on television that God is a human invention and should have no successor. Shuji becomes God; years later, after Mirai and Saki marry, he shoots himself with the last white arrow, and all life in the universe vanishes (ch. 55-58; Wikipedia; fan wiki; VIZ).

## Life message

**Happiness is found in ordinary life and in loving another person, not in power or in becoming God, and killing to win is wrong; but the story ends by asking whether life, meaning and God themselves can last, and lets them all end.**

The ending turns biology itself into the question: the beings who made God want to die and envy mortals (ch. 58). |

Outlook: Bleak — Mirai survives, chooses life and finds happiness with Saki, but the new God's suicide erases all life, and the final page shows the makers of God starting over because they want death (ch. 58).
Hope source: L — love between two: Mirai's way through is ordinary happiness with Saki, and they die together grateful for the six years they were given (ch. 57-58); friendship and family (F) are close behind.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes Mirai's refusal to kill and his choice of life after abuse. They divide over the theology and the ending. Theistic traditions (most Christian churches, Muslim, Sikh, Bahá'í, Jehovah's Witness, Orthodox Jewish) see a God who is made, replaced and destroyed as contrary to their teaching on God's eternity and sovereignty, and weigh the recurring suicide as grave (D or F). Liberal and secular frames read it as a serious, if bleak, philosophical thriller (C), and Agnostic readers welcome its open debate of worldviews (B), while Nothing in particular families, many of whom believe in God and an afterlife, are troubled by its nihilistic close.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who killed for power (Metropoliman, Misurin) or for cruelty die, and those who refuse to kill (Mirai, Saki) find ordinary happiness; but the boy who believed in helping people die becomes God and ends everything, so no side truly wins.

## Creator and publisher context

- Tsugumi Ohba is a pen name; the writer's identity is not public. Ohba wrote Death Note (2003-2006) and Bakuman (2008-2012) with Takeshi Obata and has said he draws more on films (Kurosawa, Chaplin) than on books (Wikipedia, Tsugumi Ohba).
- Takeshi Obata (born 1969, Niigata) drew Hikaru no Go (Tezuka Osamu Cultural Prize, Shogakukan Manga Award), Death Note, Bakuman and All You Need Is Kill (VIZ series page; Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: editor Koji Yoshida said the idea grew out of an angel story mentioned in Bakuman, that the angels were drawn with geometric, futuristic motifs to avoid echoing Death Note's shinigami, and that where Death Note's theme was evil or death, this series' theme is "happiness" (Wikipedia, Production). The Outerhaven wondered whether Yoneda's religious philosophy was the creators' own; no statement by Ohba on his religious views was found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Jump Square, monthly shonen). VIZ Media publishes it under its Shonen Jump imprint, rated Mature (VIZ vol. 1 and vol. 14 pages).
- No controversies involving the creators over this series were found in sources opened.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is happiness, and what would you do for it if you could become God? Mirai has nothing left on the day he jumps; Nasse catches him because she wants him to be happy. Every candidate is someone who wanted to die, handed the power to take anything. The series asks whether happiness can be taken by force (Metropoliman, Tonma), argued into being (Yoneda), or only received in ordinary life with someone you love.

**The answer the story gives.** Mirai's answer is plain: a normal life, never killing, staying with Saki. He wins the reader but not the contest. The last chapter adds a darker answer: even God and his makers can come to want death. Readers split on whether this is a warning against despair or a statement that nothing lasts; The Outerhaven found it profound, others called it hollow (AniList reviews).

**Characters as arguments.** Mirai argues for life and restraint. Metropoliman argues that the strong and beautiful should rule and the poor should be removed; he dies by a dying father's rifle. Nanato argues that a father may do anything for his family. Hajime, the bullied boy who worships power, changes when he is loved. Shuji argues that death is a mercy for those who want it; Mirai stops him from helping his brother die. Yoneda argues that God is an idea people made and that people should end on their own terms; at the end he is still trying to understand.

**Symbols and motifs.** Wings (freedom) and arrows (love that is forced, death that is painless). The four-leaf clover and ladybug Mirai once gave Saki (ch. 10-12), later the flower shop that sells clovers (ch. 57). The tower block where it starts and where Saki confesses (v12-13). The final white arrow.

**Traditions it echoes.** Death Note (a boy given power over life and death), the battle-royale story, and the Book of Job's argument about God and suffering, though without Job's ending. Its makers who want to die recall Gnostic ideas of a lesser creator. Christian readers may compare the becoming-God contest with Christian teaching on humility (the UMC article); Buddhist readers may compare its end with teaching on craving and cessation.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right is defeated. Duty ethics, never killing, is honored in Mirai but does not save the world. Secular humanism gets its best spokesman in Yoneda and its limits in his last moments. Nihilism has the final word in the plot; love has the final word for the two characters readers care about.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-8 (ch. 1-27) are a thriller: Metropoliman, the stadium, Misurin, the amusement park and the duel, with the heaviest sexual content in v1-3. Volumes 9-14 (ch. 28-58) become a philosophical debate in public view, ending in the new God and the end of everything.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nasse saves Mirai because she wants him to be happy. What does happiness mean to Mirai by the end?
2. Metropoliman and Mirai have the same powers. What makes them so different?
3. Shuji thinks helping people die is kind. Why does Mirai disagree, and who could Shuji have talked to?
4. Yoneda says God is something people invented. How would your family answer him?
5. If you were feeling the way Mirai felt in chapter 1, who would you tell? (If you or someone you know is struggling, call or text 988 in the US.)
