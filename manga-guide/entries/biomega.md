**Premise.** In the year 3005 the first crewed flight to Mars in centuries brings back the N5S virus, which turns most people into disfigured, zombie-like "drones." Zoichi Kanoe, a synthetic human working for TOA Heavy Industries, rides a heavy motorcycle whose AI, Fuyu, appears as a glowing woman and advises him in battle. He is sent to the island city 9JO to find Eon (Ion) Green, a 17-year-old orphan whose body has adapted to the virus and who carries something that can turn drones back into people. She is guarded by Kozlov, a talking, gun-carrying bear with a scientist's cloned brain. The Public Health Service's execution units and the Data Recovery Foundation (DRF), led by the ancient psychic Niarudi, want her too: the DRF plans to infect all of humanity in what it calls "the baptism," remaking a few into a new race and killing the rest. In volume 4 the DRF releases an alien polymer that consumes Earth and remakes it as an immense cord-shaped world; volumes 5-6 jump far into the future, where Zoichi, Fuyu and fellow agent Nishu Mizunoe make a last assault on Niarudi, who seeks a child, Funipero, as her new host (Wikipedia; ANN vol. 4; VIZ synopses; TV Tropes; Vaguely Offensive).

## Creator and publisher context

- Tsutomu Nihei (story and art), born February 26, 1971, in Koriyama, Fukushima. He worked in construction, studied at Parsons School of Design in New York, assisted Tsutomu Takahashi, won the 1995 Afternoon Four Seasons Award special prize, and debuted with Blame! (1997). Later works: Knights of Sidonia (2015 Kodansha Manga Award), Aposimz, Kaina of the Great Snow Sea, Tower Dungeon (Wikipedia, Tsutomu Nihei).

- Biomega shares TOA Heavy Industries and its synthetic humans with Blame! and other Nihei works (TV Tropes: "Stealth Prequel"; Wikipedia navbox).
- No interview about Biomega's themes, religion or politics was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's intent.**
- Publishers: Kodansha (Weekly Young Magazine, a seinen magazine) then Shueisha (Ultra Jump, also seinen). VIZ Media published the English edition, rated M (Mature) per reviewers.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is worth saving when the world itself is being replaced? Biomega begins as a zombie chase on an island city and ends in a remade cosmos. Its hero is not human, his best companion is a program, and the most fatherly figure is a bear with a cloned scientist's brain. The series keeps asking, without words, who counts as a person and who gets to decide what humanity becomes.

**The answer the story gives.** Loyalty and protection matter more than what you are made of. Zoichi never wavers from his mission to find Eon; Fuyu gives herself for him; Kozlov never stops guarding the girl. The DRF's answer, that a power may force a "baptism" on everyone and keep only those who become a new race, is the story's clearest evil. At the end Zoichi does not restore the old Earth but lets the new world go on (TV Tropes), a choice reviewers call open-ended.

**Characters as arguments.** Zoichi argues for duty without self-interest. Niarudi, once a child used for her powers, argues that the organic should consume everything else; her fear and madness make her the dark mirror of the made beings who serve with loyalty. General Narain, who keeps changing his body out of fear of death, shows where the craving for immortality leads. Kahdal Spindal, who obeys while doubting and once gives Zoichi an antidote, shows conscience inside a bad system. Eon, mostly absent, is the hope everyone fights over and almost never speaks for herself, which critics noted (TV Tropes).

**Symbols and motifs.** The motorcycle as a home and a partner. The virus from Mars. The "baptism" that kills. The cord-shaped world, built partly of reconstituted flesh. The orbital column Zoichi races up while the polymer chases him (ANN vol. 4). A bear with a borrowed mind as the most human character.

**Traditions it echoes.** The zombie pandemic story and Resident Evil-style corporate conspiracy (Thompson), Akira's transformed bodies and city-destroying power, Giger's biomechanics, and the end-of-the-world-and-new-world pattern found in many mythologies. The villains' "baptism" inverts Christian rebirth: a death imposed by others rather than a new life freely received.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is the air the story breathes, explored rather than praised. Authoritarian remaking of humanity is condemned. Naturalism frames the world, though the ending, where a mind becomes a world, slides toward the mystical. Nihilism is the mood of the middle volumes but not the conclusion.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (Young Magazine and early Ultra Jump) are a grim cyberpunk zombie chase on 9JO and the hunt for Eon. Volume 4 destroys the Earth and remakes it. Volumes 5-6 jump forward into a fantasy-tinged new world with a heroic tone (TV Tropes: Genre Shift), ending in a rushed final battle (Bureau 42).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Zoichi, Fuyu and Kozlov were all made by people. Are they people too? Why or why not?
2. The DRF calls its plan "the baptism." Why might villains borrow a religious word?
3. Narain changed his body again and again because he feared death. What did that fear cost him?
4. Eon is the key to the cure but hardly speaks. Who should decide what happens to her?
5. At the end Zoichi lets the new world continue instead of bringing back the old one. Was he right?
