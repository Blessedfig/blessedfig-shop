**Premise.** October 1986, the third replay of the Ushiromiya family's gathering on Rokkenjima. This arc centers on Eva, the patriarch Kinzo's eldest daughter. Flashbacks show her as a clever teenager whom Kinzo refused to send to college, telling her a woman's role was to marry well and support a man, while her older brother Krauss was handed the succession (ch. 2, likely; Gaffney calls the father abusive and misogynistic). A younger version of Eva lives on in her head and pushes her to win. Kinzo's letter says whoever solves the witch's epitaph gets the gold and the headship (ch. 3). The witch Beatrice kills Kinzo and the servants on the first night, defeats her own silver-haired teacher (later named Virgilia) in a magic duel (ch. 7-8), and on the meta-board starts winning Battler over. Eva solves the epitaph, finds ten tons of gold, and in the fantasy layer becomes a new witch, "EVA-Beatrice," who kills and revives her siblings for fun (ch. 10-14). Battler is repelled and slaps Beatrice (ch. 12); Beatrice begins to change, revives Shannon for George's sake (ch. 15) and sacrifices her standing to save Jessica (ch. 17). Battler proves through a cigarette butt that the witch is Eva (ch. 18), but Eva shoots him (ch. 19). In a false "Golden Land" Beatrice tries to make Battler sign away his denial; a stranger, Ange, breaks in (Final Chapter). In 1998, Eva, the only survivor, dies embittered; her niece Ange steps off a skyscraper at the invitation of the witch Bernkastel (Secret Tea Party) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Gaffney; ANN; Yen Press synopses).

## Life message

**Cruelty that plays with people as toys is the opposite of real "magic," which restores and makes people happy; human lives are finite and precious, and the gold and power people fight over leave even the winner alone.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame can agree that the arc condemns cruelty for fun, shows greed for gold ending in loneliness, and treats Kinzo's contempt for his daughter as a wound. The split is again over the occult. Frames that forbid sorcery and dealings with spirits (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness, Orthodox Jewish, Muslim, Anabaptist) read a cast of witches and named demons, resurrection spells and a witch's paradise as occult power at the center and grade F; Catholic, Reformed, Lutheran and Anglican readers note that the arc itself calls destructive magic a failure and exposes the paradise as a lie, and give C. Secular, atheist and agnostic frames grade it highly: the witch persona is unmasked as a person by evidence, and Beatrice herself denies witches in red. Ange's suicide at the end, framed as stepping into the witches' game rather than judged, is a concern for nearly every frame. Depiction concern is Significant almost everywhere because of the repeated torture-killings.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

## Creator and publisher context

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is magic for? Beatrice's teacher tells her in the first chapter that destroying is easy and that true magic restores what was lost and makes people happy. The whole arc tests that claim: a woman who wins the gold and the witch's power uses it to torment her siblings, and a witch who has only ever played with people tries, too late, to use what is left of her power for mercy.

**The answer the story gives.** Power without love becomes cruelty, and wealth without love becomes a curse. Eva gets everything she fought for and ends, in 1998, alone, hounded by the press, and wishing her niece the same loneliness (Secret Tea Party). Beatrice's real magic is small: a moment between George and Shannon, Jessica's life. Battler's answer is humanist: people live short lives as fully as they can, and anyone who sneers at that cannot be forgiven (ch. 12-16).

**Characters as arguments.** Eva argues that effort deserves recognition and, wounded by a father who denied it, that winning is everything; her younger self is that argument stripped of conscience. Krauss argues for birthright and regrets it. Hideyoshi argues for love and dies for it. Kyrie argues, frankly, that jealousy can be a weapon (ch. 13). Virgilia argues that magic is only science not yet understood, and that both stories can stand until the box is opened (ch. 9). Battler argues from evidence and wins the argument but not the game. Ange argues for truth at any cost.

**Symbols and motifs.** The broken vase restored (ch. 1). The head's ring. The ten tons of gold. Golden butterflies. The cigarette butt. The Braun tube and Schrodinger's box. The North Wind and the Sun. The Golden Land as a too-perfect family portrait, which Gaffney finds disturbing.

**Traditions it echoes.** The closed-circle mystery and its "fair play" rules (Knox and Van Dine, the series' wider frame; my own framing), Schrodinger's thought experiment, Hempel's raven paradox, Aesop's fable of the North Wind and the Sun, Norse myth (Gungnir, Odin, Thor's hammer) in the witches' duel, and the Western demonology of the seven deadly sins.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan/occult is the surface and is unmasked from inside: the witch persona is a person, and the witch denies witches. Postmodernism is the method: the reader must decide which layer of the story to trust. Feminism appears in Eva's backstory, where patriarchal favoritism is shown as the seed of her ruin. Consumerism is critiqued: the gold solves nothing. Secular humanism and existentialism are Battler's ground.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-5): Beatrice's past and Eva's childhood; the epitaph challenge. Volume 2 (ch. 6-9): the first twilight, the witches' duel, Virgilia's lesson. Volume 3 (ch. 10-13): Eva solves the epitaph, becomes the new witch, and the torture of Rosa and Maria; Battler's slap. Volume 4 (ch. 14-17): the hall battle, George and Shannon's reunion, Beatrice's sacrifice. Volume 5 (ch. 18-Secret Tea Party): Battler unmasks Eva, the Golden Land, and 1998.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kinzo told Eva her place was to marry well. How did that shape what she became?
2. Beatrice's teacher says true magic restores and creates happiness. Which characters use their power that way?
3. Eva wins the gold. What does she lose?
4. Battler slaps Beatrice for treating death as a game. Was he right? What changed in her afterward?
5. Ange chooses to jump. What could have helped her, and who failed her?
