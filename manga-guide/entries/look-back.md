**Executive summary.** A short, quiet drama about two girls who make manga together, and about grief after one of them is killed in a random attack on her art school. The overall content level is 6 out of 10, set by grief, self-blame and a mass killing that is mostly reported rather than shown. There is almost no sexual content, language or substance use. The work drew a real-world controversy: its attacker was first written with hints of a psychotic illness, and after criticism that this stigmatized mental illness, Shueisha revised the lines twice (August 2021); the print edition carries the revised text. Many readers also read it as a response to the 2019 Kyoto Animation arson attack. Most traditions would find the message (friendship, diligence, art as a gift to others, living on after loss) a good fit; the points families discuss are the violence against art students, survivor's guilt, and the ambiguous "what if" timeline.
Outlook: Bittersweet — the friend cannot be saved, but the story ends with the survivor choosing to keep drawing because of what their friendship gave her.

**Premise.** Ayumu Fujino is a fourth grader who draws four-panel gag strips (yonkoma) for her school newspaper and is the class star. Her teacher gives part of her space to Kyomoto, a classmate who has stopped coming to school. Kyomoto's detailed background art is far better, and Fujino, jealous, practices obsessively for two years before quitting. At graduation she delivers Kyomoto's diploma and learns Kyomoto is her biggest fan. The two become partners and publish one-shots under the shared pen name "Kyo Fujino." After high school Kyomoto chooses art school instead of a serialization, and Fujino goes on alone to a hit series, Shark Kick. On January 10, 2016, a man who claims the school stole his work attacks it with a pickaxe, killing Kyomoto and others (Wikipedia; Cartoon Brew gives 12 dead). In grief, Fujino tears up the strip that first drew Kyomoto out of her room, and the story moves into an alternate timeline in which the two never met and Fujino stops the attacker. A strip from that timeline, titled "Look Back," reaches the real Fujino, who returns to her desk and keeps drawing.

## Life message

**A good life is spent making things with and for other people. Talent and rivalry are not enough; art is worth the grind because of who it is shared with. Loss cannot be undone, but it can be carried forward into work.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Fujimoto was born in Nikaho, Akita Prefecture, and graduated in Western painting from Tohoku University of Art and Design in Yamagata in 2014; he first took oil-painting classes that his grandparents also attended (Wikipedia). The art school in Look Back shares the name of his own university (Wikipedia plot summary).
- On the work's origin (via Anime Hunch, October 2021; original interview not identified): Fujimoto said Look Back is based on his own past. He volunteered after the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, felt drawing had no point, and felt helpless when a day of work showed no progress; drawing Look Back was "his attempt to sort out his feelings of helplessness." Nippon.com reports him saying the work was him forcing himself "to come to terms with something he previously couldn't."
- On the edits: Fujimoto said he made the changes "out of a desire to avoid depicting something that could be linked to promoting bias or discrimination" (ComicBook.com, August 2021). Shonen Jump+ said readers had pointed out "inappropriate expressions" and that it wanted to avoid "prejudice and discrimination" (Epicdope).
- He has not officially confirmed the Kyoto Animation attack as an inspiration (ANN This Week in Anime). The one-shot came out around the attack's second anniversary on July 18 (Wikipedia gives July 19, 2021; Cartoon Brew gives July 18; WWAC says it was "deliberately released on the 2nd anniversary").
- Statements on religion or politics: none found. In 2017 he posted online under an alias claiming to be his younger sister (Wikipedia). He keeps a low public profile and does not show his face (from general knowledge; to verify). **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Shonen Jump+). VIZ Media publishes the English edition under its VIZ Signature line, rated Teen Plus. VIZ is owned by Shueisha, Shogakukan and Shogakukan-Shueisha Productions (from general knowledge; to verify).
- Film: director Kiyotaka Oshiyama kept hand-drawn imperfections and positioned the film against generative AI (ANN This Week in Anime; ANN interview found, not opened).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why make art at all, when it is hard, lonely and cannot protect the people you love? Fujino asks it out loud near the end: she says she hates drawing, and the remembered voice of her friend asks, "Then, why do you draw, Fujino?" (Wikipedia). Fujimoto has said the work grew from his own sense that drawing was pointless after the 2011 earthquake (Anime Hunch).

**The answer the story gives.** The answer is a person. Fujino first drew to be praised and to beat a rival. She kept drawing because Kyomoto loved her work, and the two made strips together. After Kyomoto's death, Fujino finds that Kyomoto kept buying her series and voting for it in reader surveys (Wikipedia). The ending does not undo the loss. Fujino tapes Kyomoto's last strip above her desk and goes back to work. The story says the work is worth doing because of who it was for, and that it can carry the dead forward.

**Characters as arguments.** Fujino is the argument that talent without humility stalls, and that envy can turn into partnership. Kyomoto is the argument that a person shut away from the world can still have a rich inner life, and that leaving one's room is an act of courage (Common Sense Media notes her anxiety is "portrayed sympathetically"). The attacker is not much of an argument at all. He is a force of random harm, and after the edits even his motive is reduced to "general apathy and hostility" (Epicdope). The alternate-timeline Fujino is a wish: the person who could have stopped it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The four-panel strip is the unit of the story: the strip that slips under Kyomoto's door starts the friendship, and a torn piece of it starts the alternate timeline (Wikipedia). Fujino's back, seen at her desk, recurs as the image of work. Kyomoto's door marks the line between isolation and the world. The title links to Oasis's "Don't Look Back in Anger" (TV Tropes): looking back is allowed, anger is not the end. Sketchbook piles stand for years of practice.

**Traditions it echoes.** The artist's coming-of-age story (Bakuman, Blue Period). Japanese writing about the manga trade. The "what if" grief narrative found in many literatures. CBR compares the rescue fantasy to Quentin Tarantino's rewriting of historical tragedies. Many Japanese and Western critics read it in the tradition of memorial art after the Kyoto Animation arson (Cartoon Brew; nippon.com).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialist and secular humanist readings fit the ending, where meaning is made by a person rather than given. Stoic acceptance is the turn of the final pages. Virtue ethics fits the long practice that makes Fujino skilled. Religious readers can see grief, memory and a voice from beyond in the ambiguous final act; the text does not settle what that voice is. Critical theory enters only through the reception, in the debate about how the attacker was first written.

**Shifts across the series.** The first half is light, funny and fast, a comedy of rivalry and friendship. The middle turns quiet as the two part. The last third turns sharply dark with the attack, then dreamlike with the alternate timeline, then still. ScreenAnarchy calls the turn "bewilderingly brutal"; ANN's discussion says the back half "loses some steam" while defending the twist as necessary.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Fujino first draws to be the best. Why does she keep drawing later? Have your own reasons for doing something changed?
2. Fujino blames herself for Kyomoto's death. Was it her fault? What would you say to a friend who felt that way?
3. Is the alternate timeline real, a dream, or something else? What does your family believe about the dead?
4. The publisher changed how the attacker was described after readers said it was unfair to people with mental illness. Was that the right decision? Why?
5. Kyomoto chose to go to art school instead of staying with Fujino. Is it possible to love someone and still choose your own path?
