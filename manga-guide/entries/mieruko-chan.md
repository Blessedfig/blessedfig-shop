**Premise.** Miko Yotsuya is an ordinary high school girl who one day starts seeing grotesque ghosts and monsters that no one else can see. They react when they sense they are seen, so she decides to act as if she sees nothing. She tries to keep a normal school life and to shield her best friend Hana, whose strong "life aura" draws spirits. Along the way she meets Yuria, a classmate who sees spirits less clearly and wants to be an exorcist; Godmother (Mitsue Takeda), a fortune teller; Rom, a medium; Zen Tohno, a substitute teacher followed by spirits; and Michiru Ichijo, a model whose head Miko sees covered by a tentacled spirit. The series mixes horror with school comedy (Izumi: "not just a horror story but also a story about the girls' daily lives with comedy elements," ANN interview).

## Life message

**A good life is lived with steady nerve and quiet kindness: you keep your fear to yourself, protect the people you love, honor the dead, and find out that many "monsters", living or dead, are carrying pain.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Threat detection is the engine of the story: Miko sees predators others cannot and freezes rather than flees, a classic survival response. Protecting a close friend (Hana) and a younger brother is kin and ally care. The ghosts drawn to Hana's "life aura" frame vitality itself as a lure for danger. There is no romance plot; Michiru's fixation on Miko (vol. 8-10) is read by Siliconera as a trauma response, not courtship. |
| Pragmatic | Avoidance works, most of the time: the ghost "wanders off and fades away… but not for long" (Beneath the Tangles). Charms and wards fail when a stronger spirit comes (Godmother quits after her wards fail, ch. 6, per TV Tropes). The story tests what works: silence, salt, a shrine's "three times" of protection (ch. 11), and finally allies who share the burden (Godmother, Rom, Seto, vol. 5-12). |
| Religious | Japanese folk religion is treated as real: Buddhist mantras, prayer beads and salt are named as remedies (episode 1, per Beneath the Tangles); a fox shrine's gods promise protection "three times" (ch. 11) and later look less benevolent (ch. 24, 27); a "God of the Mountain" demands a price (vol. 6). Miko sees her dead father's ghost at the breakfast table and offers him pudding (ch. 9), an echo of Japanese household offerings to the dead. Professional mediums and fortune tellers are shown as partly genuine. No Christian, Jewish or Islamic content was found. |
| Socio-cultural | School life, friendship lunches, a fan-figure craze (the "Ramrabi"/"Lambun" figures, vol. 11-12) and a donut chain (Mrs. Donut) frame ordinary teen life in Japan. Family is warm but marked by loss (the father died a year before, ch. 9). Abusive parenting appears in Zen's "Education Mama" mother (ch. 21). Loneliness appears in Yuria eating lunch alone in the girls' bathroom (ch. 17) and in Michiru's controlled upbringing (vol. 9-10). Critics single out the camera's attention to the bodies of teenage girls as at odds with the story (see How readers took it). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the ghosts never leave and the dead father cannot come back, yet small kindnesses, friends and the release of suffering spirits keep the story warm.
Hope source: F — Miko's way through is the people around her: shielding Hana, honoring her father, and gaining allies (Yuria, Godmother, Rom) who share what she sees.

**Where traditions disagree.** The dividing line is spirit contact. For Pentecostal, Adventist and Jehovah's Witness families, ghosts of the dead, mediums and a bargain with shrine gods are the premise, so the message grade is low; Orthodox, Baptist, Reformed, Muslim, Sikh and Orthodox Jewish readers raise the same point more moderately. Buddhist, Hindu, Shinto and Pagan readers find the spirit world familiar, though Shinto readers may object to sinister shrine gods. On depiction, the fan service of high school girls is the shared concern across very different frames: Catholic and evangelical modesty, Muslim lowering of the gaze, and secular progressive concern about sexualizing minors all point to the same scenes, mainly in the anime and early manga.

## Creator and publisher context

- Tomoki Izumi posted the first chapter on Twitter in 2018, and it went viral (Japanese Wikipedia). In an ANN interview (December 2021, with anime director Yuki Ogawa), Izumi said: "I want to draw my own manga and go viral too!", that he had long been interested in horror, that he wanted "an option for serialization that isn't the traditional route," and that the series "isn't just a horror story but also a story about the girls' daily lives with comedy elements as well." The interview did not address fan service or the characters' ages.
- No public statement by Izumi on religion, politics, or the fan service criticism was found. Earlier career and other works were not confirmed (Pixiv Encyclopedia unreachable). **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Kadokawa (MF Comics; web serialization on ComicWalker / Web Comic Apanta). Yen Press, the English publisher, is a joint venture of Kadokawa and Hachette (from general knowledge; to verify). Anime by Passione; licensed by Funimation, now Crunchyroll (Wikipedia).
- No reputable reports of controversy involving the creator were found.

**Profile notes for parents:** The series is a horror comedy built on fear and care: a girl keeps her nerve to protect a friend, and many hauntings turn out to be about grief or abuse. Its spirit world is Japanese folk religion treated as real, which matters most to families with teaching on spirit contact. The issue most critics raise is the camera's focus on the bodies of teenage girls, strongest in the anime and the first manga volumes.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do when you see something terrible that no one else sees? Miko cannot make the ghosts go away, cannot prove they exist, and learns early that reacting makes them worse. The series asks whether pretending not to see is cowardice, wisdom, or a kind of love.

**The answer the story gives.** At first, avoidance is survival. As the story goes on, it becomes clear that Miko's silence also protects others: Hana, her brother, and her mother would be endangered or burdened. The answer the story gives is not to confront every horror, but to keep your nerve, choose your moments, and let others share the load. Allies (Yuria, Godmother, Rom, Seto) slowly replace isolation. The story is ongoing, so its final answer is unknown.

**Characters as arguments.** Miko is the argument for self-control as courage. Hana is innocence that draws danger without knowing it. Yuria is enthusiasm without the full picture; she sees less and fears less. Godmother is the professional who fails, quits, returns and finally offers herself in another's place (ch. 6, 26, 37). Zen is the argument that a frightening exterior can hide a hurt and gentle man (ch. 16-23); his mother is the abuse that made him. Michiru is the argument that a cold, even menacing person may be a victim (Siliconera on vol. 9).

**Symbols and motifs.** Eyes and seeing: the title itself. Food: Hana's appetite feeds her "life aura," and the pudding Miko offers her dead father becomes a sign of reconciliation (ch. 9). Cats: the ghost cats around Zen turn out to be comforters, not victims seeking revenge. The shrine bell and the rule of three: protection that has limits and a price.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese kaidan (ghost stories) and urban legends; TV "real scary story" segments, which the anime imitates (ANN ep. 4); Shinto shrine customs and the idea that kami can be dangerous; Buddhist ideas of attachment holding the dead in place; Western horror's long habit, noted by Beneath the Tangles, of putting frightened young women at the center.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Folk religion is shown as real and morally mixed: helpful spirits, predatory spirits and gods with a price. Virtue ethics and Stoicism are affirmed through Miko's self-command. Pragmatism runs through the trial-and-error methods. Feminist and progressive readers find a capable female cast framed at times by a camera they call exploitative. Secular readers will see the hauntings as stories about grief and abuse.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-17 (v1-3) are episodic scares with the heaviest fan service. The Zen arc (ch. 16-23) and the father reveal (ch. 9) add emotional weight. The shrine and mountain-god arc (ch. 24-37, v4-6) turns darker and more mythic. From vol. 7, Michiru's arc and the investigations of Rom and Seto (vol. 7-12) turn the series toward a mystery with a growing cast.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Miko chooses to ignore what she sees. When is ignoring something wise, and when is it wrong?
2. The shrine offers protection "three times" but at a price. What does your family's tradition say about asking spirits or gods for favors?
3. Zen looks frightening but cares for cats. Have you misjudged someone by how they looked?
4. Miko offers her dead father the pudding they fought over. How does your family remember people who have died?
5. Critics said the camera stares at the girls in ways that do not fit the story. Why might that matter in a story about a frightened teenager?
