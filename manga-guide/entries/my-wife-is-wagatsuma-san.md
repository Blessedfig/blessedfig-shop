**Premise.** Hitoshi Aoshima, an unpopular second-year high school student and volleyball benchwarmer, longs for a girlfriend, above all the school's beauty, Ai Wagatsuma, a top student and swimmer who wants to be a veterinarian. One day he slips ten years into the future and finds himself married to her. He cannot control when he slips or returns, and he soon learns that what he does in the present changes the future: in some futures he is married to someone else, such as Silvia Shimotsuma, a half-Dutch exchange student, Ran Itou, a shy aspiring manga artist, or a substitute teacher, Itsuko Kaji. He falls in with the DX Corps, a secret society of unpopular boys who resent popular ones, and uses what he sees to steer his friends away from ruined futures: he keeps his friend Shirou Itou from becoming a sex offender (vol. 3) and turns the life of the popular boy Dobashi around. Near the end, the new math teacher, Fumio Seki, is revealed as a fellow time-slipper who has been watching over him (vol. 13). Reviewers say the comedy gives way to melodrama in the second half and that the ending, in which Aoshima and Ai get together, is rushed (Wikipedia; AniList; MyAnimeList reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could see your future, would you live differently today? Aoshima is shown a prize, a happy marriage to the girl he adores, and then learns the prize is not guaranteed. The series asks whether a future can be earned, and what kind of person you must become to deserve it.

**The answer the story gives.** The future follows character, not tricks. Aoshima first uses his glimpses to game Ai's affections, then finds that every selfish shortcut changes things in ways he cannot control. What earns him a good future is the growth reviewers note: he steps up for friends and even strangers. ANN's Jason Thompson calls it "a cute premise handled in clever, silly ways."

**Characters as arguments.** Aoshima argues for the ordinary boy who becomes reliable. The DX Corps argues the case of resentful outsiders, and their futures (a flasher in prison, a NEET) are the warning. Dobashi, the popular boy they hate, turns out to need saving too. Ai is the ideal more than a person, which reviewers fault. Seki, the teacher who watches over Aoshima, suggests that adults who know where choices lead should guide the young.

**Symbols and motifs.** The ten-year jump; the wedding ring in the future; chapter titles taken from rock songs; the DX Corps' codenames; the swimming pool where Ai is hurt (ch. 2).

**Traditions it echoes.** Time-loop and branching-future stories (Back to the Future, Butterfly Effect, as reviewers note), How I Met Your Mother (Publishers Weekly), and the shōnen harem comedy it partly parodies. Its moral that deeds bear fruit later echoes Galatians 6:7 for Christian readers and karma for Hindu and Buddhist readers.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is affirmed in a light key: the future is made, not given. Communitarianism wins as Aoshima's goals widen from himself to his friends. Hedonism is present as fan service and otaku comedy but is not the story's answer.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 are gag comedy built on time-slip setups. Around the middle (reviewers place it near the halfway point) the series turns toward drama and the love polygon with Silvia and Ran. Volume 13 reveals Seki and closes quickly.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Aoshima saves Itou from a terrible future. What would you do if you saw a friend heading the wrong way?
2. Is it fair to Ai that Aoshima learns her tastes by seeing the future? Why or why not?
3. The DX Corps hates popular kids. What changes when they become friends with Dobashi?
4. Many jokes in the book are about looking at girls. How would the girls in the story feel about them?
