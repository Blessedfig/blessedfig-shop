**Premise.** Buntaro Mori is a withdrawn high school transfer student who has shut himself off from people after something happened at his last school. On his first day, a classmate, Miyamoto, bets him that he cannot climb the outside of the school building. Mori climbs it with no rope and feels alive for the first time. He joins the school climbing club under teacher Masao Onishi. After a death and a scandal, he becomes a solo climber. As an adult he works ordinary jobs and trains alone, joins and loses a K2 expedition team, marries, has a child, and finally attempts the unclimbed East Face of K2. Unlike the novel, in which the real Buntaro Kato dies in a blizzard, the manga's hero survives and comes home to his family (TV Tropes; YAMA HACK; Honcierge).

## Life message

**A life is worth living when you give all your strength to something, but no one can live entirely alone. The rope that ties climbers together, and in the end a family, is what brings a person back down the mountain.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Shin-ichi Sakamoto (born 1972, Osaka; debut 1990). He says that before The Climber he drew what he thought would sell, and that in this series he put his own self, lifestyle and thought process into the work. He calls it a turning point that set his style. He said the novel uses metaphors and he chose to show them visually, for example mirrored lettering for a character's panic from lack of oxygen (Wikipedia, opened). He visits a shrine to pay respects for the many lives lost in his manga (Wikipedia). No statements on politics or religion found. Later works: Innocent, Innocent Rouge, #DRCL midnight children.
- Staff changes: Yoshio Nabeta wrote vols. 1-2, Hiroshi Takano vols. 2-4, then Sakamoto wrote alone (Wikipedia; ANN; Japanese Wikipedia says the original-writer credit was later dropped). Reasons not stated in any source opened. Reviewers link the tone shift to this change (MAL; TV Tropes).
- Source: Jiro Nitta's novel about Buntaro Kato, a working-class solo climber of the 1930s who died in a blizzard (sabukaru; Kato died in 1936, from general knowledge; to verify). The manga keeps the name and the theme of solitude but changes the setting, characters and ending (Japanese Wikipedia; Honcierge).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Hitotsubashi Group, from general knowledge). Viz Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge).
- No controversies involving the creators found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person live entirely alone, and what does it cost to try? Mori begins as a boy who wants nothing from anyone. Climbing gives him the first moment in which he feels alive. The series asks whether that feeling is a path to life or a slow way of leaving it.

**The answer the story gives.** At first the story seems to side with solitude. Mori turns away from the club, from rope partners and from ordinary work, and the mountains reward him with beauty and danger in equal measure. The deaths around him, the scandal and the betrayals push him further away. But the late story changes the answer. He marries Hana and has a child, and the question becomes "Mountains or family" (ch. 129). On K2 he climbs alone after Takemura cuts the rope (YAMA HACK), survives the descent only because of a rope left by an earlier team (TV Tropes), and loses fingers and toes. The last chapter, "Survivor", shows him content with his family and no longer needing extreme climbing (TV Tropes). The novel's real climber died in the mountains; the manga lets him come home.

**Characters as arguments.** Mori is the argument that a person can be self-sufficient. Onishi is the argument that climbing is about trust: the rope between partners. His early death removes that voice and the story darkens. Miyamoto argues for wanting the glory without the work, and he falls into lies and easy money. Niimi argues that success and honor justify anything. Takemura climbs to be seen, and Mori answers that what they do needs no witness (v15). Hana argues for belonging.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rope stands for human connection (Animechu review). The summit stands for the self alone. Altitude and oxygen loss stand for isolation ("Solitude as an oxygen-free state", ch. 114). Sakamoto draws inner states as visual metaphor, such as mirrored lettering for panic (Wikipedia). Mountains appear as both horror and freedom (ANN).

**Traditions it echoes.** Existentialist ideas of meaning found at the limit. Romantic mountain literature and the solitary hero. Buddhist language about craving and liberation in its chapter titles. Japanese mountain reverence. The Japanese "I-novel" habit of self-examination, which Sakamoto echoes when he says he put himself into the work.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is the story's own frame. Expressive individualism is the hero's starting creed and is finally set aside. Stoic endurance is admired. Nihilism and hedonism appear as traps. Feminist readers find little: ANN and MAL reviewers criticize how women are written. Religion is present only as metaphor.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 read as a school sports manga. After Onishi's death (likely v3-4) and the writer change, it becomes a dark psychological drama (TV Tropes; MAL). The middle volumes follow adult isolation, the K2 team and its collapse. The last volumes bring marriage, a child and the final K2 climb, ending in survival.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mori says climbing makes him feel alive. Why might danger make someone feel that way, and what are safer ways to feel it?
2. Is wanting to be alone always a problem? When does it become one?
3. Takemura cuts the rope so Mori can go on. Was that brave, or a loss that should not have happened? What does your family's tradition say?
4. Why do you think the manga lets Mori survive when the real climber died?
5. What did Hana and the child give Mori that the summit could not?
