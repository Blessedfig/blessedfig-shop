**Premise.** Buntaro Mori, a withdrawn transfer student in Yokosuka, wants only to be left alone. When a loud classmate, Miyamoto, dares him to climb the school building, Mori free-solos it to the roof and discovers the one thing that makes him feel alive (VIZ vol. 1; SLJ). His teacher Onishi, himself a climber, starts a rock-climbing club to keep him safe, but a journalist, Kurosawa, writes a sensational article that gets the club shut down, and Mori goes alpine climbing with the man who ruined it (VIZ vol. 2). After a tragedy in the high-school years and a time skip of about two years (TV Tropes), Mori works low-paid jobs to fund climbs, joins a wealthy sponsor's K2 team whose members compete ruthlessly (VIZ vol. 3; Mangapedia), and survives a training expedition in the Japanese Alps in which teammates die (VIZ vol. 5). He retreats alone to the Mount Fuji weather station, where a chance rescue of a lost hiker, Hana, changes his life (VIZ vol. 6). Married, with a baby daughter and a research job, he takes his wife's surname, Katō (Mangapedia; TV Tropes), until K2's unclimbed East Face calls him back (VIZ vol. 7). The final arc is that climb (VIZ vol. 8). TV Tropes reports that he reaches the summit alone, loses fingers and toes to frostbite, and comes home to his family.

## Life message

**A life is worth living when you give all your strength to something, but no one can live entirely alone. The rope that ties climbers together, and in the end a family, is what brings a person back down the mountain.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Shin'ichi Sakamoto debuted in 1990; The Climber and Innocent each won an Excellence Award at the Japan Media Arts Festival; #DRCL midnight children is also published by VIZ (VIZ series page).
- ComicBook.com reports that Yoshio Nabeta left after about ch. 30, that Sakamoto had until then chased the market with generic shōnen series, and that he reshaped The Climber into a psychological story because he thought it might be his last manga. Wikipedia and MangaUpdates credit Hiroshi Takano with story for vols. 2-4 and Sakamoto alone from vol. 5.
- Jirō Nitta (1912-1980) was a novelist and meteorologist at the Central Meteorological Observatory who pioneered the mountain novel; Naoki Prize 1955 for Gōriki-den (VIZ). The hero is partly based on Buntarō Katō, a real solo climber of the 1920s-30s (Wikipedia), who died in the mountains (from general knowledge; to verify). The manga moves the story to the present day and, per TV Tropes, gives it a different ending.
- No creator interviews on themes, religion or politics were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Young Jump, seinen). VIZ Media publishes it under VIZ Signature, rated T+. No controversies were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why climb, and why live? Mori finds that the only time he feels alive is on a wall where one mistake kills him. The series follows that discovery for a whole life and asks whether a person can live entirely for one thing, and entirely alone.

**The answer the story gives.** Devotion is real and beautiful, but solitude is not freedom. Mori's refusal of the rope, of teammates and of help costs him friends and nearly his life; what brings him back is a family (VIZ vols. 6-7). TV Tropes reports an ending in which he summits K2's East Face alone and then comes home, no longer needing the extreme climbs.

**Characters as arguments.** Onishi argues for trust and care; Kurosawa for pushing a gifted person at any cost, and he turns out to carry his own guilt; Miyamoto for show and shortcuts, and he fails; Niimi for success at any price to clear a father's name, and dies; Hana for belonging and ordinary love; Takemura for following a hero, and he dies on K2.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rope that ties two climbers, which can save both or be cut. The school wall in chapter 1. The weather station on Fuji as a hermitage. Volume titles such as "Memento Mori" and "Katō Buntarō" (Mangapedia). Aftermath describes avalanches drawn as skyscrapers and trucks falling: nature as overwhelming force.

**Traditions it echoes.** Nitta's mountain novels and the life of Buntarō Katō; existentialist literature on authentic choice and death; the hermit and the pilgrim. Christian readers may hear "it is not good for the man to be alone"; Buddhist readers, the problem of craving.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism is affirmed in its seriousness about death and choice; expressive individualism is shown as a trap; stoic endurance is honored; communitarian and family values win the ending.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (high school; Nabeta and Takano as writers) start as a sports story. From vol. 5, after a time skip, Sakamoto alone turns it into a psychological drama (ComicBook): poverty, the K2 team and deaths in the Alps (vols. 5-10), the Fuji station and Hana (vols. 11-12), family life (vols. 13-14) and K2 (vols. 15-17).

**Discussion questions.**
