**Premise.** In 2015, fifteen years after a catastrophe called Second Impact killed half of humanity, giant beings called Angels attack the city of Tokyo-3. Fourteen-year-old Shinji Ikari is summoned by his estranged father, Gendo, the commander of the agency NERV, to pilot Evangelion Unit-01, a giant bio-machine. He fights alongside the pilots Rei Ayanami and Asuka Langley Soryu. He lives with his guardian, Major Misato Katsuragi. Behind the war, Gendo and a secret council called Seele each pursue the "Human Instrumentality Project", a plan to merge all human souls into one. Sadamoto's Shinji is more argumentative and less passive than the anime's (Wikipedia; Manga Bookshelf), Gendo is more openly cruel (EvaGeeks; TV Tropes), and the ending differs: Shinji rejects Instrumentality and humanity is restored (EvaGeeks vol. 14).

## Life message

**A good life means choosing to live among other people, with the pain that comes from being separate, rather than escaping into isolation or into a merged, painless collective. Facing others, and your own fear, is worth the hurt.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | The story turns on attachment: children deprived of parents (Shinji's absent father, Asuka's mother's suicide, Rei's lack of any parent) and adults who use them. Shinji's longing for maternal contact drives him (Wikipedia). Romance is tentative and mostly stalls; the one clear adult couple (Kaji and Misato) is broken by death. Instrumentality is a fantasy of escaping individual survival into one organism, and the story chooses separate individuals who must cooperate. |
| Pragmatic | Institutions fail: NERV lies to its pilots, Seele plots in secret, and the government sends troops to kill the staff and pilots (stages 77-83). Kaji's manga-only backstory shows the post-catastrophe world as a place where soldiers shoot orphans for stealing food (stage 43). Adults put children in combat because only children can pilot. Competence matters (Misato's tactics, Ritsuko's science) but cannot fix the lies at the top. |
| Religious | Christian and Jewish names and symbols (Angels, Adam, Lilith, the Spear of Longinus, crosses, the Kabbalistic Tree of Life in the franchise) are used heavily. Manga Bookshelf calls the series "drenched in religion". Anime staff said the symbols were used to "look cool" (Kazuya Tsurumaki, via the Forward), and a Christian blogger calls the crosses imagery that "does absolutely nothing in the greater scheme of the religion itself" (Beneath the Tangles). There is no God figure; humans and Seele try to engineer a god-like merged humanity, and the attempt is undone. |
| Socio-cultural | Japanese school life, a family of misfits in Misato's apartment, duty to pilot, and a father who must be obeyed. The manga sharpens the critique of a cold patriarch: Gendo tells Shinji he resents him as a rival for Yui (EvaGeeks), then, dying, remembers loving him (stage 92). Death is frequent and mostly unheroic; the restored world at the end is a second chance without memory of the war. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the story says life with others is worth its pain and ends with humanity restored and Shinji looking forward, but only after most of the adults and friends around him have died and the war is forgotten (reviewers call the ending "bittersweet": TV Tropes; ComicBookBin; CBR calls it "more optimistic" and "more uplifting" than the anime's endings).
Hope source: F — the way through is connection with other people: Shinji rejects a merged humanity so he can live with separate others, Rei helps him back (stage 93), and the epilogue opens on new friendship (Kensuke) and a first meeting with Asuka.

Each row describes how that tradition's own teaching on art and media would usually read the series, following the frame guides in /mnt/project-files/frame-guides/. Intensity alone never lowers a grade. **Depiction concern** is that tradition's view of the explicitness itself. All 31 frame guides are drafts from general knowledge, so every row is a draft. What the rows weigh: the message chooses life with others over escape, despair and forced merger, and the god-making plans of Seele and Gendo fail; adults who exploit children are shown as wrong; a dying father repents in part (stage 92). The sacred is treated as imagery: Angels are monstrous enemies, Lilith and Adam are giant beings, the Spear of Longinus is a weapon, crosses are visual motifs, and staff called the symbols decorative (Forward). **Where traditions disagree.** Most frames find the core message (choose life with others; human god-making fails) a good fit. ## Creator and publisher context

- Yoshiyuki Sadamoto (born January 29, 1962, Yamaguchi Prefecture) is a founding member of Gainax and character designer for Nadia, Evangelion, FLCL, The Girl Who Leapt Through Time, Summer Wars and Wolf Children (Wikipedia, opened).
- In a September 2016 interview (Wave Motion Cannon, opened), Sadamoto said there was no script for the manga: "I just wrote it on my own, looking at the storyboards. So I didn't consult anyone, and of course no one from the TV series' staff intervened in my manga." He called the extra chapter with Mari "not in the storyline" and "like fanservice. Just something you mustn't think too hard about." He named Leiji Matsumoto, Go Nagai and Katsuhiro Otomo as manga influences. CBR (opened) says he did not share Anno's vision for the series.
- On-record statements about the manga (Wikipedia, opened): it began as a supplemental work promoting the TV series; he calls it "his own individual work" that "should never be used as reference for anything in the anime"; he made it without consulting anyone and all differences were his decisions. In a 2014 interview he said Rei and Shinji love each other, with Rei as a motherly figure, and he described Kaworu's relationship with Shinji as like an older student impressing a younger one, not romance. He called the bonus chapter "fan service". No statement on religion or the religious imagery by Sadamoto was found.
- On the imagery, the anime's staff (not Sadamoto) are on record: assistant director Kazuya Tsurumaki said the Christian symbols were mainly meant to "look cool", and director Hideaki Anno called a Kabbalah reading "quite a misconception" in 1997 (both via the Forward, opened).
- Sadamoto left Khara and said he had "no involvement whatsoever" in the final Rebuild film after fan protests (Wikipedia).
- Controversy: on August 9, 2019 Sadamoto tweeted about the "After 'Freedom of Expression'?" exhibition at the Aichi Triennale, calling the Statue of Peace (a comfort-women memorial) a "dirty statue of a girl" and a film in the show "indistinguishable from a certain country's style of propaganda" and "just vulgar and tedious"; in the same thread he said seeing the statue in person might change his view and apologized to the model, "if there was one" (Anime News Network, opened). Korean, Japanese and English-speaking fans criticized the remarks (ANN). Wikipedia (opened) adds that he later said he disliked the statue's design and had Korean friends. The exhibition itself closed after three days amid complaints and threats (ANN). Stated neutrally; no other controversy found.
- Publisher: Kadokawa Shoten (Kadokawa Corporation). English publisher Viz Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it worth living as a separate person, when other people always hurt you? Every main character answers it. Shinji runs from his father and from danger. Asuka hides behind pride. Rei does not know she may want anything. Gendo wants to undo his wife's loss. Seele wants to end separateness for everyone.

**The answer the story gives.** Sadamoto's ending gives a clearer answer than the anime. Shinji first wishes Third Impact into being, believing it will spare the people he loves (stage 91). In the sea of merged souls, Rei helps him see that this is not what he wants (stage 93). He reverses it and frees the souls (stage 94), says goodbye to his mother (stage 95), and the world returns. In the final stage, people have no memory of the war; Shinji moves to Tokyo and meets Asuka as if for the first time. Reviewers call the ending "bittersweet" (TV Tropes) and "lyrical" (ComicBookBin). The answer is that separate life, with its pain, is worth choosing.

**Characters as arguments.** Shinji is the argument that a frightened person can still choose. Sadamoto made him more argumentative than the anime's Shinji (Manga Bookshelf), so his choice reads as his own. Gendo is grief turned into domination: he tells his son he resents him (EvaGeeks) and only on his deathbed remembers loving him (stage 92). Rei is a manufactured person who becomes a person by caring; she dies for Shinji (stage 65) and later guides him home. Kaworu is a colder figure in the manga: he kills a starving kitten (stage 57), presses himself on Shinji (stages 62, 67) and asks to be killed (stage 73). He stands for a love that is really possession and a peace that is really death. Misato is the flawed adult who finally acts for Shinji's sake, at the cost of her life.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Angels, Adam, Lilith and the Spear of Longinus give the war a biblical scale, but the series uses them as names and images more than as doctrine. The "A.T. Field" is the wall around each self that Kaworu explains everyone has (stage 73). LCL, the liquid of the entry plugs, becomes the "sea of life" where all souls merge (stage 93). The petrified Mass Production Evas in the final stage stand as relics of a war no one remembers. The manga opens and closes with Shinji walking a city street, first downcast, then hopeful (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Babel story and the Fall, read by some as a warning against making oneself god. Kabbalah's Tree of Life and the legend of Lilith, though a rabbi quoted by the Forward finds the use incidental and partly wrong. Jung's psychology, which the Forward suggests is the likelier source. Freudian themes of mother and father. Mecha and "monster of the week" conventions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and secular humanism shape the answer. Transhumanism and collectivism live in the villains' plans and fail. Nihilism is a real temptation for Shinji and is overcome. Christian and Jewish imagery is everywhere on the surface, without a God who acts; Christian commentators distinguish this "mythos" from real religious themes (Beneath the Tangles).

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 follow the anime's early arc with sharper humor (AIPT; ComicBookBin calls them "giant robot with quality soap opera"). Vol. 6 is the turn: Toji dies (stages 39-40), and Manga Bookshelf reads it as Sadamoto answering talk of a "lighter, more optimistic" manga. Vols. 7-8: Kaji's backstory (stages 43-44), Unit-01's awakening (46-47), Shinji absorbed into Unit-01 and meeting his mother (49-51), Kaji killed (55). Vols. 9-11 bring Kaworu, Asuka's breakdown, Rei's death and Kaworu's death. Vols. 12-14 cover the assault on NERV, the deaths of Misato (83), Ritsuko and Gendo (85-88), Third Impact and the restored world.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shinji almost chooses a world where no one can hurt anyone. Why does he refuse it in the end?
2. Gendo remembers loving his son only as he dies. Can a parent's late regret repair years of coldness?
3. The adults send 14-year-olds into battle "for humanity". When, if ever, is that defensible?
4. Kaworu presses closeness on Shinji when Shinji does not want it. What makes affection respectful or not?
5. The series uses angels, crosses and the Tree of Life as images. How does your family's tradition understand those symbols, and does the series change them?
