**Premise.** Rei Yokoe, tall, vain and scatterbrained, and Kei Sugiura, short, boyish and always frowning, have been inseparable since middle school and bicker in nearly every line; Rei has had a crush on Kei since middle school but hides it behind teasing (AniList profiles; Okazu). Around them is an ensemble from two schools: Yatsude Negoro, the meddling Kansai-dialect president of a two-member Film Studies club; her underclassman Tsubaki Hinase, an honor student with an obsessive "sister complex" who keeps photos of her twin; the twin, Hiiragi, who resented always being compared to Tsubaki and chose a different, all-girls school, where the bubbly Ren Utsugi will not stop trying to befriend her; Matsuri Fuji, from a shrine family, who tries to do good deeds and becomes fixated on a crane game and on Yuka Kishiya, an arcade clerk who lets Fuji think she is a university student but is actually a first-year high schooler (and a smoker); and two gyaru, Hinako "Piyoko" Nakatani and Kaname Yamashiro (AniList; TV Tropes). Chapters are short, low-stakes comedy (shoe-kicking contests, forgotten umbrellas) with occasional bittersweet flashbacks, such as the middle-school moment that froze Rei's confession (Okazu; ANN; The Magic Planet). In the later volumes Rei and Kei's relationship starts to move forward (vol. 4) and Fuji realizes her feelings for Kishiya at the culture festival (vol. 5) (Penguin Random House blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why is it so hard to tell the person closest to you how you feel? Rei has loved Kei since middle school, says so only when she thinks Kei is not listening, and is misunderstood anyway (Okazu vol. 1). Around them, each girl hides something: a twin's resentment, a sister's insecurity, a clerk's real age.

**The answer the story gives.** Friendship first, and feelings in their own time. The comedy protects the girls from saying too much; the flashbacks show what silence costs (the aborted confession, ch. 49 per The Magic Planet; "It Was a Joke," vol. 2 per Okazu). By the last English volumes, feelings move into the open (vols. 4-5 blurbs).

**Characters as arguments.** Rei argues for love hidden in jokes; Kei for guarding yourself. Tsubaki is the comic picture of devotion gone obsessive; Hiiragi, of identity built against someone else. Fuji asks whether good deeds are good if they don't succeed; Kishiya shows that adults, and those who seem adult, are imperfect (Okazu vol. 2).

**Symbols and motifs.** The crane game that never pays out. The two schools and two uniforms that tell the twins apart. The first morning of a new semester, which opens the series and closes ch. 99 (TV Tropes).

**Traditions it echoes.** Four-panel school comedies (Okazu compares its rhythm to yonkoma), the "boke and tsukkomi" comic duo, and the yuri genre's long-unrequited crush. For faith readers, Fuji's motto of good deeds echoes many traditions' teaching on small kindnesses.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Friendship (communitarianism) is celebrated; the absurd is the comic engine; self-expression is the slow arc. Progressive views of sexuality are assumed rather than argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes are mostly gags with Rei and Kei; later volumes widen to the twins, Utsugi, Fuji and Kishiya, and the romance moves forward (vol. 4) before the culture festival (vol. 5). The Japanese run stopped on hiatus after ch. 99.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rei teases Kei instead of telling her how she feels. Why do people do that?
2. Hiiragi went to a different school to stop being compared with her twin. Did it help? What would?
3. Fuji tries to do good deeds even when they go wrong. Does a good deed have to succeed?
4. Yatsude's locker-room joke upsets Rei. When does teasing about someone's body stop being funny?
5. Which friend in the group would you most want to have, and why?
