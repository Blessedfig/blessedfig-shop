**Premise.** Reiko Kujirai, 32, works at Wong Loi Realty inside Kowloon Walled City alongside the teasing, easygoing Hajime Kudou, 34, whom she loves and who does not return her feelings. A photo of herself with Kudou that she cannot remember, and talk of a "Kujirai B," reveal that Kudou's fiancée, a woman identical to Reiko, died three years earlier. The city itself turns out to be "Generic Terra," a recreation of the second Kowloon (demolished three years before) generated from Kudou's memories and regret, peopled by copies of its former residents who vanish if their originals enter. Reiko, a copy whose original is dead, must decide who she is. Around her: Yaomay, a seamstress who remade her face to escape comparison with her famous actress mother; Xiaohei, a young woman who is the copy of a young man; Gwen, a former tea-house waiter; and Miyuki Hebinuma, the intersex heir of a pharmaceutical company, whose revenge on his adoptive father drives the corporate plot. Kujirai B's death is revealed as a suicide by overdose of "Infinity," a banned Hebinuma drug whose scandal the demolition helped hide (Wikipedia, anime episodes 1-13; ANN vols. 7-10).

## Life message

**You are your own person, not a copy of anyone else's past, and the only way out of regret is to face it and live in the present.**

| Pragmatic | Small daily skills keep people going: fixing up apartments, finding small quirks to remember people by, eating well. Reiko works out the city's rules by testing them (pulling talismans to find error codes, ch. likely 79-87). Clinging to an impossible past is shown not to work. |
| Religious | No faith shapes the plot. Chinese paper talismans hang on the walls, but in this world they hide computer error codes (anime ep. 10). A dead woman is recreated by technology, not by any afterlife. Faith readers weigh the strong affirmation of each person's worth against unmarried adult sex, constant smoking and a story where meaning is made entirely by people. |
| Socio-cultural | A love letter to Kowloon Walled City and to nostalgia, which Anime Feminist reads as also questioning nostalgia's links to Japanese imperialism in Hong Kong. A pharmaceutical company's cover-up and a rich family's use of people as replacements (Miyuki was adopted to stand in for a dead son) are the social villains. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction (via Wikipedia) reads it as a study of memory and identity through cloning, mind uploading and virtual reality. |

Outlook: Bittersweet: the original Kujirai is dead and the city that held Kudou's regret dissolves, but Reiko survives as herself and, in the anime's ending, meets Kudou again outside the walls (the manga's ending was not confirmed in sources opened).
Hope source: S: the way through is being true to yourself: Reiko refuses to die so that Kudou can be free and insists on becoming fully herself (ANN vols. 7-8; anime ep. 12); friendship with Yaomay and Xiaohei is the close second (F).

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame welcomes the defense of each person's worth, the honest look at grief and the warmth of the friendships. The divide is the adult content: an unmarried couple sleeping together, nudity, constant smoking and a suicide by overdose. Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint, Adventist and Sikh readings weigh that as accepted by the story (C or D), and several note the intersex and gender-variant characters as a teaching concern, while secular, humanist, Unitarian Universalist and Satanist frames read it warmly (A). Buddhist readers find a close echo in the lesson that clinging to the past causes suffering. Progressive readers praise the gender themes but flag the two unwanted kisses.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who face the truth and let go of regret (Reiko, Kudou, Yaomay, Miyuki and Gwen) are freed, while the projects that tried to recreate or replace people (Generic Terra, the Zirconian Project, the Hebinuma Group) collapse.

## Creator and publisher context

- Jun Mayuzuki (story and art), born April 27, 1983; her After the Rain (Weekly and Monthly Big Comic Spirits, 2014-2018) won the 63rd Shogakukan Manga Award (general) and was adapted as an anime and a live-action film (Wikipedia, Jun Mayuzuki).
- On-record intent: she had the idea for a Kowloon Walled City series while After the Rain was still running; she liked the subject and first learned of it as a child from the video game Kowloon's Gate (Netorabo/ITmedia interview, 2020, via Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes you you? Reiko Kujirai has a body, a job, a habit of smoking with watermelon and a love for her coworker, but her memories go back only months, and the woman she resembles is dead. If she is a copy, are her feelings real, and does she owe her life to the woman she replaced?

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, she is real, and no, she owes nothing. Reiko sets out to become a self entirely her own (ANN vols. 7-8), refuses Yulong's demand that she die to free Kudou (anime ep. 12) and helps Kudou face the regret that built the city. The city of nostalgia dissolves; the people who let go walk out into the present.

**Characters as arguments.** Reiko argues that a person is defined by present choices, not by origin. Kudou argues, and loses, for living inside a beloved past: his grief literally generates a looping city. Yaomay, who rebuilt her face to escape her mother, shows that self-reinvention can also be flight, and she goes home to reconcile. Xiaohei (male) learns he cannot return to who he was. Miyuki, adopted to replace a dead son, is the dark mirror of Reiko: used as a copy, he turns to revenge, then gives it up for love (Gwen). Yulong is the utilitarian voice that one copy's life is worth less than the original's freedom; the story rejects him.

**Symbols and motifs.** Eyesight and glasses: Reiko's failing vision and the original's spectacles that show her lost memories. The goldfish (named Success) that leads her out of the drowning city. Zirconia versus diamond: Yaomay's ring and earrings, a copy that still shines. Perpetual summer and a two-month loop: grief that will not let time move. Paper talismans hiding error codes. Food that erases worries, which ANN compares to the Persephone myth.

**Traditions it echoes.** Science fiction of copies and memory (cloning, mind uploading, simulated worlds), the Showa-nostalgia romance, and the real Kowloon Walled City (demolished 1993-94). For religious readers its themes echo teaching on human dignity and on rightly mourning the dead; for Buddhist readers, the suffering that comes from clinging; for existentialists, the self made by choice.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and expressive individualism are affirmed: the copy claims a self. Transhumanism is critiqued: technology that recreates the dead traps the living. Secular humanism frames the whole: there is no afterlife, only memory, friendship and the present. The sexual and gender content is presented as part of adult life without judgment.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-26) are a slow, sensual office romance with uncanny hints. Volumes 4-6 (ch. 27-53) bring Kujirai B, Gwen, Miyuki and the Generic Terra project. Volumes 7-8 (ch. 54-70) reveal the suicide, the copies and the first sexual scene. Volumes 9-10 (ch. 71-87) uncover the drug Infinity, the talismans and Kudou's role; the anime ends here with its own conclusion. Volumes 11-12 (ch. 88-108) conclude the manga; not described in sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Reiko is told she is a copy. Does that change whether her love and choices are real?
2. Kudou cannot let go of the woman he lost. What is the difference between remembering someone and clinging to them?
3. Yulong says Reiko should die so Kudou can be free. Why does she refuse, and was she right?
4. Yaomay changed her face to stop being compared to her mother. What did she gain and lose?
5. The original Kujirai hid her pain from the man she loved. How can friends notice someone who is hiding?
