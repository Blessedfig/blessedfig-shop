**Premise.** Yukari Marii, a middle school girl with purple eyes, sees every human as a robot, and can take apart and rebuild things (and bodies) as if they were machines; she helps the police assess suspects but has few friends. Her one friend, the boyish, naginata-trained Manabu "Gaku" Hatou, narrates (Japanese Wikipedia; Samantha's Yuri Garden). In the first part, the two are caught up in a dismembering serial killer's crimes; Yukari defeats the killer and rebuilds Manabu's severed arm with cellphone parts (AniList review). In the second part, Alice Foil, an 11-year-old genius (Samantha's Yuri Garden) who was abused by a mother caught up in drugs and a new religion (Japanese Wikipedia), arrives as a transfer student to recruit Yukari into JAUNT, an organization that protects and uses geniuses. A tragedy follows: Yukari is killed (Manga Bookshelf; AniList review). Using the phone in her arm, Manabu learns to contact her selves in parallel worlds and then to move among them, trying a near-infinite number of times to save Yukari and fighting JAUNT; across timelines she steals, kills, manipulates and betrays (AniList review). In one line of worlds she enters a romantic and sexual relationship with Alice, at least partly as a means to her goal (Okazu). At the end Manabu's many selves merge into something godlike that reaches the beginning of the universe; there Yukari survives and convinces Manabu that reality cannot be bent the way she tried, and Manabu returns to being an ordinary girl as her memories fade (AniList review). Reviewers find the ending ambiguous (Samantha's Yuri Garden).

## Life message

**People see the world in different ways, and loving someone means accepting them and the world as they are; trying to force every possible world to give you the one thing you want can turn love into something monstrous.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could try every possible world, would you do anything to save the person you love, and what would you become? Qualia the Purple starts with a girl who sees people as robots and ends with her friend becoming something like a god. In between it asks what a person is (a machine, or something with inner experience, the "qualia" of the title) and whether love can justify any act.

**The answer the story gives.** No. Manabu's love is real, but forcing every world to give it back to her turns her into someone who kills, betrays and uses people, including a child. At the end, Yukari survives and persuades Manabu that reality cannot be bent the way she tried; Manabu lets go and returns to an ordinary life as her memories fade (AniList review). The core message, one reviewer says, is about perception and humanity (Samantha's Yuri Garden).

**Characters as arguments.** Yukari argues that perception shapes reality: she sees people as machines, yet loves them. Manabu argues that love justifies any means, and the story lets her follow that argument to its end. Alice, a child genius abused by her mother, is the case of a person used as a means; reviewers find her treatment the book's worst misstep (Okazu). JAUNT stands for institutions that claim and use gifted children.

**Symbols and motifs.** Purple eyes and robots: how we see others. The flip phone embedded in Manabu's arm, the channel to her other selves. Countless parallel worlds as a picture of temptation: every option open, every cost hidden. The beginning of the universe, where the god-like Manabu finally stops.

**Traditions it echoes.** Philosophy-of-mind thought experiments (Mary's Room, philosophical zombies, Schrodinger's cat), the time-loop rescue story (a Japanese reader compares Life is Strange; a Goodreads reader compares Madoka Magica), and Faust-like bargains with power. Faith readers may hear echoes of the temptation to "be like God"; Buddhist readers, of craving and letting go.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialist choice is explored seriously. Naturalism is tested by the question of inner experience. Utilitarian trade-offs are rejected at the end. Transhumanist power is reached and given up. The yuri love is the heart, but the storyline involving Alice is the part reviewers, and nearly every tradition here, object to.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 (about the first third; vol. 1) is a lighter school mystery with a serial killer; part 2 (vols. 2-3) turns to Alice, JAUNT, Yukari's death and the multiverse, where Manabu becomes the focus and the tone darkens (Okazu; Manga Bookshelf).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yukari sees people as robots. Does that make her care about them less?
2. Is there anything you should never do, even to save someone you love?
3. Why do reviewers think Alice's storyline was a mistake?
4. What does Manabu give up at the end, and why?
5. If every choice happens in some world, do your choices still matter?
