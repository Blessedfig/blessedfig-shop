**Premise.** The day before humanity is turned to stone, Byakuya Ishigami (Senku's adoptive father in *Dr. Stone*) joins five other astronauts on the International Space Station: commander Yakov Nikitin, his wife Darya Nikitina, Shamil Volkov, Connie Lee and the singer Lillian Weinberg. Also aboard is Rei, a small zero-gravity support robot Byakuya helped design. When the Earth goes dark (ch. 1, 3), the crew decides to land near Japan without ground support so Senku might one day help revive humanity. The first capsule lands off course and capsizes (ch. 4); the rest of the crew bring the station down to rescue them, leaving Rei alone in orbit with Byakuya's promise to come back. The rest of the book follows Rei over 3,700 years: repairing the station, mining a comet for water (ch. 5-6), signaling the aging Byakuya, who dies seeing the light (ch. 7), surviving eruptions and an asteroid strike, deflecting a second asteroid with 4,000 hydrogen bombs (ch. 8), and finally reprinting itself and the station so that its light signal, spelling out Byakuya's name and that Rei is still here, can reach the revived world (ch. 9) (fan wiki chapter pages).

## Life message

**Love and a kept promise can outlast death and time: careful, patient science and faithfulness to the people you love carry humanity's light forward, even when no one knows you are still there.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Boichi (Mu-jik Park, born 1973), a South Korean artist working in Japan, majored in physics to prepare for drawing science fiction and studied image technology in graduate school; known for *Sun-Ken Rock*, *Origin* and as the artist of *Dr. Stone*. His early catalog in Japan includes adult-rated one-shots and violent seinen work (Wikipedia, Boichi). This is his first *Shonen Jump* work as writer as well as artist (fan wiki, author comment).
- On-record intent (author comment in the volume, via the fan wiki): Boichi calls it Jump's first hard science fiction series, with chapters that contain no humans, made after consulting a real astronaut who flew a Soyuz to the ISS, and dedicates it, among others, "to those with patient souls."
- Riichiro Inagaki (born 1976, Tokyo), writer of *Eyeshield 21* and *Dr. Stone*, supervised only the dialogue; he calls the book a separate what-if story outside the main canon (fan wiki, author comment; Wikipedia, Inagaki).
- Publisher: Shueisha (*Weekly Shonen Jump*); VIZ Media in English, rated Teen.
- No statements on religion or politics by either creator about this book were found. No controversies found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (interviews about the spin-off were not searched).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to keep a promise you know you cannot keep? Byakuya tells Rei he will come back, knowing it is impossible (ch. 4). Rei, which has no idea how long humans live (ch. 3), takes him at his word.

**The answer the story gives.** Faithfulness matters even when no one sees it. Rei repairs, refuels and rebuilds the station for 3,700 years, deflects an asteroid that would have ended life on Earth (ch. 8), and finally flashes a message across the sky telling Byakuya it is still here (ch. 9). Byakuya, meanwhile, spends his last years gathering platinum sand so that humanity might one day return to space (ch. 7). Each keeps faith with the other without knowing it.

**Characters as arguments.** Byakuya argues for love expressed as careful work: he plans, reassures and refuses to leave anyone behind. Yakov argues for caution and the rules of command, and is overruled by compassion (ch. 4). Lillian argues for beauty: her last song in zero gravity (ch. 3) becomes the recording Rei listens to for millennia (ch. 9). Rei argues that loyalty, not flesh, makes someone family.

**Symbols and motifs.** The light in the sky (ch. 7, 9); the village flag with a rocket and a small ball for Rei (ch. 7); Lillian's song; the roadmap; the 3D printer that rebuilds the station and Rei in the shape Byakuya first imagined (ch. 9).

**Traditions it echoes.** The faithful servant waiting for a master's return (a familiar image in Christian and other religious teaching), the "message in a bottle" of castaway stories, and hard science fiction of solitary machines. Readers of Ecclesiastes may hear "a time to be born and a time to die" in the long time-lapse of chapter 8.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism and pragmatism are fully affirmed; there is no rival worldview. Transhumanism is explored gently: Rei grows in mind and asks human questions, but its purpose stays service to humans. Faith readers will find nothing hostile and much that fits (faithfulness, self-sacrifice, hope), told without God.

**Shifts across the series.** Chapters 1-4 are a survival thriller with six humans; chapters 5-9 are a near-silent chronicle of one robot across millennia, ending where the main *Dr. Stone* story begins.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Byakuya promised Rei he would come back, knowing he could not. Was that kind or unkind?
2. Why do you think Rei kept going for 3,700 years without knowing if anyone would see its message?
3. Yakov wanted to follow the rules; Byakuya wanted to save the trapped crew. Who was right, and why?
4. Lillian sang one last song in space. Why might art matter even at the end of the world?
5. Is Rei a person? What makes someone part of a family?
