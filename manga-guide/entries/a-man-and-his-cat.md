**Premise.** Fukumaru, a chubby, flat-faced Exotic Shorthair, waits in a pet shop as his price drops and customers pass him over for cuter kittens; near his first birthday he has all but given up (Square Enix, vol. 1). Fuyuki Kanda, a renowned classical pianist who now teaches music, widowed and lonely, takes him home (Wikipedia). Told from both the man's and the cat's points of view, the episodic series follows their life together. Kanda's grief for his wife, Suzune, comes out gradually (vol. 3); he has not performed since her death and freezes at concert halls (Wikipedia). With Fukumaru's help he steps back into the world: he wins over a rival pianist, Hibino, through cats (vol. 4), overcomes stage fright to rescue a colleague's band gig (vol. 6), houses the prickly pianist Geoffroy Lambert and five kittens (vols. 7-8), reconnects with his adult daughter and son (vols. 8-9), visits his father-in-law (vol. 11), plays street piano and meets his old teacher (vol. 14), and helps Fukumaru search for his stray big brother (vol. 15) (Square Enix blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What brings a person back after the worst loss? Kanda has stopped playing, stopped performing, stopped living fully; Fukumaru has stopped hoping. The series asks whether two lonely creatures can save each other.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly, through daily care. Choosing the cat no one wanted (vol. 1) is the first act of love Kanda has made since his wife died. Caring for Fukumaru gets time moving again (vol. 2); love for a creature becomes love for people again, from rivals to his own children.

**Characters as arguments.** Kanda argues that gentleness is strength. Fukumaru argues that worth is not price. Hibino and Geoffroy show that envy and pride can be softened by kindness and by small creatures. Kanda's children show that distance in families can close (vols. 8-9). Kobayashi, the loud dog lover, shows friendship that does not depend on liking the same things.

**Symbols and motifs.** The piano (grief, then recovery, then rival for the cat's attention); the pet-shop price tag; the empty concert hall; the cat's point of view, which turns sadness into comedy; strays outside the window as reminders of animals no one took home.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese iyashikei and cat literature; the widower story; for religious readers, the Franciscan love of creatures and the comfort of mourners; for secular readers, the psychology of grief and companionship.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian care is the core and is celebrated. The market in animals is gently critiqued. Expressive individualism appears as Kanda reclaiming his music. No religious or political lens is argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: adoption, naming and the revelation of Kanda's grief. Vols. 4-7: rivals and colleagues, the first public performance (vol. 6). Vols. 8-11: a full house, Kanda's children and his father-in-law. Vols. 12-15: new characters (a teen who stopped going to school, rescue animals), street piano and Fukumaru's brother.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think no one wanted Fukumaru, and why did Kanda choose him?
2. How does caring for Fukumaru help Kanda with his grief?
3. Hibino and Geoffroy start out unfriendly. What changes them?
4. Have you ever felt "overlooked" like Fukumaru? What helped?
5. What does the story say about buying animals from shops versus rescuing them?
