**Premise.** Edo-period Japan, 1782 (fan wiki). Manji is a former samurai who killed a hundred men on a corrupt lord's orders, including his sister's husband. An 800-year-old nun, Yaobikuni, puts "sacred bloodworms" (kessen-chū) in his body that heal any wound, so he cannot die. After his sister is murdered, he vows to kill 1,000 evil men to atone and win back his mortality. Rin Asano, 16, asks him to be her bodyguard: two years earlier the Ittō-ryū, a renegade sword school led by Kagehisa Anotsu, killed her father, raped and carried off her mother, and wiped out her family's school (Wikipedia; fan wiki). Anotsu wants to unite all schools under one that values only what wins. A shadowy group of death-row convicts working for the shogunate, the Mugai-ryū, hunts the Ittō-ryū with equally ruthless methods; its worst member is the sadist Shira. Later arcs follow Manji's capture and the shogunate's immortality experiments on prisoners (the "prison arc"), Shira's return, the government's war on the Ittō-ryū, and Rin's final confrontation with Anotsu. The last chapter jumps to the Meiji era, where Manji, still alive, meets Rin's descendant (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; fan wiki).

## Life message

**A good life is not won by vengeance: the story follows a girl through two years of killing and asks what revenge costs, and it holds out compassion, even for enemies, and loyalty to the people who protect you as the way to live on after loss.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Kin revenge drives Rin, Magatsu, Renzo and others; each killing creates a new avenger (Renzo after his father's death, acts 14-20 and the final Shira arc). Sexual violence by men in groups (the Ittō-ryū attack on Rin's family; Hyakurin's captors, acts 53-57) shows male coalition violence. Manji's protection of Rin resembles a surrogate older-brother bond: she reminds him of his dead sister (Wikipedia, Manji). |
| Pragmatic | Anotsu's school prizes "whatever works" over form (TV Tropes; fan wiki). The shogunate's agents use convicts and torture because it works. The story keeps asking whether ends justify means, and shows the costs: the immortality experiments kill many prisoners (TV Tropes, "Surprisingly Realistic Outcome"; acts 99-134). |
| Religious | A Buddhist frame: Yaobikuni is a nun who says she cannot die "until I've saved the souls of all the poor sinners in Japan" (fan wiki quote), and Manji's vow is a scheme of atonement. His name and robe carry the Buddhist manji symbol (Wikipedia). Isaku, an Ittō-ryū member, is half-Portuguese and Christian (TV Tropes, character page), in an era when Christianity was banned. No religion is shown as a final answer; the bloodworms are never explained (TV Tropes). |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of bushido and rigid samurai hierarchy from two sides: Anotsu, abused by his grandfather, rejects honor rules (fan wiki), and Kagimura Habaki serves the shogunate with total loyalty and is ordered to commit seppuku when he fails (TV Tropes). Women are often victims (Rin's mother, Hyakurin, prostitutes Shira mutilates), but Rin, Makie, Hyakurin and Doa are also skilled fighters; Anime Feminist faults the 2019 anime for how often women on screen are "sliced up, raped, or both." |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Rin survives, ends her quest and later has a family, but nearly everyone around her dies or is maimed, and Manji walks on alone into a new century.
Hope source: P — the bond that carries the story is Manji's protection of Rin and hers of him; ANN's Kimlinger and the fan wiki both center it, and Dark Horse's editor calls Rin "the real heart of the series" (CBR).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The story ends with the vengeful and the cruel mostly dead, the heroine at peace, and the immortal hero still walking: those who lay down the sword (Rin, Magatsu, Giichi and Hyakurin) are the ones who get ordinary lives.

| Side | Worldview it carries | Outcome | How the story treats it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rin Asano | Revenge, then compassion | Faces Anotsu at the end; lives on in peace with a family; a descendant appears in the Meiji era (TV Tropes; fan wiki) | Sympathetic; her mercy is endorsed more than her revenge |
| Manji | Atonement by killing evil men; protecting Rin | Survives, still immortal, into the Meiji era (Final Act) | Sympathetic; his bargain is questioned |
| Kagehisa Anotsu | Victory by any means; anti-tradition | Loses an arm, stabbed with Rin's dagger, carried off by sailors, fate left open (TV Tropes); "expresses remorse" for what he did to Rin | Mixed: humanized, his ideology rejected |
| Kagimura Habaki | Total loyalty to the state | Blinded, killed by Anotsu after being ordered to commit seppuku (TV Tropes) | Critiqued |
| Shira | Sadism, cruelty for pleasure | Killed by Manji and Magatsu, eaten by dogs (acts 167-169, v25, likely) | Condemned |
| Magatsu, Giichi and Hyakurin | Killers who choose to stop | Magatsu returns to farming; Giichi and Hyakurin raise her child together (TV Tropes; fan wiki) | Endorsed |

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** No official popularity poll was found in sources opened. Reviewers single out Shira as the most frightening villain (Ash Brown: "the most unquestionably villainous and terrifying") and treat Anotsu and Makie as the series' most humanized antagonists. No Flying No Tights says the villains have humanizing traits that complicate the moral picture. The film and anime were praised mainly for action; Miike said he was drawn to Samura's villains with "realistic characterization" and to Manji as a "dark hero" (Wikipedia, film). Readers' affection for Anotsu sits apart from the story's final verdict on his methods.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does vengeance cost, and can a person ever pay for the killing they have done? The story begins with two bargains. Manji, who cannot die, promises to kill a thousand evil men to make up for the hundred good men he killed. Rin, sixteen, promises to kill the men who murdered her father and raped her mother. Both bargains assume that more killing can settle an account. The series spends thirty volumes testing that assumption.

**The answer the story gives.** It is skeptical. Every killing in the story makes a new avenger: Manji kills the mask-maker Araya, and Araya's son Renzo, who never knew his father was one of Rin's mother's attackers, swears revenge on Manji (acts 14-20). Rin hides the truth from Renzo to keep the cycle from passing to him (fan wiki). By the end, Rin has learned to protect even Anotsu from other people's revenge, and the survivors who put down the sword (Magatsu, Giichi, Hyakurin) get quiet lives. Rin's last answer to Anotsu is left partly open: sources disagree on whether he dies (TV Tropes says he was carried off alive and missing an arm). Manji's bargain is never settled; in the Final Act he is still alive in the Meiji era.

**Characters as arguments.** Rin is the argument that compassion can survive grief: the fan wiki calls compassion, "even for an enemy," the one trait she always shows. Manji is the argument that atonement by numbers does not work but protection does; his care for Rin, who reminds him of his dead sister, is what gives his endless life a purpose. Anotsu argues that the samurai's forms and honor made them weak and that only what wins should survive; his grandfather's abuse made him, and he shows remorse late in the story. Habaki argues for total service to the state, and the state discards him. Shira argues nothing at all: he is cruelty for its own sake, and the story shows where that ends, in a death the Slings & Arrows reviewer calls "prolonged and terrible."

**Symbols and motifs.** Immortality as a curse rather than a gift: Samura designed it as something readers "would not like to become" (Wikipedia, Manji). The bloodworms that heal everything but the soul. The manji symbol on Manji's back, a Buddhist sign; Samura asked Dark Horse not to mirror his pages, and Wikipedia notes mirroring would have turned it into the Nazi swastika. Severed and reattached limbs: Manji ends the series with Anotsu's sword arm in place of his own (TV Tropes). Snow, which falls on the last arcs ("Snowfall at Dawn," "Blizzard," "Sullied Snow"). Rin's "Golden Wasps" throwing blades and the poison hidden in her hair rings (fan wiki), the weapons of a fighter who is never as strong as the men around her.

**Traditions it echoes.** The chanbara sword film and the wandering-ronin story (Tange Sazen); the Japanese legend of Yao Bikuni, the nun who lived 800 years after eating mermaid flesh (fan wiki); Buddhist ideas of karma and the cycle of suffering; Western existentialist questions about meaning in an endless life. Critics compare Manji to Marvel's Wolverine (Wikipedia, Manji).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might-makes-right and state authority are given full voice through Anotsu and Habaki and both end in ruin. Existential questions are posed and left open. Buddhist imagery frames the story without offering release. Feminist readers find strong women fighters and also, in Anime Feminist's words, women who are "sliced up, raped, or both"; ANN's Kimlinger says the series' earlier climaxes were "marred" by "sadistic sexual violence." Faith readers will find atonement and mercy at the center, and also a cruelty on the page that many of their traditions ask families to avoid.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 are short episodes of Manji fighting Ittō-ryū members one at a time, with full-page kill splashes that later fade as the story turns toward Rin (CBR). Volumes 7-15 widen into the three-way war among the Ittō-ryū, the Mugai-ryū and the shogunate, including Makie's story and Hyakurin's capture. Volumes 16-20 are the prison arc of experiments on Manji and other prisoners. Volumes 21-30 follow Shira's return, the government's final war on the Ittō-ryū and the closing duels, then jump to the Meiji era.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Manji thinks killing 1,000 evil men will make up for killing 100 good ones. Can harm ever be balanced out this way?
2. Why does Rin hide the truth about his father from Renzo? Was that kind or unfair?
3. Anotsu says honor codes made samurai weak. Where is he right, and where does his "whatever works" idea lead?
4. Habaki obeys the state completely, and the state orders his death. What does the story say about loyalty to institutions?
5. Many characters who survive are the ones who stop fighting. What does that suggest about revenge?
