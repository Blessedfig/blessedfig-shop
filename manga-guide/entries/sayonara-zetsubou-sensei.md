**Premise.** High school teacher Nozomu Itoshiki takes everything in the most negative light; the kanji of his name can be read as "despair," so his class calls him Zetsubou-sensei (Mr. Despair). He keeps a suitcase of suicide tools and tries to hang himself whenever something upsets him, but his instinct for self-preservation always wins (Wikipedia). In ch. 1 he is found hanging from a cherry tree by Kafuka Fuura, a relentlessly cheerful girl who insists he was trying to make himself taller, as her father sometimes did (Manga Bookshelf). His class 2-He is a gallery of extremes: a stalker (Matoi), a shut-in who lives at school (Kiri), a perfectionist who turns violent (Chiri), a bandaged girl wrongly thought to be abused (Abiru), a girl who speaks only through vicious text messages (Meru), an undocumented refugee girl (Maria), a high-school-age housewife in debt (Manami), a boys'-love fan (Harumi) and others. Each mostly standalone chapter takes a phrase, custom or social trend to an absurd extreme to satirize politics, media and Japanese society (Wikipedia). The ending (ch. 300-301) reveals that "Kafuka" was the spirit of a dead girl, An Akagi, whose donated organs went to the girls of the class, who take turns embodying her; the Tropedia summary adds that the girls had attempted suicide before the story began and that Nozomu's class was a way of helping them, and Nozomu marries all the girls who carry "Kafuka" to devote himself to An (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Tropedia).

## Life message

**Laughing hard at the absurd and painful sides of modern life is one way of surviving them; and beneath the despair jokes, the story ends by showing a teacher quietly keeping fragile young people alive.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Rivalry among the girls for one high-status man (MangaUpdates Harem; AniList Female Harem), territorial fights (Kiri and Matoi), and his constant failure to die: the will to live beats the wish to die every time. |
| Pragmatic | Little is solved: Nozomu's rants list problems without fixes, and schemes (Kafuka's, Miko and Shoko's) backfire. The rare practical goods are small kindnesses: classmates care for Kiri, Maria and Majiru (character list). |
| Religious | Religion is satirized more than engaged: Kafuka leads cult-like groups and preaches impossible optimism; a Buddhist sutra story is turned into a gag when she offers herself to a tiger (ch. 4, fan wiki); an image of her mother being exorcised with a cross (ch. 7); a curse ritual (Chiri) and a shrine-maiden student (Ai). The ending is a ghost story about organ donation and the dead. Faith readers weigh constant suicide jokes and fan service of high-school girls against a finale about grief, care and life. |
| Socio-cultural | A satire of 2000s Japan: amakudari and bureaucrats, media, consumer manias, otaku culture, idol groups, hikikomori, illegal immigration and xenophobia, and Japanese conformity (Wikipedia; character list). The Manga Critic found it uneasy in its portrait of teenage girls as boy-crazed and manipulative. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the comedy is cheerfully bleak, but the ending reframes it: the despairing teacher has been helping girls who once tried to die, and the dead girl's memory is honored, though at the cost of a strange, sad final bond.
Hope source: P — P — protecting others: the last chapters reveal that the class itself was a way to keep suicidal girls alive and help them move on (Tropedia; fan wiki ch. 300); friendship and community among the misfits (F) is close behind.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Almost no one wins in the episodic chapters: schemers, hypocrites and the teacher himself get comeuppance. The finale gives the real verdict: the hidden work of keeping the girls alive and honoring the dead girl was the point.

## Creator and publisher context

- Kōji Kumeta (story and art), born September 5, 1967, in Yokosuka, Kanagawa; Wako University alumnus. He worked mainly for Shogakukan (Go!! Southern Ice Hockey Club, Katteni Kaizō) before moving to Kodansha after Katteni Kaizō ended abruptly; freelance since 2014; later works include Joshiraku (story) and Kakushigoto (Wikipedia). Kenjiro Hata was once his assistant (character list).
- Kumeta appears in the manga as an unshaven, grumbling self-insert who misses deadlines and nurses old grudges about Katteni Kaizō's cancellation (character list).
- Nami was first planned as the heroine (character list). No interviews on themes, religion or politics were opened; the ANN news on the finale's 15 extra pages was found but not opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Shōnen Magazine). Del Rey and then Kodansha USA published 14 volumes in English, rated OT 16+; the English run stopped at vol. 14.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with despair? Nozomu's answer, for hundreds of chapters, is to complain, list everything wrong with the world, and reach for the noose, then flee when death gets close. Kafuka's answer is to deny that anything is wrong. The series laughs at both.

**The answer the story gives.** For most of the run, the answer is only laughter: naming absurdity is a way to bear it. The ending changes the frame. The girls had once tried to die; a dead girl's organs, and in the story's fantasy her spirit, live on in them; and the teacher who joked about dying has been helping them live (ch. 300; Tropedia). Despair, it turns out, was the costume, and care was the work.

**Characters as arguments.** Nozomu argues that everything is hopeless and proves himself wrong by never dying. Kafuka argues that everything is fine, and her optimism is shown as spin and manipulation, until we learn who she was. Chiri is the case against forcing order on the world. Maria, a refugee, shows how much comfort the others take for granted. The fujoshi, the stalker, the shut-in and the hostile texter are each a social type pushed to its limit.

**Symbols and motifs.** The noose on the cherry tree (ch. 1). Kafuka's cross-shaped hairpin, worn by whichever girl is "being" her (fan wiki, ch. 300). Taishō clothes in a 2000s Tokyo. "Make himself taller," the cheerful euphemism that hides a family's pain.

**Traditions it echoes.** Franz Kafka (Kafuka's name), Edward Gorey, Japanese literary titles (Snow Country, Takamura's poems), Goodbye, Mr. Chips (the title), and the long Japanese tradition of comic satire. The finale draws on Japanese ideas of restless spirits who need to be released.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Absurdism is the method. Nihilism is the costume the story removes at the end. Consumerism and conformity are the main targets. Religion appears mostly as cults and rituals to mock, though the ending treats the dead with tenderness.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes (English vols. 1-8) lean hard on suicide gags and introductions of the class. Middle volumes broaden into social satire and running gags (Mayo, AKaBane 84 idols). The last volumes reveal the Itoshiki family's secret (ch. 297) and Kafuka's identity (ch. 300), ending in ch. 301; the final Japanese volume adds 15 pages (ANN headline seen in search). Only vols. 1-14 exist in English.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nozomu always backs out when death gets close. What does that say about what he really wants?
2. Kafuka finds a "positive" spin on everything. When is optimism helpful and when is it a lie?
3. Which of the social targets in the chapters you read seem fair to mock? Which seem unfair?
4. The ending says the girls had once tried to end their lives. How does that change the jokes that came before?
5. If a friend joked about suicide the way Nozomu does, what would you do?
