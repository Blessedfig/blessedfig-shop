**Premise.** Asako Suga, 26, a manga editor, is dumped by her girlfriend and wants a quiet place to rebuild her life. She rents a charming new one-story house with a garden, then discovers the lease said "monthly, garden included, landlord included": the landlord, Miyako Kitano, 19, lives in the attic (Wikipedia; AniList ages). Miyako turns out to be the former leader of the idol group Elm, who quit suddenly; she is sheltered, untidy and childlike, and Asako ends up cooking for her and looking after her (Okazu, vols. 1-2). A stalker incident tied to Miyako's past is resolved quickly (vol. 1; Walt's Comic Shop). Asako's author, the manga artist Hato Hatomori, is an Elm superfan who is stunned to find her idol living with her editor, and is drawn into the group's affairs and a teasing friendship with its new leader (vols. 1-5). Miyako falls for Asako (vol. 2); after a birthday that exposes how little Asako's past partners cared for her, the two decide to risk a relationship (vol. 3). They visit Asako's parents, where her father kindly asks what Miyako means to his daughter (vol. 4), and Miyako's family (vol. 5). When paparazzi catch Miyako with Elm, she and the group make a bold public plan to settle the rumors and put the past behind them (vol. 5) (Okazu).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do two hurt people build a home together? Asako has been dumped by partners who never cared for her; Miyako has left a career that controlled her life since her teens. The series asks what it takes to start again: a quiet house, a garden, and someone who notices you.

**The answer the story gives.** Daily care and honest talk. Asako cooks, nurses and listens; Miyako learns to care back, choosing a birthday present with great seriousness (vol. 4). Okazu praises the series as adults having meaningful conversations, and the conflicts are resolved by talking rather than drama.

**Characters as arguments.** Asako argues for giving, and must learn that she also deserves to receive. Miyako argues for freedom after control, and must learn to think of someone else. Asako's father argues for a parent's protective concern and is answered kindly (vol. 4). Miyako's grandmother Matsuba argues for thinking about the long-term future (vol. 5). Hato, the superfan, shows how fandom can turn into friendship.

**Symbols and motifs.** The garden and the attic: shared space and private space. The lease's small print ("landlord included"): life's surprises that turn out to be gifts. A houseplant Miyako is jealous of (vol. 2). Birthdays, once painful, made new (vol. 3).

**Traditions it echoes.** The cohabitation romance and the "iyashikei" (healing) slice of life; the adult yuri tradition of editors, artists and idols (Okazu, vol. 2). Faith traditions will recognize virtues of patient care and honoring parents, while weighing the same-sex relationship according to their own teaching.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is affirmed: both women choose their own lives. Communitarian ties (family, friends, the idol group) support rather than oppose that choice. Progressivism appears as quiet normalcy, not argument. The idol industry's control is mildly critiqued.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 sets up the house and the comedy; volume 2 opens Miyako's past and her feelings; volume 3 turns to romance; volumes 4-5 move to families, maturity and public life (Okazu).

3. Why does meeting each other's parents matter so much to them?
4. What did Miyako give up when she left the idol group, and what did she gain?
5. What makes a house feel like a home?
