**Premise.** Tsukasa Takamine, a cool, glasses-wearing high schooler, has quit her volleyball team and drifts through days of school and home with nothing she really enjoys (Seven Seas; AniList). She meets Shiori Sakura, a girl from another school who loves books and talks about them with an enthusiasm Takamine has never felt; an accidental book swap gives her a reason to meet Sakura again (Seven Seas). They spend after-school hours together in a relationship that is neither quite friendship nor romance (COMIC OGYAAA!! description; Okazu, vol. 3). In volume 4 the story turns toward love as the two collaborate on a joint club magazine (Okazu). In volume 5 Sakura wonders whether wanting her feelings returned is greedy, realizes what she truly feels, and gives Takamine a birthday present carrying those feelings (PR TIMES, vol. 5). Supporting characters include Hirose, Hatori and Hinata (AniList); MangaUpdates lists a love triangle.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to care about something? Takamine has quit her team and feels nothing much; Sakura feels everything about books. The story asks whether passion can be caught from another person.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly. Takamine is drawn first to Sakura's excitement, then to books, then to Sakura herself (Seven Seas). Love is shown as something that grows from shared time and honesty, and the story is content to leave it without a name for a long while (COMIC OGYAAA!!; Okazu).

**Characters as arguments.** Takamine argues that a cool surface can hide a person waiting to be woken. Sakura argues for passion, and later for restraint: she wonders whether wanting love returned is greedy (vol. 5).

**Symbols and motifs.** The swapped book; the club magazine they make together (vol. 4); the birthday gift (vol. 5); the color blue in the title, often used in Japanese for youth (from general knowledge; to verify).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese yuri school romances and the "book club" love story. Readers of any faith can recognize the value it places on friendship, patience and reading; traditions differ on the romance itself.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is affirmed gently: finding what you love is the good. Progressivism appears in the matter-of-fact portrayal of same-sex love rather than in argument.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (ch. 1-15): meeting, book swap, after-school friendship at different schools; vol. 3 (ch. 16-23): a bond neither friendship nor romance; vol. 4 (ch. 24-31): the turn toward love through the club magazine; vol. 5 (ch. 32-39): Sakura's realization and the birthday gift (MangaDex volume grouping; Okazu; PR TIMES; chapter grouping to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Takamine could not find anything she liked. Have you ever felt that way? What helped?
2. What did Takamine like first: books, or the way Sakura talked about them?
3. Sakura worries that wanting her feelings returned is greedy. Is it?
4. Why might two people leave their relationship without a name for a while?
5. What book would you swap with a friend, and why?
