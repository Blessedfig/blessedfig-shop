**Premise.** In a near future where humanlike personal computers called persocoms are everywhere, Hideki Motosuwa, 19, a farm boy repeating a year at a Tokyo cram school, cannot afford one. Walking home from his pub job, he finds a persocom shaped like a girl with floor-length hair lying in the trash. Her power switch is in an unusual place, between her legs, which CLAMP says was a deliberate plot point (Wikipedia). She can only say "chi," so he names her Chi and begins teaching her words and daily life. His circle: Shinbo, a fellow cram-school student with a tiny laptop persocom, Sumomo; Minoru, a 12-year-old prodigy who built Yuzuki to resemble his late sister; Yumi, 17, his coworker; Takako Shimizu, his cram-school teacher; Chitose Hibiya, his landlady; and Ueda, a bakery owner. Chi reads a picture-book series, A City with No People, that awakens her other self. Chi turns out to be a Chobit, a persocom with real feeling, built by Chitose's late husband as a daughter for the childless Chitose. Her twin Freya fell in love with their maker and broke down; Chi (once Elda) took Freya's heart and memories into herself. In the end Chi chooses Hideki as her "person just for me"; Freya reveals that Chi cannot have intercourse without being reset and losing her memories, and Hideki chooses to stay with her anyway (vol. 8; Wikipedia chapter list).

## Life message

**Real love is a free choice to stay with someone as they are, not ownership or gratification; and machines that only serve us can pull people away from each other.**

Hope source: L — the hope is love between two: Chi finds her "person just for me," and Hideki chooses her knowing what that means.

## Creator and publisher context

- CLAMP is an all-female group led by writer Nanase Ohkawa (born in Osaka) with artists Mokona, Tsubaki Nekoi and Satsuki Igarashi (born in Kyoto); it began as a doujinshi circle in the mid-1980s and debuted with RG Veda in 1989. By 2007 it had sold nearly 100 million books (Wikipedia, Clamp).
- On record about this series (Wikipedia, Production): the idea came from the group's own frustrating computer error messages, given a "sexier spin"; Ohkawa designed Hideki and Chi first; Chi was made a computer to heighten the "emotional discomfort" of loving something thought lifeless; Ohkawa considered an aloof hero or a sex-obsessed one and chose instead a poor, kind student. The art was drawn in ballpoint pen, 14 pages a week.
- Chobits shares a world with Angelic Layer: Chitose's late husband, Ichiro Mihara, comes from that series, and Minoru's late sister is Angelic Layer's Kaede (Wikipedia, characters).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Young Magazine, seinen). Kodansha USA's 20th Anniversary Edition is rated 13+.
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a made thing love, and can loving it be real? Hideki finds a persocom in the trash and treats her as a person. Every friend's story tests the same question from another side: a husband who forgets his wife for a machine, a baker who married one, a boy who built a copy of his sister, a girl who fears being compared with one.

**The answer the story gives.** Love is real when it is freely chosen and when it seeks the other's good rather than one's own use. Chi chooses Hideki; Hideki chooses Chi knowing they can never have sex (vol. 8). Minoru learns to love Yuzuki as herself, not as a stand-in (vol. 7). Those who use persocoms or people as things, like Kojima, lose.

**Characters as arguments.** Hideki argues for kindness and restraint. Chi argues that a made being can grow a heart. Minoru argues, at first, that a persocom only follows its program, then changes his mind. Yumi and Shimizu argue the human cost of machines that never disappoint. Dita argues that machines cannot feel and is quietly proved wrong. Freya is the tragedy: love for someone who could never return it.

**Symbols and motifs.** The trash pile where Chi is found. The picture books, A City with No People, a story within the story. The power switch whose placement ties love, sex and memory together. Matching rings (vol. 6). Citywide blackouts when Chi's heart reaches every machine.

**Traditions it echoes.** Pygmalion and his statue; Pinocchio wanting to be real; The Stepford Wives (named by the Encyclopedia of Science Fiction as a comparison); and CLAMP's own Angelic Layer. Some readers will also hear the old teaching that love is willing the good of the other, here pressed to its limit.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism is explored with sympathy but not settled. Consumerism is criticized in those who prefer products to people. Feminist critics turn the lens on the series itself: it questions the ideal of the obedient woman while also selling it. Hedonism is played for laughs, then set aside by the ending.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 are mostly romantic and sexual comedy, with the peep-show scene and Shinbo's affair. Vols. 4-5 bring the kidnapping and Chi's other self. Vols. 6-7 tell the friends' stories and Chi's origin. Vol. 8 resolves the question with Hideki's choice.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hideki found Chi in the trash. Why does he treat her as a person when others would not?
2. Shimizu's husband forgot her for his persocom. What do people today let take the place of the people around them?
3. Minoru decides not to restore his sister's memories in Yuzuki. Why does that matter?
4. Hideki chooses Chi knowing they can never have a physical relationship. What does the story say love is?
5. Critics say the persocoms are "perfect women" who never say no. Do you agree, and does the story agree?
