**Premise.** Fumi Manjōme, a tall, shy girl who cries easily, moves back to Kamakura and starts at Matsuoka Girls' High School, where she meets her childhood protector Akira Okudaira, now at the elite Fujigaya Girls' Academy, after about ten years apart (v1). Fumi is a lesbian; her first relationship was a sexual one with her older cousin Chizu, who is now marrying a man (v1; retold in v5, ch. 30). Fumi dates the popular senior Yasuko Sugimoto, who breaks up with her because she still loves a teacher who is marrying her sister (v1-2). Fumi tells Akira she was her first love (v3), confesses to her (v4), and the two date while Akira, who does not feel romantic or sexual attraction the way Fumi does, tries to understand her own heart (v5-7). They part after graduation, and in the final chapter, as young adults, Akira confesses her love (v8; Hecker). Around them, friends and family work through an arranged engagement (Kyōko and Kō, who finally marry by choice), a teacher couple (Hinako and Orie) coming out to family, and the drama clubs' plays (Wuthering Heights, Rokumeikan).

## Creator and publisher context

- Takako Shimura, born October 23, 1973, Kanagawa Prefecture; known for manga on LGBTQ+ topics, especially lesbian and transgender lives; works include Wandering Son (licensed by Fantagraphics), Scenes from Awajima and Even Though We're Adults; she also drew original character concepts for Aldnoah.Zero and designs for the anime Battery, and is active in doujinshi (Wikipedia, Takako Shimura).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, Production): she became interested in a story between girls while writing Dōnika Naru Hibi; wanted a few male characters for contrast; did not want to write about puberty; and said that when starting the series she felt like someone depicting sexual perversion for writing yuri. She scouted Kamakura with her editor for settings.
- Hecker reads the series as a homage to Nobuko Yoshiya's Class S fiction (Hana monogatari) that turns its older-younger pattern toward relationships between equals.
- No statements by Shimura on religion or politics were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Ohta Publishing (Manga Erotics F). English: VIZ Media (owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan; from general knowledge), rated T+.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes love real? Fumi has already loved twice when the story begins, once in a way that hurt her. The series asks how a girl who knows she loves girls, and a friend who does not know what she feels, can find their way to each other honestly.

**The answer the story gives.** Love is real when it is between equals and honest about itself. Fumi's love for Chizu was real to her, but Chizu was older, hid it and left; Yasuko loved someone else. Akira, who takes years to understand her heart, is the one with whom Fumi can be herself. Friedman praises the ending's ambiguity; the last chapter promises a shared future without tying every thread.

**Characters as arguments.** Fumi argues for honesty about who you are. Akira argues for patience and for love that does not have to look like everyone else's. Chizu argues, by her regret, against living by others' expectations, and by her history shows harm that the story feels but does not name. Yasuko argues that charm can hide a wound. Kyōko and Kō show an arranged match turned into a chosen one. Hinako and Orie show what a lasting adult life together can be.

**Symbols and motifs.** The blue flower (Romantic longing); Kamakura's old streets and the Enoden line; the plays (Wuthering Heights, Rokumeikan) mirroring the characters' loves; childhood photographs; Chizu's baby who looks like Fumi did.

**Traditions it echoes.** Class S girls' fiction (Nobuko Yoshiya), which Hecker says the series honors and revises; Jane Austen-like character drama (Friedman via Wikipedia); the shojo school romance; Mishima's Rokumeikan.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: being honest about whom one loves is the good. Traditionalism is explored rather than mocked: Kyōko keeps her engagement but makes it her own. Feminism is present in a world of women. Religion is cultural backdrop only.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-13) are reunion, Fumi and Yasuko, and the first play. Volumes 3-4 (ch. 14-25) are summer, weddings and Fumi's confession. Volumes 5-6 (ch. 26-38) are Rokumeikan, Chizu's story, the hot spring and the start of Fumi and Akira's relationship. Volumes 7-8 (ch. 39-52) are the relationship's strains, graduation, a parting and the adult ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Fumi says her love for Chizu was real. Chizu was older and married someone else. What does the story want us to feel about that, and what do you think?
2. Akira needs a long time to understand what she feels. How does Fumi wait, and is it fair to her?
3. Kyōko's engagement was arranged by her mother. Why does she choose to keep it?
4. Hinako and Orie come out to their families as adults. What changes for the younger characters when they see that?
5. What makes a relationship equal?
