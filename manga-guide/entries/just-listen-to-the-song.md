**Premise.** A high school boy uploads a love song to YouTube as a confession to a girl in his class. The next day she turns him down, and she has already shared the song with the class over LINE. When classmates spot what looks like a ghost in the video, he wants to delete it, but another student warns that deleting it would anger the spirits. The video goes viral worldwide as viewers find more anomalies and hidden meanings when the lyrics are translated or played backward. Encouraged by his mother, he posts a second song, which flops; he deletes both. The girl meets him and says deleting them was pointless because people had already downloaded them. She knows both songs grew out of the time he sketched her in middle school art class, and the two listen to the songs together (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A sincere song reaches the one person it was made for, even when a crowd online reads into it whatever it likes; honest expression matters more than viral fame.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who decides what a song means: the person who made it, the person it was made for, or the millions who watch it?

**The answer the story gives.** The crowd can turn any song into a mystery, but the meaning that matters is the one shared between the maker and the person it was for. The girl had understood it all along; she knew it came from the day he drew her (Wikipedia; Real Sound).

**Characters as arguments.** The boy argues for sincerity. The class argues for mockery. The crowd argues that hidden meanings are everywhere. His mother argues for chasing success, and the flop answers her. The girl, at the end, argues that real understanding does not need an audience.

**Symbols and motifs.** The video as a message in a bottle; the "ghost" as the crowd's projection; the deleted videos that cannot be deleted because others downloaded them; the middle school sketch as the hidden key.

**Traditions it echoes.** Death-of-the-author debates in literary criticism; Fujimoto's other stories about art and its audience (Look Back, Goodbye, Eri); internet creepypasta and fan theory culture.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Postmodern interpretation is shown as both real and absurd; expressive sincerity wins; consumerist fame is hollow.

**Shifts across the series.** One chapter: confession and rejection, viral spread, failed sequel, quiet reconciliation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The girl shared the song with the whole class. How do you think the boy felt? What should she have done?
2. Why do people find hidden meanings in things online?
3. Why did the second video fail when the first one went viral?
4. At the end, why does it matter that she understood the song?
5. Have you ever had something you made misunderstood? What did you do?
