**Premise.** Yukiya Asagi and Miku Sakura have been friends since they were small. In sixth grade, after Miku's real confession got an awkward reaction from Yukiya, they started the "Love Game": take turns saying "I love you," and whoever gets flustered loses (Wikipedia; ANN Preview Guide). Four years later, as first-year high school students, both are truly in love and neither will admit it, because confessing would mean losing. Yukiya drops his quiet-nerd image and borrows moves from Miku's favorite shōjo manga (and his sister Wakana's advice) to win; his uncle, who runs a café, gives love advice. Volumes 2-3 bring sleepovers at Yukiya's house and a student council president, Kazane Shirayuri, who asks to learn the game for her own crush (vol. 3); a game with a Pocky stick ends in an accidental kiss (vol. 4); they try being a couple for three days (vol. 5); Yukiya trains for a relay race, promising to say something important if he wins (vol. 6-7). By the chapter titles, they confess for real and become boyfriend and girlfriend in Games 60-61 (vol. 8, not yet in English) (Wikipedia; The OuterHaven).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why is it so hard to tell the truth about how you feel? Yukiya and Miku say "I love you" every day and mean it, but as long as it is a game, neither has to risk a real answer. The game began, by the anime's account, when Miku's real childhood confession got a clumsy reaction (ANN Preview Guide).

**The answer the story gives.** Honesty takes courage, and the courage is worth it. Each tactic (shōjo-manga moves, sleepovers, a three-day trial couple) brings them closer without ending the fear. The turning point is a promise: if Yukiya wins the relay, he will say something important (vol. 6-7). By Games 60-61 they confess and become a couple (chapter titles, Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Yukiya argues that a cool facade protects you; his introversion and self-doubt (vol. 5) show what it hides. Miku argues that teasing is safer than asking; her flashbacks show a timid girl who leaned on him (vol. 4). Shirayuri, direct and strong, shows another way to use the game: as practice for a real confession (vol. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The game's catchphrase repeated until it means something; shōjo manga as a love manual; the Pocky stick; the relay baton (Game 56 "Pass the Baton") as a promise carried to the finish.

**Traditions it echoes.** The teasing romantic comedy (Teasing Master Takagi-san; Kaguya-sama: Love Is War, as critics note), the childhood-friends-to-lovers story, and the old comic theme of lovers too proud to speak first.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics (courage, honesty) is affirmed gently. Expressive individualism appears as learning to show your real self, not inventing one. There is no ideology to argue with; the story's only target is pretending.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (Games 1-15) are gag chapters on the game. Volumes 3-4 (Games 16-32) add Shirayuri, a sleepover and an accidental kiss. Volumes 5-6 (Games 33-49) are the trial couple and the sports festival. Volumes 7-9 (Games 50-68, partly untranslated) bring the relay, the confession and dating, then a beach trip.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did the Love Game start, and why couldn't either of them stop it?
2. Is teasing someone you like ever unkind?
3. Yukiya copies moves from shōjo manga. Do stories give good advice about real relationships?
4. What rules would your family set for sleepovers between friends who like each other?
5. Why does Yukiya need to win the relay before he can say it?
