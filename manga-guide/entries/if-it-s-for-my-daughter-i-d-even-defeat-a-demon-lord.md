**Premise.** Dale, an 18-year-old adventurer with a big reputation, finds Latina, an eight-year-old devil girl, starving in the forest beside the remains of the man who had been caring for her. One of her horns has been broken off, the mark of a criminal among devils (Wikipedia; ANN LN review). Dale takes her home to the Dancing Ocelot tavern in the town of Kreuz, where its owners Kenneth and Rita become her second parents and the rough adventurer regulars dote on her (v1). Latina learns the language, works in the tavern, goes to school and makes friends (v2-4), meets prejudice against devils, and travels with Dale toward his home village, meeting another devil who tells her about her origins (v5-6). The manga stops there. In the light novels (not adapted in this manga), Latina grows up, tells Dale she loves him, and they marry (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes someone your family? Latina is not Dale's by blood, race or law, and she carries the mark of a criminal. The series asks whether love chosen freely can be as strong as blood, and whether a community can be family too.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes: Dale's choice to take her home (v1) is the turning point of both their lives, and the tavern regulars, the shopkeepers and her classmates become her family. The danger is not her origin but other people's fear of it.

**Characters as arguments.** Dale argues for self-giving love, and also shows how love can smother (v4). Rita argues for practical, firm care. Latina argues for gratitude and diligence, and her worst moment (breaking off her horn) shows how a child can blame herself for other people's prejudice. The teacher is the argument that fear of the outsider, dressed in religion, harms the innocent.

**Symbols and motifs.** Latina's horns, a mark of difference she tries to remove; the ribbons that hide them; rainbows (her first caretaker showed her one, v1); shepherd's pie and home cooking; the armlet that reveals her father's name (v6).

**Traditions it echoes.** Adoption stories from Silas Marner to Usagi Drop, the biblical and Islamic praise of caring for orphans, and the fairy-tale outsider child. Its source novels also echo the troubling guardian-ward romances that reviewers compared to Bunny Drop (ANN).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian care is celebrated; acceptance of the outsider wins; the fantasy religion is respected, while one bigoted cleric is condemned.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 are rescue and settling in; volumes 3-4 bring school, prejudice and the crisis of the horn; volumes 5-6 widen the world on a journey that reveals Latina's origins. The manga stops there.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Dale was not Latina's father by birth. What made him her father?
2. Why did Latina break off her horn? What would you say to a friend who wanted to change who they are to fit in?
3. The teacher said terrible things about devils. Where do people learn prejudice?
4. Latina wondered if Dale protected her too much. How do you know when protection becomes too much?
5. Who in your life acts like the tavern regulars, helping even though they don't have to?
