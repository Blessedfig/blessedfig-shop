**Premise.** Akemitsu Akagami was abandoned by his womanizing father and has spent his life trying not to become him: he studies to be a civil servant and refuses to date. When he falls for a girl he sees on the street, he panics at his own desire and goes to Mikazuki Temple to become a monk. The temple turns out to be a nunnery run by the Aoba sisters: Yuzuki (17), who is training to become head priestess so the temple will not be closed; Tsukuyo, a high school archer; and Kurage, a third-year junior high student. His father owes the temple ¥20 million, so Akemitsu stays as its handyman to pay the debt. Also living there are Mia, a foreign student fleeing a family "curse" of lust, and her attendant Kagura, who secretly schemes to turn Mia into the sexually dominant heir her family expects; the strict trainer Kiki prepares Akemitsu, Yuzuki and Mia for the priesthood. Through a fake engagement to save the temple, repairs, debts and constant sexual mishaps, Akemitsu fixes up the temple and becomes part of the family, and the household resolves to keep the temple running together (Wikipedia, episodes 1-12).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you escape your desires by running from them? Akemitsu fears he has inherited his father's lust and tries to bury it in a monastery. The series asks whether desire is a curse to be cut out or a part of being human.

**The answer the story gives.** Desire is universal, so running from people is no cure; what matters is how you treat others and whether you face life with them. The title flips his father's excuse (no one can live alone) into a truth about community (ep. 12).

**Characters as arguments.** Akemitsu argues for self-control and decency. Yuzuki argues for duty carried alone, until she learns to accept help. Kurage argues, comically, that lust is in everyone, even the Buddha's story. Mia shows a girl whose family tries to control her sexuality, and Kagura shows manipulation softening into care. The absent father is the warning.

**Symbols and motifs.** The temple image held together with tape: a tradition patched and kept going. The ¥20 million debt: the father's sins passed to the son. Prayer beads exchanged as Christmas gifts. Archery: discipline and aim.

**Traditions it echoes.** The harem comedy and ecchi manga; the Buddhist ideal of renouncing desire, and Japan's family-run temples (from general knowledge; to verify); the older story of a young man fleeing to a monastery from the world and finding the world there.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism wins the jokes, but Akemitsu never acts on his desires in the chapters covered. Buddhist ideas are explored without a verdict: the training is comic, the temple worth saving. Communitarianism is the stated message. Feminist critics (Anime Feminist) see women's bodies and nonconsensual contact as the real engine.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-36, the anime) set up the debt, the sisters, Mia and Kagura, priestess training and the household. Later volumes (ch. 37 onward, still running) could not be checked.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Akemitsu is afraid he will become like his father. Is that a reasonable fear? What makes a person different from their parent?
2. The series jokes that desire is in everyone. Is resisting desire good, bad, or something else?
3. Many jokes depend on someone being seen or touched without agreeing to it. How would the story change if everyone had to agree?
4. Yuzuki tries to save the temple alone. Why does she need the others?
5. What does "no one can live alone" mean to Akemitsu at the start, and at the end of volume 5?
