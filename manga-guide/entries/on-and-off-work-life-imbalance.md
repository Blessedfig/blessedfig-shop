**Premise.** Soutaro Amata, 27, is a sociable, popular office worker; his coworker Akira Hanku, 27, is reserved and thought unapproachable (AniList; Wikipedia). Off the clock, Amata dresses in lolita fashion as "Kanade" and Hanku in punk fashion as "Aki." They meet by chance in full outfits and become weekend friends without knowing they work together (ch. 1-6, vol. 1). A neighborhood cat, Little Mitsu, helps Amata realize that Aki is Hanku (vol. 3). Hanku's friend Tamotsu Roku, 27, a neat teacher by day and a punk style icon after hours, is jealous of the new friend Kanade (vol. 2). The two share their real selves and grow closer (vol. 3-4, ch. "Resolve," "Realization," "Hanku's Place"); when Roku learns Kanade is Amata, the three go shopping, and Roku asks Amata whether he has romantic feelings for Hanku (vol. 4, ch. 19-23, "Trio," "Confirming Feelings," "From Now On") (Square Enix product pages; Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Which self is real: the one at work, or the one off the clock? Amata is popular at the office and secretly loves lolita fashion; Hanku is cold at the office and secretly warm and punk. Each thinks the other half of their life must stay hidden.

**The answer the story gives.** Both selves are real, but a life is happiest when at least one person sees all of you. By vol. 4 the two have "shared their real selves," and their friendship has deepened because of it (Square Enix vol. 4 blurb).

**Characters as arguments.** Amata argues for keeping worlds apart to stay safe; Hanku shows that the cold face is a shield, not the person. Roku, a teacher by day and a punk icon by night, is proof that two lives can coexist, and his jealousy shows what friends risk when new people arrive. Little Mitsu, a cat, is the go-between that lets the truth come out.

**Symbols and motifs.** Clothes as armor and as freedom. The work suit and the lolita dress. The weekend meeting place. The neighborhood cat.

**Traditions it echoes.** The secret-identity romance (Clark Kent and Superman, in reverse); office romance josei; Japanese street-fashion culture. For faith readers, 1 Samuel 16:7 ("the Lord looks on the heart") fits the story's theme, while teaching on gendered dress (Deuteronomy 22:5) cuts against one of its premises.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is the story's center and is celebrated. Progressivism on gender expression is assumed, not argued. Consumerism is present (fashion and shopping) but gentle.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-6): chance meeting and weekend friendship. Vol. 2 (ch. 7-12): Roku and jealousy. Vol. 3 (ch. 13-18): Amata realizes who Aki is; visit to Hanku's place. Vol. 4 (ch. 19-23): the trio; Roku asks about romance; Obon plans. Vol. 5-6: not covered by sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do Amata and Hanku keep their hobbies secret at work?
2. Is the "work self" fake? When is it right to keep part of yourself private?
3. What did the coworkers get wrong about Hanku?
4. How does your family think about clothes and what they say about men and women?
5. What makes it safe to show someone who you really are?
