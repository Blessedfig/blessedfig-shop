**Premise.** A horror anthology of very short, loosely linked vignettes, most five pages or fewer, set mainly in and around a Japanese town. Each is labeled with a radio frequency, and stories that share a frequency continue each other out of order (The Comics Journal; Sportskeeda). What links them is Ogushi-sama, an old god connected to hair, once honored with offerings of human hair at a village shrine. As the pieces come together, the curse is traced to the Second World War: a soldier asked a friend to bring home a lock of his hair if he died, the friend substituted someone else's hair, and the real lock was later stolen and used in a curse rite (TV Tropes; Sportskeeda). Wealthy villagers had also misused the god to kill rivals and let a loyal woman take the blame (TV Tropes). Since then, people who neglect old warnings or are simply in the wrong place meet uncanny figures, warped faces, grasping shadow arms, crows that are not crows and creatures that live inside bodies; most stories end at the moment of revelation, with no rescue (The Comics Journal; Phantasmic). An early thread follows a girl whose grandmother shaves her head to protect her (vol. 1 ch. 1, 3, 5; vol. 2 ch. 28, Sportskeeda). Vols. 5-6 add "True Stories," first-person comics in which Nakayama describes strange events and an illness he links to making the series (The Comics Journal; Sportskeeda).

## Life message

**Wounds, broken promises and neglected obligations leave after-effects that spread and linger; the series offers no rescue, only unease about what lies under ordinary life.**

| Pragmatic | Keeping customs and heeding warnings is the only protection; those who smash the idol or skip the tribute suffer (TV Tropes; Phantasmic). Nothing practical defeats the god. |
| Religious | A neglected local deity takes revenge (close to the Japanese idea of tatari, a curse from an angered kami; from general knowledge, to verify). Nakayama says Ogushi-sama is not based on a real belief, though he draws on folk religion, mountain worship, Buddhism and Shinto (ANN interview). Readers of every faith meet a sacred power shown only as dread. |
| Socio-cultural | Nakayama says the stories are about people facing disasters and accidents and the psychological wounds that do not fade (ANN interview). Outlook: Bleak: victims are rarely saved, the god is never stopped or appeased, and the series breaks off unfinished.

## Creator and publisher context

- Masaaki Nakayama: drawn to horror by an uncle's scary-story gatherings; debuted in 1990 after a 1988 Afternoon contest runner-up prize for "Ridatsu"; his 2002 Fuan no Tane (Seeds of Anxiety) established his short, startling style and became a live-action film (ANN interview, 2023). He calls himself a scaredy-cat who avoids horror films (ANN interview).
- On-record intent (ANN interview, 2023): the series shows people caught in disasters and accidents and how they respond, with psychological wounds that do not simply fade; he aims for a slight disturbance each reader completes in imagination; Ogushi-sama is not based on a specific real belief, though he studies folk traditions, mountain worship, minority beliefs, Buddhism and Shinto; hair is believed in Japan to hold spiritual power; he leaves the creatures unnamed because a frightened person would not stop to name them.
- The True Stories segments (vols. 5-6) are part of the published work: in them Nakayama describes strange events at his workplace and an illness, and keeps open the possibility that they are supernatural (The Comics Journal). The Comics Journal suggests the hiatus may simply reflect a health problem; Sportskeeda repeats the online claim that he quit out of fear. Neither is confirmed by the author in sources opened.
- Publisher: Kodansha (Nemesis, a seinen magazine). Kodansha USA published the English edition, rated 16+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens after harm is done and forgotten? The Japanese title means something like "after-effects radio," and Nakayama says the series is about people hit by disasters and accidents and wounds that do not simply fade (ANN interview).

**The answer the story gives.** They linger and spread. A broken promise to a dead soldier, a stolen lock of hair, a god misused by the powerful: each sends out effects that reach people who never knew the cause. Most vignettes end the moment the victim sees what is there; no one explains and no one comes (The Comics Journal; Phantasmic).

**Characters as arguments.** There are no heroes (Phantasmic). The grandmother who shaves her granddaughter's head argues that old customs protect; the worker who smashes the idol argues the modern disregard that is punished at once. The girl who thanks a helpful ghost dog shows the one posture the story rewards: gratitude and care for the dead.

**Symbols and motifs.** Hair: given to loved ones, cut in penitence, offered to gods (Kodansha blurb); here it carries the curse. Radio: stories fade in and out like signals, and the narration between chapters reads like broadcast fragments. Faces with too many eyes or teeth staring at the reader.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese kaidan and urban legends, the tatari of an angered kami (from general knowledge; to verify), Junji Ito's Uzumaki in its single obsessive motif (Kodansha; Sportskeeda), and the J-horror of The Ring (Phantasmic). Religious readers will notice the old warning that idols and curses bring harm; Buddhist readers may hear the teaching that actions leave traces.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult is real and terrible. Tradition is vindicated as protection but not as goodness. Nihilism is explored as mood rather than argued. Nothing is endorsed except, faintly, respect for the dead.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 introduce scattered vignettes and the shaved-head girl; by vol. 2 recurring threads (the crows, ch. 22) begin to cohere around Ogushi-sama. Later volumes fill in the WWII origin and the shrine. Vols. 5-6 add the author's True Stories, and vol. 6 stops on a promise of continuation (Sportskeeda; The Comics Journal).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think the series never explains or defeats Ogushi-sama?
2. The curse begins with a broken promise to a dead friend. Why might a small betrayal have such large effects?
3. Old warnings in the story turn out to be right. Are there warnings in your family or community that sound strange but have reasons?
4. Some people enjoy being scared and some do not. What does a story like this do to you?
5. The author tells stories about his own illness and strange events. How would you decide what really happened?
