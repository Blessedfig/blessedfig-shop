**Premise.** 1878, eleven years into the Meiji era: the samurai have lost their swords and crime is rising, so the government has built Gokumonjo, an inescapable prison inside a giant tree in Lake Biwa. The Kumo brothers of a shrine in Otsu ferry convicts there: carefree eldest brother Tenka (24), who raised his siblings after their parents were murdered; serious Soramaru (16), who longs to match him; and cheerful Chutaro (12). Their housekeeper Shirasu lives with them. Above Otsu the sky has stayed cloudy for a long time, a sign that the Orochi, a demon serpent reborn in a human vessel every 300 years, is returning, and the Yamainu, a secret unit under the Minister of the Right, hunts the vessel. Tenka, scarred by a government drug made from "Orochi cells," hands himself over for execution to protect his brothers (vol. 3); Soramaru turns out to be the true vessel; Chutaro runs off with a killer to seek revenge; and Shirasu is revealed as the Fuma ninja leader who killed their parents (vol. 5). The brothers, the Yamainu and their allies destroy the Orochi together (vol. 6). (Japanese and English Wikipedia; fan wiki.)

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to protect the people you love? Tenka has raised his brothers since their parents were murdered, and he carries a terrible secret: the serpent's cells are in his body. He decides that protecting them means facing it alone, even to death.

**The answer the story gives.** Protecting means trusting. Tenka's solitary sacrifice (ch. 11-12) leaves Soramaru shattered and Chutaro set on revenge, and the despair it causes is what lets the Orochi take Soramaru (vols. 4-5). The serpent is beaten only when the brothers and their allies fight together, and the last chapter brings everyone back under a clear sky (ch. 26-27). Laughter here is not denial; it is the choice to stay hopeful together.

**Characters as arguments.** Tenka argues for self-sacrifice and learns its limits. Soramaru argues for earning one's own strength and becomes the family's center when Tenka is gone. Chutaro shows how love can curdle into revenge and come back. Sōsei argues for duty to the state; Kagami for violent rejection of it. Shirasu is the hardest argument: love for the family he lived with, against a clan duty that made him their parents' killer.

**Symbols and motifs.** The cloudy sky that lifts at the end; the rusty ancestral swords that seal the serpent; the prison tree in the lake; Tenka's iron fan; Gerokichi the tanuki; the scar on Tenka's back from shielding his brother.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Yamata no Orochi myth of the Kojiki; the samurai-twilight stories of Meiji Japan (Rurouni Kenshin is a common comparison; from general knowledge); for Christian readers, a brother who lays down his life and a younger son who comes home; for Buddhist readers, hatred as the door to suffering.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian and Confucian family ethics are affirmed. Bushido and duty ethics are explored: duty matters, but duty without trust nearly destroys the family. The spirit world is the setting. State power is treated with suspicion.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 is chapter 1 plus the 600-years-earlier prequel of Hirari and Botan (Manga-News; ANN). Vols. 2-3 (ch. 2-12) are prison and family adventures that end with Tenka's apparent execution. Vol. 4 (ch. 13-18) is grief, revenge and the Orochi's revival. Vols. 5-6 (ch. 19-27) bring Tenka back, reveal Shirasu, and end the Orochi.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tenka kept his secret to protect his brothers. Did it protect them?
2. Chutaro wanted revenge for his brother. What brought him back?
3. How do you feel about Shirasu, who loved the brothers and also killed their parents?
4. The government secretly experimented with the serpent's cells to cure illness. Was that wrong, and why?
5. What does "laughing under the clouds" mean in your own life?
