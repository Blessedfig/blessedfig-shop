**Premise.** Yuki Machida, a second-year in high school and manager of the boys' basketball team, is the eldest of six children (younger triplet brothers and twin sisters) with a father working abroad; years of looking after them have taught her to hide her feelings and expect little (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; ANN). She joined the team for the captain, Kido, and is crying alone after learning he has a girlfriend when the cocky first-year ace Sho Naruse sees her (Yen Press vol. 1; Japanese Wikipedia). He keeps her secret, needles her with rude and lecherous remarks, comforts her, and then sets out to win her (Wikipedia). Reviewers note that his early advances include unwanted kisses (vols. 1 and 3; ANN; The OASG). After rivals on both sides (his old friend Arisa, who kisses him, vol. 6; his childhood rival Shizuka Hakamada, who falls for Yuki), the two confess and become a couple (vols. 5-8; ciatr). Yuki retires as manager, passes her entrance exams and moves out to college (vols. 8-10); Naruse finishes high school and follows her to Osaki University, where both join the basketball team (vols. 11-14). The college arc brings new rivals (Hakamada again, the playboy teammate Suwa, Naruse's ex-girlfriend), rings (vol. 17), meeting each other's families (vol. 18) and, at the end, Naruse's chance to play basketball in America, which Yuki supports (ciatr; Yen Press blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to someone who has spent her life taking care of everyone else and expecting nothing? Yuki has learned that not hoping is the safest way to avoid hurt. The series asks whether she can let herself want something, and be loved for herself.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if someone keeps showing up. Naruse's refusal to give up is the engine of the story; through him Yuki learns to admit jealousy, need and love. Over 23 volumes the relationship shifts from his pursuit to their partnership: by the end she supports his dream of playing in America even at the cost of distance (ciatr).

**Characters as arguments.** Naruse argues that wanting something openly is better than hiding; the story also shows him learning restraint and maturity (vols. 13-16). Yuki argues for responsibility, then learns its limits. Hakamada and Arisa show love that lets go. Suwa shows how a missing parent can be mistaken for romance (Wikipedia).

**Symbols and motifs.** Basketball games as tests of growth; the manager's role as care given and not returned; Yuki's poker face; rings (vol. 17); her large, noisy family.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo "cheeky younger boy" romance and the senpai-kōhai love story; the domineering hero that critics see as an older shōjo convention (ANN, Silverman).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: being honest about what you want is the moral. Family duty (Confucian and traditional values) is honored but no longer allowed to erase the self. Critics read the romance's start against modern ideas of consent.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-8 are the high school basketball romance; vols. 9-12 move Yuki to college while Naruse finishes high school; vols. 13-23 are the college arc with new rivals, families and the future.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yuki is used to hiding her feelings. Why might the eldest child in a big family learn to do that?
2. Naruse kisses Yuki when she has not agreed. Is that romantic or a problem? Why do stories sometimes treat it as romantic?
3. How does Naruse change between the first volumes and the college years?
4. What do Hakamada and Arisa show about letting go of someone you love?
5. Would you support someone you love moving far away for a dream?
