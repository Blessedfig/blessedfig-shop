**Premise.** Hiroto Ikuta, 29, from Yamagata, gave up acting after failed auditions and works part-time at a fishing pond in Asagaya, Tokyo, with no girlfriend, no career plan and no worries. He shares dinner twice a week with Hanae Wada, an 83-year-old retired school cook; when she dies suddenly of a heart attack she leaves him her old one-story house (vol. 1). His 18-year-old cousin Natsumi moves in from Yamagata to attend art college and secretly wants to become a manga artist (vol. 1). The house draws in others: Yomogi Tachibana, a real-estate agent who lies to customers all day (vols. 1-3); Hideki, Hiroto's high-school friend, married with a baby on the way and crushed by a bad boss, whose work phone Hiroto throws into the fishing pond, after which Hideki quits (vols. 4-5); Natsumi's shy classmate Akari, who dates Yamada (vol. 4). Hiroto collapses from overwork (vols. 6-7); Natsumi takes a break from drawing, falls from a roof and is hospitalized, remembering Hanae (vols. 8-9); Yomogi turns down both Hiroto and the novelist Ishikawa, who casts Hiroto in a film (vols. 8-10); Hiroto helps Natsumi meet her manga deadline and promises to tell her what he wants to do (vol. 10) (ciatr; Wikipedia; Yatta-Tachi; Walt's).

## Life message

**A slower, kinder life of small pleasures and real attention to the people around you is worth as much as conventional success, and no job is worth your health or your friends.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a good life if you never "succeed"? Hiroto at 29 has no career, no partner and no plan, and he is happy. The series asks whether that is wisdom or avoidance, and lets people around him, who are all trying harder, answer for themselves.

**The answer the story gives.** Attention to people and to small pleasures is a real good, and work that destroys health is not worth it. But the series is not naive: Yatta-Tachi notes it is less cozy than it looks, with characters who suffer, regress and find no easy answers. Hiroto himself collapses from taking on too many jobs (vols. 6-7).

**Characters as arguments.** Hiroto argues for enough. Hanae, grouchy and lonely, argues that a single friendship can change a life, and her bequest proves it. Natsumi argues for the hard, anxious pursuit of a dream. Yomogi argues for money and success until she questions the lies they require. Hideki argues for duty to family, and learns that duty can mean quitting (vols. 4-5).

**Symbols and motifs.** The old house, a gift from the dead that becomes a home for the living. The fishing pond, where time slows (and where a work phone sinks). Meals shared. Natsumi's manga pages. A fall from a roof that becomes a turning point (vol. 9).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese iyashikei (healing) story, Ecclesiastes' praise of simple meals and work, the Stoic and Buddhist counsel of contentment, and the Western slow-living movement. Reviewers compare it to Inio Asano's and Taiyō Matsumoto's portraits of young adults, though kinder.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Community and individual choice are both affirmed: Hiroto's freedom is made possible by others' care and given back to them. Consumer success is questioned through Yomogi and Hideki. No religion is present; meaning is human and close at hand.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 set up the house, Natsumi and Yomogi. Volumes 4-5 deepen into Hideki's burnout and his escape. Volumes 6-9 bring Hiroto's collapse, Natsumi's crisis and accident, and Hideki's return home. Volume 10 settles the romances and points toward Hiroto finally saying what he wants (ciatr). The ending (vol. 11) was not summarized in sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hiroto has no career plan and is happy. Is that a good way to live? What would you need to feel content?
2. Why do you think Hanae left her house to Hiroto, who was not family?
3. Hiroto threw Hideki's work phone into a pond. Was that friendship or going too far?
4. Natsumi drank too much at her first college party. What would you want a friend to do in that situation?
5. Yomogi earns a lot by telling customers what they want to hear. What is honest work worth to you?
