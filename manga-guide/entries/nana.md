**Premise.** Two 20-year-old women, both named Nana, meet on a train to Tokyo and end up sharing apartment 707. Nana Komatsu, nicknamed "Hachi," is warm, impulsive and wants love and an ordinary happy life. Nana Osaki is the guarded, ambitious singer of the punk band Black Stones (Blast). Their friendship is the center of the story. Around it run Hachi's relationships with Takumi of the rival band Trapnest and with Blast guitarist Nobu, Nana's on-off relationship with Trapnest guitarist Ren, a tabloid press that hunts both bands, Ren's heroin addiction, an unplanned pregnancy and a hurried marriage. Flash-forwards set years later show that the group has broken apart and that Nana has disappeared. The story stops mid-arc because of the hiatus.

## Life message

**A good life is found in honest, loyal bonds with the people you choose, more than in romance, fame or getting what you want; the story shows that running from loneliness through sex, drugs or dependence on another person costs a great deal, and it does not say whether a happy ending is possible.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Mate choice drives the plot. Hachi is drawn to Takumi, a high-status, controlling male, over Nobu, the kind and loyal one; the pregnancy settles the choice (v8-9, ch. 25-29). Nana Osaki, abandoned by her mother, shows the lifelong cost of lost early attachment (v16-17). Pair-bond jealousy and possessiveness (Nana toward Ren and toward Hachi) are constant. |
| Pragmatic | Choices have results that cannot be undone. Unprotected sex leads to a pregnancy (v8); heroin leads to Ren's decline and death (v11, v19-20); an underage band member's drug arrest sinks a debut tour (v18). Junko's blunt practical advice is usually proven right (The Hooded Utilitarian). Fame is shown as a job controlled by labels and tabloids. |
| Religious | Organized religion is almost absent (from general knowledge; to verify). Read through religious lenses, the series is a picture of restless hearts seeking fulfillment in people, music and pleasure. It shows the costs of sin as many traditions name it (infidelity, drugs, exploitation of a minor) without a religious framework or a way of repentance or grace beyond human forgiveness. |
| Socio-cultural | Young adults in early-2000s Tokyo: punk and Vivienne Westwood fashion, the music industry, tabloid culture. Web Japan reported in 2005 that readers saw "the realities of life" in it and that its men fall short of a "prince charming." Hachi's path (marriage, motherhood, a husband's career first) and Nana's path (independence, art) are set side by side; The Independent read its appeal as "the juxtaposition between a rebellious character and a character aligned to traditional values" (via Wikipedia). |

## Creator and publisher context

- Ai Yazawa (born 1967, Amagasaki) began submitting to Ribon's manga school at 12, briefly studied at Osaka Mode Gakuen to become a stylist, and debuted in Ribon in 1985. Nana grew from two short stories she drew for the launch of Cookie; she made them related so they could be serialized (Wikipedia, opened). Vivienne Westwood clothing in the series partly comes from her own collection (Wikipedia).
- **The hiatus, from on-record sources only:**
  - Nana had an earlier break from June to November 2007 (ANN, 2009, opened).
  - In June 2009 Shueisha's Cookie announced in its August issue that Nana would not run "until further notice" because Yazawa had fallen ill with "a sudden illness" and would "need several months of treatment and rest"; the editorial department apologized to readers. Japan Expo had already announced on June 12 that she canceled her appearance for "health problems" (ANN, June 26, 2009, opened).
  - In the May 4, 2010 issue of Josei Jishin (as reported by J-Cast and ANN), Yazawa said she had returned home in early April from a Tokyo hospital and did "not know when or if she will return to working again" (ANN, April 25, 2010, opened). The illness has never been named by Yazawa or Shueisha in the sources found; this entry does not speculate about it.
  - A two-page "Junko's Room" side story ran in Cookie's March 2013 issue, her first manga after 2009 (Wikipedia).
  - In a July 2022 email interview with Da Vinci, Yazawa wrote: "It's been a long time since NANA went on hiatus. I am sorry that I still cannot restart the serialization" (ANN, July 7, 2022, opened). Wikipedia, citing a Nippon Television report (July 2022), says she said she was still recovering and was interested in continuing.
  - In August 2025 Heibonsha published a 25th-anniversary mook with a "110 Questions" interview (Mantan Web, opened; her answers not seen). A fan-news site relays a social-media summary that she said in a fanzine interview the story is in its final stages and she plans to finish it (AnimeMojo, opened); no primary source was seen, so this is unverified.
- Statements of intent: none found about the themes of Nana in the sources opened. A 2022 Yomiuri interview titled (in translation) "I love all the characters" is cited by Wikipedia but was not opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha (Hitotsubashi Group); Viz Media is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge). No reputable reports of personal controversy involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can two people save each other from loneliness? Both Nanas arrive in Tokyo at 20 carrying wounds: Hachi a habit of falling for whoever will hold her, Nana a mother who left her. The series asks whether love, friendship, fame or pleasure can fill that gap, and what happens when people try to make another person their whole world.

**The answer the story gives.** So far, a sober one. Romance rarely delivers what it promises. Hachi's need for love leads her through an affair with a married man (prologue), a breakup, an unplanned pregnancy (v8) and a marriage that the flash-forwards show failing. Nana's love for Ren is real, but it is entangled with jealousy and his addiction, and it ends with his death (ch. 77). Fame brings tabloids and exploitation. What the story treats as most precious is the friendship of apartment 707, and the flash-forwards show even that broken, with Hachi waiting each year for Nana to come back. Because the series is on hiatus, its final answer is unknown. Critics on all sides note that the open ending lets readers draw different conclusions (Anime Feminist, 2020).

**Characters as arguments.** Hachi is the argument for an ordinary life built around love and family, and also a study of how dependence leaves a person open to harm. Anime Feminist reads her as "an imperfect victim" who has "internalized the belief that she doesn't deserve to be treated well." Nana Osaki is the argument for independence and art, undercut by her own fear of being left. Ren is love undone by addiction. Takumi is control and career dressed as responsibility: he takes charge of the pregnancy but keeps cheating. Junko is the argument for plain honesty; The Hooded Utilitarian contrasts her advice with "platitudes." Shin and Reira show two lonely people whose relationship crosses an age line that critics read as grooming.

**Symbols and motifs.** Apartment 707 stands for the chosen family. Strawberry glasses, the band name Black Stones, and Vivienne Westwood clothing and jewelry mark identity and belonging. Cigarettes are a constant prop of cool and of self-damage. The name "Hachi" (a nickname after the loyal dog Hachiko, from general knowledge; to verify) frames loyalty and waiting. Snow marks Ren's death on Nana's birthday (ch. 77).

**Traditions it echoes.** Punk's rejection of conventional life and the London punk scene (Mantan Web on the 25th-anniversary mook). Shojo romance, turned toward realism (Web Japan: its men fall short of "prince charming"). Japanese josei realism about work, money and love. The Romantic idea of the doomed artist.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is given full sympathy and full cost. Hedonism is shown as an escape that fails. Feminist readers find two complex women but also a controlling husband who is not yet judged. Traditional readers can find a case for family and against pleasure-seeking, though the marriage shown is not a happy model. Religion is absent. Buddhist readers may see a long picture of craving and attachment.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 are a lively roommate story with romance and band ambition. From volumes 7-8 Nana Osaki starts narrating and the tone darkens with the pregnancy (The Hooded Utilitarian). From volume 12 flash-forwards reveal the group's breakup. Volumes 16-21 bring tabloid exposés, Shin's arrest, Ren's decline and death. The uncollected chapters 81-84 show grief and a first step back to singing, and then the story stops.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hachi wants an ordinary happy life. Why does she find it so hard, and what would have helped her choose well?
2. Nana loves Hachi and Ren but is afraid of being left. How does fear change the way people love?
3. Shin is 15 and Reira is an adult. Why do many readers and critics call this relationship harmful, even though the story shows it tenderly?
4. Ren's drugs were supplied by his own label boss. Who is responsible for Ren's death?
5. The story may never be finished. What ending do you think it is heading toward, and what would your family's tradition hope for these characters?
