**Premise.** Misao Harada has seen spirits and demons all her life. On her sixteenth birthday a demon possessing a popular classmate tries to kill her, and she is saved by Kyo Usui, the boy next door from her childhood, who has returned as an adult, as the head of the tengu (crow-demon) clan, and as an assistant math teacher at her school. He heals her wounds by licking them. Misao learns she is the "Senka Maiden," the bride of prophecy: drinking her blood gives a demon great power, eating her flesh gives eternal youth, and marrying her brings prosperity to the groom's whole clan (Wikipedia; VIZ). Kyo has claimed her as his bride since they were children; rival clans (the kitsune Kuzunoha, the snake Dodoji) and Kyo's cruel older brother Sho want her too. The middle volumes move to the tengu village and its politics, Kyo's family secrets (his mother's death, vol. 10) and a long war with Sho (vol. 11-14). The last arc follows Misao's pregnancy, the prophecy that the Senka Maiden dies bearing a demon child, and her choice to carry the baby, ending with the birth of their son Sou and her survival (vol. 15-18; Wikipedia; VIZ synopses).

## Life message

**Devoted love and family are worth any danger: a girl who accepts the demon who has always protected her, and a mother who chooses her child even at the risk of her own life, are rewarded with a family.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame welcomes the loyalty, the care for orphans and the sick, and the honoring of motherhood; religious traditions that hold strongly to the protection of unborn life will see Misao's refusal to end her pregnancy as the series' best moment, while secular progressive readers will value that the choice is hers. The near-universal concern is the romance itself: an adult who teaches at her school pursuing and sleeping with a 16-year-old student, with possessive behavior shown as devotion. Frames differ mainly in how they weigh the folk-religious world: Shinto and Pagan frames read the tengu and kitsune warmly, while Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness and Muslim frames treat demons as lovers and saviors as a serious concern.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who love and stay loyal (Misao, Kyo, the daitengu, Tadanobu and Renko) end with families and peace; those who use cruelty (Sho) or refuse to accept human-demon love (Raikoh) die or are left estranged.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is love when one partner holds all the power? Misao's life depends on Kyo: without him she will be eaten. The series asks whether she can freely love the one man who can keep her alive, and answers that she can, and that his protectiveness is the proof of his love. Critics argue the question is never really asked (Dacey; Silverman).

**The answer the story gives.** Devotion conquers everything: rivals, a cruel brother, a prophecy of death. Kyo keeps his childhood promise, and Misao, who begins by resisting him (Manga Bookshelf 2009), comes to choose him, then to choose their child at the risk of her own life. The ending rewards both choices with a family.

**Characters as arguments.** Kyo argues for love as protection and possession. Misao argues for trust and self-giving; reviewers disagree whether she grows (ANN vol. 11 notes her using her influence) or remains a victim (ANN vol. 8). Sho argues for cruelty and resentment and dies for it, though he saves Misao once and warns Kyo at the end (Wikipedia). Raikoh argues that humans and demons cannot love each other and is shown as dangerous. Tadanobu, who would not fight his brother for leadership and gave everything for a human girl, argues for a gentler love than Kyo's (Wikipedia; VIZ vol. 4).

**Symbols and motifs.** The black feather Kyo gives Misao as a talisman (vol. 3). Blood: food, medicine and power. The wound-licking that heals and binds. The Senka Roku, a scroll whose missing last pages hold the fate of every Senka Maiden (vol. 16). The crow's mask and black wings of the tengu.

**Traditions it echoes.** The demon-lover romance (Beauty and the Beast; Twilight and Vampire Knight, which critics name), Japanese tengu and fox-spirit lore, the shojo "dangerous protector." The pregnancy arc echoes older stories of a mother who gives her life for her child, turned at the last moment into survival.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Traditionalism is affirmed in the ending of marriage, heir and family. Might makes right is half-affirmed: Kyo wins by strength, but Sho's strength without love is condemned. Communitarianism appears in the village and orphanage arc. Feminist critics turn the lens on the series and find it lacking.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-5 (ch. 1-21): school, attacks, rival clans, the most sexually charged early chapters. Vol. 6-9 (ch. 22-35): the tengu village, consummation and the sex scenes. Vol. 10-14 (ch. 36-53): family secrets, the village, Sho's war; ANN notes a move away from sex toward a darker fantasy (vol. 12). Vol. 15-18 (ch. 54-72): pregnancy, the Senka Roku's prophecy and the birth.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kyo says he protects Misao. Where does protection end and control begin?
2. Kyo is an adult teacher at Misao's school. Why do rules keep teachers from dating students?
3. Misao chooses to have her baby even though she might die. What makes that choice hers?
4. Raikoh thinks humans and demons cannot live together. Is he wrong, or just shown badly?
5. Many readers love Kyo. What do they see in him, and what do critics see?
