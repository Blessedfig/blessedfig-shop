**Premise.** Nanami "Nana" Takahashi starts high school in Hokkaido hoping to make friends and, despite herself, falls for Motoharu Yano, the boy every girl likes. Yano is still grieving his older girlfriend, Nana Yamamoto, who died in a car crash the year before while out with her ex-boyfriend, so he suspects she was cheating and finds it hard to trust anyone. Nanami's confession, their dating, break-ups and reunions, Yano's secrets (among them a one-time encounter with Nana's younger sister Yuri after the funeral) and the quiet love of Yano's best friend Takeuchi fill the high-school volumes (v1-8). Yano then moves to Tokyo with his ill mother; after six months he stops calling (v8-9). The story jumps about five years: Nanami, now working, dates Takeuchi but cannot forget Yano. Flashbacks show that Yano's mother took her own life after a violent quarrel with him, and that he blamed himself, cut all ties and took his father's surname. He is found living with Yuri out of pity while her mother is dying. In the last volumes Yuri tells him her sister was never unfaithful, Nanami collapses from overwork, Yano rushes to her, and the series ends with his proposal and the two of them visiting Nana Yamamoto's grave (Wikipedia; EBSCO; Comic Book Bin).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you love someone who is still in love with the dead, and can someone who has lost love learn to trust it again? Yano cannot believe in love because the girl he loved died, perhaps unfaithful; Nanami must decide whether to love a boy who keeps a grave in his heart.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, but only through honesty. Every disaster grows from something left unsaid: Yano's encounter with Yuri, his mother's illness, his shame after her suicide. Nanami's faithfulness keeps the door open, Takeuchi's selflessness pushes Yano through it, and the truth about Nana Yamamoto, told at last by Yuri, lets him forgive the dead and himself.

**Characters as arguments.** Nanami argues for patient, unconditional love, though friends ask whether she gives up too much. Yano argues, by failing, that hiding pain to protect others only multiplies it. Takeuchi argues for love that wants the other's good more than its own. Yuri shows love bound to need and pity, and grows when she lets go. Yano's mother shows despair's cost to those left behind.

**Symbols and motifs.** The two Nanas, the dead girl and the living one, with the same name. Trains and stations, places of parting. Photographs and the looking-back narration. The grave visit at the end, where the living make peace with the dead together.

**Traditions it echoes.** The long Japanese shoujo romance, the separated-lovers story, and the coming-of-age drama. Some Christian readers will hear 1 Corinthians 13 in Nanami's patience; Buddhist readers may see attachment and letting go; the honesty theme is common to almost every tradition.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtues of faithfulness and honesty are endorsed. Existential questions about guilt and freedom are explored seriously. Expressive individualism is double-edged: following feelings brings both joy and harm. Duty to the dying is honored, but love built on pity is rejected.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-8 (ch. 1-31): high school in Hokkaido, first love, Yuri, Takeuchi, and Yano's move to Tokyo; the anime covers this part. Vols. 9-12 (ch. 32-48): about five years later, with flashbacks to Yano's Tokyo years and his mother's death. Vols. 13-16 (ch. 49-70): Takeuchi's proposal and Nanami's refusal, Yuri's release of Yano, Nanami's collapse, and the reunion and proposal.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yano hid many things from Nanami to "protect" her. Did it protect her? What would honesty have cost him?
2. Takeuchi loved Nanami but helped her get back to Yano. Is that the strongest kind of love in the story?
3. When is waiting for someone faithful, and when does it become giving up your own life?
4. The story shows teens deciding about sex and drinking. What does it show about the consequences of those choices?
5. Yano blamed himself for two deaths. Who could he have talked to, and what would you say to a friend who felt that way?
