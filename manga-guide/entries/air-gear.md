**Premise.** Itsuki "Ikki" Minami, a 14- to 15-year-old junior high brawler who leads his school's gang, lives with the four Noyamano sisters who took him in as a child: Rika (an adult), Mikan, Ringo (his childhood friend, in his grade) and Shiraume (about 10) (fan wiki; ANN DVD review). Beaten by the Skull Saders, a gang of Air Trek riders (motorized inline skates that let "Storm Riders" leap across rooftops), he learns the sisters are the legendary team Sleeping Forest and takes up the skates himself (ch. 1-3). He forms his own team, Kogarasumaru, with school friends Kazu, Onigiri and Buccha and the split-personality rider Agito/Akito (vol. 5, ANN), and climbs through team battles toward the Trophaeum Tower and the "Sky Regalia" that is said to rule all riders. Ringo, secretly the Thorn Queen of Sleeping Forest, becomes his rival; Sleeping Forest cripples Simca, the woman Ikki admires (vol. 15, ANN). The story widens into a conspiracy of bioengineered "Gravity Children," a military technology program and the Takeuchi twins, Sora and Nike, who plan to rule through the Tower. In the final volume Ikki and Ringo fight to stop Sora's plan to endanger the world, with street gangs racing to help (Kodansha USA, vol. 37).

## Life message

**Freedom is worth fighting for: a kid who refuses to stay on the ground, backed by a loyal crew, can reach the sky, while those who use strength to rule others fall.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to be free? Ikki is the toughest kid in his school but stuck on the ground; Air Treks let him fly, and the series keeps asking whether the Sky is a place of freedom or a throne someone will try to seize.

**The answer the story gives.** Freedom is won together and lost when someone tries to own it. Ikki's way up runs through his friends: Kazu, Onigiri and Buccha, who cannot keep up at first (vol. 5, ANN), and the gangs who race to help at the end (vol. 37, Kodansha). Sora's way is to rule all riders through the Tower, and he falls. Reviewers found the answer muddled by exposition (ANN vol. 15).

**Characters as arguments.** Ikki argues for raw will and loyalty. Ringo argues for duty to a team and a code, even at the cost of hurting the people she loves; she crippled Simca on her team's orders and almost kills Ikki before choosing him (fan wiki). Simca argues for a gentler freedom (she keeps weaker teams out of danger). Sora, bullied as a child, argues that bystanders deserve to perish, a dark mirror of Ikki's own fight against bullies (fan wiki). Agito is a child made into a weapon, split into three selves.

**Symbols and motifs.** Wings and crows (Ikki's pet crow Kuu, the team name Kogarasumaru, "little crow"); the Tower; Regalia as crowns; wheels that let the ground become sky.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Icarus story and the tower of Babel (climbing to the sky); delinquent manga; shonen tournament ranking. A Christian reader may set the Tower beside Babel; a Buddhist reader may ask whether the Sky is craving.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is celebrated. Might makes right is the system the riders live in, honored in tournaments and condemned in Sora. Transhumanism is critiqued: the Gravity Children program treats kids as tools. Hedonism appears as fan service and thrill-seeking, not as an argument.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5: school gang comedy and the founding of Kogarasumaru. Volumes 6-16: team battles, Ringo's secret, Simca crippled. Volumes 17-30: the Parts War, Genesis and the Takeuchi twins, deaths of adult riders. Volumes 31-37: the Tower and the conspiracy; the ending was felt to be rushed by some readers (MangaUpdates category Axed/Cancelled has 3 votes; to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ikki wants to fly. What does freedom mean to you, and what limits does it need?
2. Sora was bullied and decided bystanders deserve to suffer. How is Ikki's answer to bullies different?
3. Ringo hurt someone because her team told her to. When is loyalty wrong?
4. The book plays peeping and groping as jokes. How would those scenes feel to the people on the receiving end?
5. Adults in the story use gifted children as tools. Who protects the kids, and who should?
