**Premise.** Ryouta Murakami, a high school second-year with a photographic memory, has spent ten years trying to prove that aliens exist, the promise he made to his childhood friend Kuroneko before she died in a fall that also injured him. Then Neko Kuroha transfers into his class: she looks exactly like Kuroneko, says she has never met him, and saves him from a rockslide with superhuman strength (ch. 1). She is a "magic-user" (witch) who escaped from Vingulf, a secret lab that implants teenage girls with alien parasites (Drasils) through a device on the neck, the harness. Each power use costs her memories, and the girls die horribly within hours unless they take a "death suppressant" pill (ch. 1-12). Ryouta shelters Neko and the other escapees (Kana, Kazumi, Kotori, Hatsuna) in his astronomy club's observatory and outwits the lab's assassin witches, while uncovering that the lab, run by his own supposedly dead father, means to revive an alien "god" to destroy and remake humanity (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Many witches die; Ryouta finally lets himself be devoured to stop the revived Loki (ch. 180), and a time-skip epilogue shows the survivors living on (ch. 181) (TV Tropes; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Keeping your promises and protecting people the powerful treat as disposable is worth any cost; no institution has the right to rank human lives and use them as tools.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

The villains plan a new "Adam" and "Eve" for a remade humanity. |
| Pragmatic | Ryouta wins by memory, logic and planning rather than power: he traces the pill's factory code from memory, has Kotori tie herself to a pole to swap places with an assassin, and builds schemes against stronger witches (Wikipedia, episodes 3-6; Kotaku). |
| Religious | The lab borrows Norse myth (Valkyria, Brynhildr, Loki, Skadi, Freya, Grane, Drasil from Yggdrasil) and Genesis imagery (a new Adam and Eve) for a project to destroy existing gods and remake humanity; the resistance group defected because of that threat (Wikipedia). Ryouta's father indoctrinated him with religious ideas (Wikipedia). The "magic" is alien biology, not occult practice. No faith is shown as true or practiced by the heroes. |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of secret science and of institutions that rank people by usefulness: witches are graded from C to SS and "failures" are disposed of (Wikipedia). It also carries the seinen harem conventions of its magazine, which critics fault (THEM; ANN Preview Guide). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the end of humanity is stopped and several witches survive to live ordinary lives, but Kotori, Mina, Nanami and many other girls die, Ryouta gives himself up to stop Loki (ch. 180), and the epilogue leaves several fates unresolved (TV Tropes; Wikipedia).
Hope source: P — protecting others: Ryouta's whole story is sheltering girls the lab would kill, and it ends with his self-sacrifice to save the world (ch. 180); love between two people (L, Ryouta and Neko joined in the end, Wikipedia) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the core message: protect the people others treat as disposable, keep your word, and distrust secret power that plays God. The split is over what surrounds it. Most Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint and Adventist readings weigh the harem comedy and nudity centered on teenage girls, played as fun, as part of what the work endorses (C or D), and some add the witches and the god-killing plot. Progressive and feminist readings agree on that point from their own side: the story sexualizes the same victims it defends. Secular humanist, atheist, agnostic and Satanist readers welcome the rational hero and the exposure of god-like authority (B) but share the concern about cruelty and fan service.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a life worth when someone with power has decided it is worth nothing? The lab grades its girls like products and destroys the ones it calls failures. Ryouta, who failed to save one girl as a child, decides that none of them is disposable.

**The answer the story gives.** Every life is worth saving, and a promise is worth keeping at any cost. Ryouta uses his memory and logic to win against enemies far stronger than his friends (Kotaku), and in the end gives his own life to stop the god the lab revived (ch. 180). The cost is high: many of the girls he shelters die anyway.

**Characters as arguments.** Ryouta argues for reason in the service of love. Chisato argues for science without conscience, and shows a sliver of humanity only for his dead sister. Miki argues the hard utilitarian case that all witches must die to save the world, and keeps her word. Kazumi argues for living fully before death, which the story plays both for comedy and for pathos. Kotori and Chie argue by their deaths that a life given for a friend is not wasted. Takachiho and Makina argue that power makes gods, and are destroyed by what they made.

**Symbols and motifs.** The stars and the search for aliens; the harness on the neck and its eject button; the pills counted out each day; Neko's notebook, which remembers her love when she cannot; the Norse names (a Valkyrie chooses who dies in battle); the new "Adam" and "Eve."

**Traditions it echoes.** *Elfen Lied* above all (critics compare them constantly), the Frankenstein warning against science that plays God, the Norse twilight of the gods, and the Genesis story of a first man and woman, which the villains try to remake. Readers from many faiths will recognize the conviction that each person bears a worth no institution can assign.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanism, utilitarianism and strength-based ranking are the villains' creeds and are condemned. Duty and loyalty are the heroes' creed and are honored. The harem and sexual comedy sit beside this without judgment, which is where most frames and critics part ways with the series.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-10 (ch. 1-100) are the hunt for pills and the war with Valkyria, ending with Neko's loss of memory; this is the part the anime adapts, with a different ending. Volumes 11-15 (ch. 101-150) widen to the lab's origins (1910 Germany, ch. 115), Ryouta's father and brother, and global conspiracy. Volumes 16-18 (ch. 151-181) bring the revival of Loki, the end-of-the-world battle and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The lab ranks the girls and kills the "failures." Where do people rank each other in real life, and what does it cost?
2. Ryouta keeps a promise for ten years. Is there a promise you would keep that long?
3. Miki says some must die so the world can live. Ryouta disagrees. Who is right?
4. The story criticizes how the lab treats girls, but also shows the girls in fan-service scenes. Do those fit together?
5. Neko loses her memories but still loves Ryouta. What makes a person who they are?
