**Premise.** Seven Japanese high-school students, each the best in the world at something, survive a plane crash and wake in Freyjagard, a medieval fantasy empire with magic and beast-folk (Byuma): Tsukasa Mikogami, a teenage prime minister; Ringo Ōhoshi, an inventor (the youngest; 13 per the fan wiki, to verify); Shinobu Sarutobi, a ninja journalist; Keine Kanzaki, a doctor; Aoi Ichijō, a swordswoman; Prince Akatsuki, a stage magician; and Masato Sanada, a businessman. Rescued by the hunter Winona and her adopted elf daughter Lyrule, they repay Elm Village by modernizing it: Masato breaks a merchant monopoly and buys the freedom of Roo, an escaped slave girl (v1, ch. 6-8). When a lord's knight tries to burn the village and take Lyrule as a concubine, they lead an uprising (v2). To win over the people they found a religion, the Seven-Light Faith, with Akatsuki posing as its god and the others as angels (v3, "they're first going...to church?!" per Yen's blurb), fight the Empire's dukes with modern weapons (v4-6), and found the Republic of Elm, with free elections (v7-11). The Empire's nobility holds a social Darwinist creed that the strong rule and the weak obey, and the state suppresses religion (Wikipedia). Later volumes take them to the Yamato Dominion, where Princess Mayoi controls her people's minds, and finally to the truth of the "wicked dragon" and of Yggdra, who summoned them (v9-13, Yen blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What should the most gifted people do with their gifts? Seven teenagers who could rule any world land in one ruled by nobles who think strength gives the right to rule.

**The answer the story gives.** Use them for ordinary people, and then hand power to the people. The prodigies repay a village's kindness, free a slave girl, break monopolies and found a republic with elections.

**Characters as arguments.** Tsukasa argues for public service; his own father's corruption, which he exposed, is the warning (v1, ch. 5). Masato argues that fair trade beats exploitation. Keine argues for science without God and goes too far (the brain surgery). Akatsuki's "godhood" argues that people follow wonders. The nobles argue that the strong deserve to rule and lose. Mayoi argues for peace by controlling minds and is defeated.

**Symbols and motifs.** Mayonnaise (the first "miracle"); the seven lights of an old faith; the dragon legend; the missile as the last argument.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Connecticut Yankee story, Enlightenment revolutions and the American and French founding myths; the Exodus pattern of liberation. Its use of a staged religion echoes the old political idea of a "noble lie."

**How each lens is portrayed.** Liberalism and pragmatism win; the might-makes-right creed is defeated; secular humanism frames the heroes, but the final volumes bring a real goddess into the story (v13), so the world is not simply godless.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: village rescue and trade. Vols. 3-6: the staged faith and the war with the dukes. Vols. 7-8: the Republic of Elm, romance and currency. Vols. 9-12: Yamato and the elections. Vol. 13: the dragon and Yggdra.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Tsukasa exposed his own father's corruption. Was that the right thing to do?
2. The prodigies start a religion they do not believe in to win people over. Is that ever acceptable?
3. Masato buys Roo's freedom. What would it take to free all the slaves?
4. Keine operates on a man's brain to make him honest. Where is the line between helping and controlling?
5. Why does the story care so much about elections?
