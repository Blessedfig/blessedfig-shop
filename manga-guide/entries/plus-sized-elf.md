**Premise.** Naoe Tomoatsu is a seitai (Japanese bodywork) massage therapist at the Smiley Boar clinic. One night his last client is Elfuda, an elf from another world who got hooked on French fries during a visit to Earth and cannot fit back through the portal home until she returns to her arrival weight. His diet and exercise plan works, she goes home, and she is soon back, heavier than ever. More overweight visitors from her world follow: the dark elf Kuroeda, the flower-headed alraune Kusahanada, the beer-loving ogre Oga, the mermaid Mero, the werewolf Laika, the orc Oku, the kobold Kobo (adult but childlike in looks), the skeleton Honeda, the dragon Gonda, the cyclops Hitome and Elfuda's aunt Oeda. A satyr, Satero, who feeds on human desire, makes Naoe overeat and becomes the recurring antagonist; to protect him, the visitors move into his apartment. Naoe's 40-year-old boss, Akiho, drinks hard and is unaware the clients are not human. Later volumes bring karaoke (vol. 5), a dream-eater and the beach (vol. 7) and a summer island trip where Elfuda announces she will go home (vol. 8) (Wikipedia; Seven Seas synopses; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Health comes from steady habits and patient, non-judging help rather than shame or crash diets, and everyone backslides sometimes; the series tells this through comedy that keeps its camera on the characters' bodies.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Why is it so hard to change a habit, and what kind of help actually works? Elfuda has a simple goal, losing enough weight to fit through the portal home, and a simple obstacle, French fries.

**The answer the story gives.** Steady habits and a helper who never shames you. Naoe gives clear, practical advice, and when his clients slip, he starts again with them; ANN's McNulty notes he never shames the women even when they break their diets. Failure is normal and forgiven.

**Characters as arguments.** Naoe argues for patience and method. Elfuda argues, by backsliding, that appetite and pride are stubborn. Satero, who feeds on desire, is temptation in person. Oga and Akiho show drinking as a cause of weight gain, though the story laughs rather than warns. Oeda, sent to fetch Elfuda home, falls to soft-serve herself.

**Symbols and motifs.** The portal that only lets you through at your arrival weight. French fries as the forbidden fruit. The massage table, where health care and fan service meet.

**Traditions it echoes.** The monster-girl comedy (Monster Musume; Interviews with Monster Girls, from general knowledge; to verify) and diet-and-fitness manga such as How Heavy Are the Dumbbells You Lift? (from general knowledge; to verify). Its stated values echo the old virtue of temperance found in many traditions; its art follows the fan-service conventions of seinen ecchi.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism is affirmed in the advice; hedonism is both the problem and the pleasure; consumerism (junk food) is the trap. Critics' feminist and body-image readings are split, and the series takes no side.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: Elfuda, Kuroeda, Kusahanada, Oga and Mero. Vols. 2-4: Laika, Oku, Kobo, the satyr and the move into Naoe's apartment. Vols. 5-7: outings (karaoke, beach) and new visitors. Vol. 8: a summer island trip and Elfuda's decision to go home, followed by the separate sequel.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Naoe never makes fun of his clients when they fail. Why does that matter?
2. Why do the characters keep going back to fries and beer even when they want to change?
3. Some critics say the series celebrates bigger bodies; others say it mocks them. What do you think, and why?
4. Which of Naoe's tips are things a real person could do?
5. The boss gropes Naoe when she is drunk and the story treats it as a joke. Is it funny? Would it be funny if the roles were reversed?
