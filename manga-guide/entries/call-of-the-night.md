**Premise.** Ko Yamori, 14, a second-year junior high student and former honor student, cannot sleep and has stopped going to school after rejecting a classmate's confession and being harassed by her friends (ch. 1, fan wiki). Wandering the streets at night, he meets Nazuna Nanakusa, a vampire who looks like a teenager but is estimated to be 30 to 40 years old. She takes him to her apartment, and he wakes to her drinking his blood. In this story a human becomes a vampire only if bitten by a vampire he loves, so Ko sets out to fall in love with Nazuna. Around them gather other vampires (Seri, Nico, Kabura, Midori, Hatsuka), his childhood friends Akira and Mahiru, and Anko Uguisu, a chain-smoking detective who hunts vampires because her vampire father killed her mother. Later arcs follow the ancient vampire Kiku Hoshimi, who draws Mahiru away from his family and dies with him, and Anko's choice to give up revenge (Hokkaido arc, about vol. 15-16). In the end Ko and Nazuna learn that a vampire's bite kills a human she loves, so they part; three years later Ko, now in high school and stronger, finds her in a snowy village and they reunite (ch. 200) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Epicstream).

## Life message

**A good life is one you choose for yourself. Freedom, honesty about your feelings and real connection matter more than fitting in, and love means staying with someone even when it costs you.**

Outlook: Bittersweet. Ko's friend Mahiru dies with Kiku, and Ko and Nazuna must part for three years because her love could kill him; the last chapter reunites them in hope, with Ko older and stronger (ch. 200).
Hope source: L (love between two). The story's way through is a patient, faithful love that waits and grows; friendship and a found family of night people (F) are the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Most frames welcome the story's honesty about loneliness, the renunciation of revenge, the exposure of Kiku's manipulation and the ending's patient, faithful love. The divide is over how the story treats a 14-year-old: almost every religious frame and the progressive, humanist and nonreligious frames weigh the adult-minor sexual teasing and the absence of protecting adults, but they differ on the night itself. Christian, Jewish, Muslim and Latter-day Saint readings weigh truancy, blood-drinking and nightlife as messages the story endorses (C and D), while Shinto, Pagan, Satanist and secular readings warm to its celebration of freedom and nonconformity and grade it higher, keeping the consent concern.

## Creator and publisher context

- Kotoyama (pen name; male per the fan wiki) grew up on shonen action manga and Katsuhiro Otomo's work, began drawing around age 20, debuted with a web one-shot, and serialized Dagashi Kashi in Weekly Shonen Sunday (June 2014 to April 2018) before Call of the Night (fan wiki, Kotoyama page; Wikipedia).
- He named the series after Creepy Nuts' song "Yofukashi no Uta"; a 2020 music-video collaboration with the duo Yorushika also promoted it (Wikipedia; fan wiki). Nazuna resembles the heroine of Dagashi Kashi (fan wiki).
- No English interview with Kotoyama on the series' themes, the characters' ages or the ending was found. Sora Amamiya, Nazuna's voice actress, describes the series as accepting people's flaws without pressure (Mantan Web, 2025). **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Weekly Shonen Sunday, a boys' magazine). VIZ Media (owned by Shogakukan and Shueisha; from general knowledge) publishes it under the Shonen Sunday imprint, rated T+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a life worth staying awake for? Ko cannot sleep because his days, though outwardly fine, feel empty; Nazuna tells him on the first night that people who cannot sleep are not satisfied with how they spent the day (ch. 1, fan wiki; Aftermath). The series asks whether freedom from school and routine is enough, and what love actually is.

**The answer the story gives.** Freedom opens the door, but it is not the answer. Ko's first attraction to Nazuna is lust, and the bite that would turn him does not work (Aftermath). What changes him is slow: friendships, other people's pain (Anko's grief, Mahiru's neglect), and learning to love someone enough to let her go. The final chapter does not turn Ko into a vampire; he goes back to school, works, grows stronger, and comes back to Nazuna on his own terms (ch. 200).

**Characters as arguments.** Ko argues for honesty about what you feel, even when you do not know yet. Nazuna argues for carefree pleasure, then learns commitment and the fear that her love could harm him. Anko argues for revenge as justice and then lays it down (ch. 155). Kiku is the dark mirror of the romance: an ancient vampire who tells a lonely boy exactly what he wants to hear and draws him away from everyone until they die together (fan wiki). Mahiru, neglected by a grieving mother, shows what happens when no adult notices a child's pain. Ko's mother, who thinks she has no right to be a proper parent, is the quiet question the story never fully answers.

**Symbols and motifs.** The night as freedom and as hiding place. Blood as intimacy and danger. Keepsakes from a vampire's human life as their weakness (Nazuna's is her own umbilical cord, cut in ch. 189). Sleep itself: Nazuna's first offer is to help Ko sleep.

**Traditions it echoes.** The vampire romance (Anime Feminist and Aftermath compare Rosario + Vampire and What We Do in the Shadows), the Japanese coming-of-age story about school refusal, and the long Western tradition of the vampire as seducer. Its ending echoes stories in which love is proved by waiting and sacrifice rather than possession.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is affirmed: being honest about yourself is the story's first virtue. Hedonism is explored and then tempered. Existentialism frames Ko's boredom as a search for meaning he must make himself. Secular humanism runs underneath: no god appears, and comfort comes from people. Progressive readers welcome its LGBTQ characters and mental-health honesty but object to the adult-minor dynamic and fan service.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-49): first nights, the rules of vampirism, Akira and the other vampires, Anko's arrival (ch. 36). Volumes 5-10 (ch. 47-99, the anime's second season): Anko's past and her reconciliation with Nazuna, Kiyosumi and Akkun. Volumes 11-17 (ch. 100-169): Kiku, Mahiru and the Hokkaido arc, ending in deaths and Anko's letting go. Volumes 18-20 (ch. 170-200): Nazuna's past and mother, the discovery that her love could kill Ko, their separation and the reunion.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Ko stops going to school after one bad day. What else could he or the adults around him have done?
2. Nazuna says Ko's first feelings are lust, not love. How does the story show the difference?
3. Why is Kiku's love for Mahiru treated as harmful? Is Nazuna's love for Ko different, and how?
4. Ko is 14 and Nazuna is an adult. Does the story handle that well? Why or why not?
5. Anko gives up revenge after ten years. What changes her mind?
