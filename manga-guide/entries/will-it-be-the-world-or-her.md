**Premise.** High schooler Kouki (Koki) Nakagawa is about to confess on the school roof to his childhood friend Ayumi Fujisaki, the school's admired honor student who bickers with only him, when quiet classmate Hikari Jindou (Jindo) interrupts and announces she is his girlfriend. When he tries again, she knocks Ayumi out with a tranquilizer gun and explains: the great disaster that struck the world eight years ago came from a power inside Ayumi, triggered by her emotional wounds, and if Kouki's confession shakes her, the world could end; a meteorite drawn by the power lands on the schoolyard to prove it (ch. 1, Japanese Wikipedia). Kouki must choose: give up Ayumi and date Jindou, or make Jindou an enemy and risk the world. Jindou sets out to win him by seduction, pressing herself against him and moving into his attic (ch. 2, 6, 10), and a jealous Ayumi punishes him every time he wavers (ch. 19). The comedy darkens: Jindou turns out to be a god, the original owner of the power, who gave it up eight years ago to bring Ayumi back to life after the accident that killed Ayumi's parents (ch. 15, 22); a secret organization that has hunted the power for ages plants spies among Ayumi's friends and sends Tenshi Konoe, a terminally ill girl, to court Kouki and take the power (ch. 18, 22, 33); its boss, Gouroku, wants it to rule the world (ch. 38). Kouki looks for a way to save both Ayumi and the world (ch. 23). In the final volume Kouki is shot, Jindou has power enough to bring back only one person, and she makes the last choice herself; an epilogue shows Kouki and Ayumi's two daughters, named Hikari and Tenshi and drawn to resemble the two lost girls (ch. 40 and extra 2; Japanese Wikipedia; Kodansha vol. 9 blurb).

## Life message

**You should not have to trade the person you love for the world, or the world for her: the story rewards refusing that false choice, and its god-girl learns that love means giving herself so the people she loves can live.**

Outlook: Bittersweet — the world is saved and Kouki and Ayumi marry and raise a family, but Jindou gives herself up to save them and Tenshi is gone; the epilogue's daughters hint at rebirth, and reviewers split on whether to call it happy or sad (MyAnimeList).

**Where traditions disagree.** Most frames welcome the message: Kouki refuses to treat Ayumi as a price for the world, Jindou gives herself up for the people she loves, and the story ends in marriage and children. The divide is the means: a long run of sexual teasing between high-school students, and a goddess as the self-sacrificing savior. Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint and Jehovah's Witness readings weigh the seduction comedy and the deity (C or D, with Significant depiction concern), while Shinto, secular, Hindu and Buddhist frames read the god and the hinted rebirth more warmly. Progressive readers like the anti-utilitarian message but object to how the girls are written around a passive boy.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If loving one person could end the world, should you stop loving her? Kouki is given a choice no teenager should face: give up Ayumi and date the strange girl who claims to protect the world, or follow his heart and risk everyone. The title turns the dilemma on the reader, and the story asks whether it is a real choice at all.

**The answer the story gives.** The story refuses the trade. Kouki will not hand Ayumi over and keeps looking for a way to save both her and the world (ch. 23), even while he wavers under Jindou's seduction. The people who accept the trade, the organization that would drain Ayumi's power for the world's sake and then rule it, are the villains. But the refusal has a cost: someone gives herself up in the end, and it is Jindou, the god who started by treating love as a problem to manage and ends by choosing others over herself (vol. 9).

**Characters as arguments.** Kouki argues for faithful love, though readers find him slow and easily tempted (MyAnimeList). Ayumi argues, at first, that a survivor has no right to happiness (ch. 35), and the story answers her with love. Jindou begins as a calculating guardian who sees seduction as a tool and Kouki's heart as a variable; she learns jealousy, love and loss, and her arc moves from outcome-reasoning to self-gift. Tenshi, dying, argues for grabbing life while you can and serves the organization to be saved (ch. 18, 22). Aiko and Gouroku stand for the cold calculation that one girl is a fair price; Miki stands between, torn between orders and friendship (ch. 33).

**Symbols and motifs.** The rooftop confession, interrupted again and again. The meteor pulled down by a girl's feelings (ch. 1). The attic where a god lives like a stray (ch. 10). The tranquilizer gun. The "choices": every chapter is titled "Choice" and the last is "The Ultimate Choice" (ch. 40). Two daughters with the lost girls' names and faces (epilogue).

**Traditions it echoes.** The "sekai-kei" story, in which a teenage romance is tied directly to the fate of the world (from general knowledge; to verify), and the harem romantic comedy. The philosophers' trolley problem sits under the premise. Readers from Jewish tradition may hear the teaching that whoever saves one life saves a whole world; Christian readers may hear John 15:13 in Jindou's ending; Buddhist and Hindu readers may see rebirth in the epilogue.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarian reasoning is given a voice and rejected; duty to the one person wins. Expressive individualism carries the romance. The supernatural is fantasy without doctrine. Hedonism is the comedy's fuel early on, then fades as the stakes rise; reviewers note the fan service stays present but becomes part of romantic moments (MyAnimeList).

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-14): seduction comedy and love-triangle hijinks, with disasters that hurt no one. Volumes 4-6 (ch. 15-29): Jindou's origin and divinity, Tenshi as a third choice, the school-wide choice. Volumes 7-9 (ch. 30-40 and extras): the organization, spies among friends, abduction, a shooting and Jindou's last choice; much darker, with deaths and an epilogue years later (AniList Time Skip tag).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Would you give up someone you love to keep the world safe? Is that a fair question to ask anyone?
2. Jindou starts by seducing Kouki to protect the world. How does her idea of love change by the end?
3. Ayumi thinks she has no right to be happy because she survived. What would you tell her?
4. The early chapters play seduction for laughs. How does that sit with the serious story later?
5. What do the two daughters at the end mean to you?
