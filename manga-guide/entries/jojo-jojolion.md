**Premise.** In 2011, months after the Tohoku earthquake and tsunami, strange raised landforms called the Wall Eyes have split the town of Morioh. A student, Yasuho Hirose, finds a young man buried there, naked except for a sailor hat, with no memory of who he is. The wealthy Higashikata family, owners of a fruit company, take him in and name him Josuke Higashikata. He learns he is a fusion of two men, the sailor Yoshikage Kira and his friend Josefumi Kujo, who were buried together while using the Locacaca, a smuggled fruit that heals any injury by turning another body part to stone ("equivalent exchange"). The Higashikata family carries a curse: each generation's firstborn turns to stone at age 11, so boys are dressed as girls by family superstition. A smuggling ring of "Rock Humans" trades the fruit to wealthy buyers, and the eldest son, Jobin, joins it. The final enemy, Toru, uses a Stand called Wonder of U that strikes anyone who pursues him with "calamity." The curse is broken at great cost, and the story ends with a 1941 flashback and the family's fruit parlor (Wikipedia; jojowiki).

## Life message

**A good life is found in the people who choose you: identity is built from love, debt and loyalty, not only from blood or memory; healing has a price, and greed for a shortcut destroys families, while sacrifice for others breaks curses.**

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, most frames see greed and murder defeated and sacrifice, adoption and love honored, so B grades are common. The dividing lines are three. First, the curse: traditions with strong teaching against superstition and spiritism (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness, Sikh) weigh the curse rituals and the shrine sacrifice most. Second, inherited sin: Jewish, Muslim and Reformed families can use Araki's question of whether children inherit parents' sins to explain their own teaching. Third, depiction: the uncensored adult nudity and the assault scene weigh most for traditions with modesty rules.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki (born in Sendai) has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987. JoJolion was his first Part run fully in the seinen magazine Ultra Jump (Wikipedia; Steel Ball Run had moved there in 2005, from general knowledge; to verify).
- On the earthquake (Asahi Shimbun, July 2011): he planned the setting before 3/11, and "I couldn't avoid (the issue) because Moriocho is modeled after Sendai"; "there couldn't have been something like 'Kabe no Me' if it weren't for the disaster."
- On themes (Xtrend Nikkei, October 2012, jojowiki translation): the hero "is currently in the process of discovering himself"; he wanted to "explore how to overcome that kind of curse" and asked "how a child inherits the sins of their parents, or how a parent's teachings influence their child," calling these questions "truly terrifying."
- In vol. 1 he said the story is about solving a "curse," which he described as ancestors' sins that make people "unclean" (kegare); in vol. 2 he said "-lion" draws on the Christian idea of blessing, the gospels (evangelion) and Pygmalion (Wikipedia).
- Mainichi Shimbun (May 2022, jojowiki translation): JoJo is "an ode to humanity"; "Both antagonists and protagonists live being affirmative, to the fullest, of their course of life"; he is "drawn to the concept of passing the baton from one generation to the next"; JoJolion's heroes must choose "between two ultimate choices," which he linked to "the deadlocked times."
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media (owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan, from general knowledge; to verify) announced the English edition October 16, 2026.
- Controversies: none widely reported about this Part was found. The Japan Media Arts jury noted the work has drawn "controversy and critical comments beyond manga" without detail. Fan debate centers on the ending and unresolved threads (CBR).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who are you, if you cannot remember? And do you owe a debt for what your parents did? JoJolion opens with a naked man in a sailor hat who asks who he is (ch. 1). It is set in a town broken by an earthquake that, as Araki said, he "couldn't avoid" because Morioh is Sendai. Araki said he wanted to ask "how a child inherits the sins of their parents" and "how to overcome that kind of curse" (Xtrend Nikkei 2012).

**The answer the story gives.** Identity is not only memory or blood. Josuke turns out to be two men, Josefumi and Kira, fused when the ground rose up around them (ch. 53). He does not get back a single "real" self. Instead, he accepts Josefumi's past and Kira's mission to save Holy (jojowiki), and the adopted family slowly becomes his. The curse is real, but it is broken only when someone pays: Kaato and Tsurugi trap Toru in an exchange that ends him (ch. 107-108). Yasuho, looking at the family's losses, says it "was not despair... it looked like hope" (ch. 108).

**Characters as arguments.** Jobin argues that "the strong survive and the weak are destroyed" (ch. 85) and that the family will "shine on and on" (ch. 94); he is also a father who swears to cure his son (ch. 72). Kaato argues that a mother may kill another child to save her own (ch. 64). Toru argues that calamity makes "no distinction between right or wrong" and that his species belongs "at the top" (ch. 102-104). Josefumi is gratitude: a boy whose mother nearly let him drown (ch. 50) and who gives his life for the friend who saved him. Mitsuba is a mother who eats the fruit to save her unborn child, knowing it may kill her (ch. 82).

**Symbols and motifs.** The Wall Eyes: land raised by disaster, where exchanges happen. Stone: the curse, the Rock Humans, the price of the fruit. Fruit: a family business and a black-market cure; Norisuke says "Fruit is special" (ch. 33). Bubbles: Soft & Wet takes things away. The sailor hat and the star: an inheritance. Cross-dressing as a ward against the curse.

**Traditions it echoes.** Biblical debates over inherited guilt (Exodus 20:5; Ezekiel 18:20). Shinto ideas of kegare and purification, which Araki names. The alchemical idea of equivalent exchange. Disaster literature after 3/11. The Pygmalion myth and the gospel, which Araki named for the title. (The links to Exodus and Ezekiel and to alchemy are this entry's framing.)

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential self-making and found family are affirmed. Might makes right and greed for a cure are critiqued through Jobin and Toru, who both die. Superstition and curse rituals are shown as part of a family's tragedy. The fruit trade works like a black market in a dangerous substance; the story shows it destroying users and sellers. Religion is present only as names and ideas.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 (ch. 1-26): the amnesiac mystery and the Higashikata house. Volumes 7-12 (ch. 27-50): the Rock Humans, the fruit trade and Jobin. Volumes 13-18 (ch. 51-74): Josefumi's past, Kaato's return and the hunt for the new fruit. Volumes 19-25 (ch. 75-102): the hospital, Holy and Wonder of U. Volumes 26-27 (ch. 103-110): the final battle, the cure and the 1941 epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. If you lost all your memories, would you still be you? What makes Josuke who he is?
2. Araki asks how children inherit their parents' sins. Do you think they do? What does your family believe?
3. Kaato and Jobin harm others to save their own children (ch. 64). Where do the limits of family loyalty lie?
4. The Locacaca always takes something in return. Is there anything in real life that works like that?
5. Yasuho calls the ending "hope" despite many deaths (ch. 108). Do you agree?
