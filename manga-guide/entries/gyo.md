**Premise.** Tadashi and his girlfriend Kaori, a young adult couple, are on a scuba-diving holiday in Okinawa when a rotting fish scuttles out of the dark on metal spider legs. Soon sea creatures on legs, including a great white shark, march ashore, and the invasion spreads to Tokyo. Kaori, who has an unusually keen sense of smell, is tormented by the stench (v1). Tadashi's uncle, Doctor Koyanagi, loses an arm to one of the machines and explains that his own father built them for the Imperial Japanese Army in World War II: a germ that makes its host give off a deadly 'death stench,' and walking machines driven by that gas, meant to carry infected bodies toward enemy troops; the prototypes sank with their ship (ch. 6-7). Kaori is infected, swells and despairs; she tries to hang herself, and Koyanagi attaches her to a custom walking machine (ch. 10-12). Infected humans and animals are carried through the ruined city by machines; a circus ringmaster puts the infected on show (ch. 15-16); Koyanagi, fused with a flying machine, kidnaps his assistant (ch. 17-18). Kaori is destroyed by other machines. Tadashi, apparently immune, joins Kyoto University students who are seeking a cure, and finds Kaori's burned remains, free at last of the smell (ch. 19) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Weapons and obsessions people create can outlive them and come back to destroy everyone; against a horror no one can stop, love lingers and a few keep searching for a cure.**

## Creator and publisher context

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when something people built to kill is lost, and then comes back? Gyo answers with the sea's dead walking ashore, pushed by a weapon nobody remembers making.

**The answer the story gives.** It destroys indiscriminately, and those who are fascinated by it (Koyanagi) become part of it. The only answers are endurance, love that persists through horror (Kimlinger notes a moving tenderness, ANN vol. 2) and patient research by a few immune students.

**Characters as arguments.** Koyanagi is the scientist who cannot look away; his obsession costs his arm, his life and his humanity, and Kimlinger calls that obsession scarier than the monsters (ANN, vol. 1). Kaori, terrified of losing her beauty, embodies the body's betrayal. Tadashi is the ordinary person who keeps faith with someone he loves even when she becomes monstrous. The students stand for science used for life.

**Symbols and motifs.** Smell (Kaori's name means fragrant, ANN); legs where no legs should be; the circus as spectacle of suffering; the 'Inheritors' of a father's weapon (ch. 13); in Amigara Fault, a hole shaped for each person, a fate that fits too well.

**Traditions it echoes.** Jaws (Ito's stated inspiration), 1950s monster films and their fear of technology (ANN), Lovecraft's cosmic horror, and Japanese horror manga from Umezu and Hino. Readers who hold a sacred view of the body will feel why the story horrifies even without a religious frame.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Naturalism and nihilism are explored without endorsement: the universe is indifferent, but people still love and research. Militarism and body-machine fusion are critiqued by their results.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-10) is the invasion and the backstory, closer to monster movie and camp. Volume 2 (ch. 11-19) turns to infected humans, the circus and Koyanagi's transformation, the most nauseating stretch (ANN). The two bonus stories are unrelated.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Koyanagi cannot stop studying the machine even after it takes his arm. When does curiosity become dangerous?
2. Who is responsible for a weapon when its makers are long dead?
3. Kaori fears Tadashi will not love her if she is not beautiful. Is she right?
4. Why do some readers find Gyo funny as well as horrifying?
5. In Amigara Fault, why do people walk into holes they know will trap them?
