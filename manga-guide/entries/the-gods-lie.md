**Premise.** Eleven-year-old Natsuru Nanao moves with his widowed mother, Ritsuko, a struggling light-novel writer, to a new town. He quits soccer camp after kicking a ball into the face of an abusive substitute coach and spends the summer at the rundown house of his tall, reserved classmate Rio Suzumura, who is raising her little brother Yuuta alone; she says their father is crab fishing in Alaska. Natsuru finds a skeleton in the yard: their grandfather, who died in a fall, and whom Rio buried so the siblings would not be separated. When Rio sees her father in town with a nightclub hostess, Natsuru empties his savings and the three run away to a coastal inn, where Rio confesses her love and kisses him just as his mother arrives with the police. Rio and Yuuta go into foster care and their father is exposed for neglect; some time later Rio calls Natsuru and promises they will meet again (Wikipedia; Solrad; ANN).

## Life message

**Children need adults who keep their promises; loyalty to a friend is precious, but some secrets are too heavy for a child to carry alone.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to children when the adults they trust lie to them? Rio's father promised to come back; he did not. The title suggests that to children, adults are like gods, and the gods lie (Solrad).

**The answer the story gives.** Children can be loyal, brave and resourceful, but they cannot carry everything alone. Rio's secret keeps her family together for a summer and almost breaks her. Natsuru's loyalty is beautiful and still not enough; it takes his mother and the police, painful as that is, to end it. The final phone call suggests that some promises are kept (ANN).

**Characters as arguments.** Natsuru argues for loyalty over rules. Rio argues for holding on to family at any cost, and shows the cost. Her father is the case against selfishness: he leaves his children to drink and to see another woman, and he is exposed. Ritsuko, a widow who writes for a living and often fails at it, is the imperfect good parent who in the end understands. The two coaches show bad and good authority side by side.

**Symbols and motifs.** A lame white kitten, Tofu, that the children rescue as they wish someone would rescue them. A stag beetle mistaken for a ghost, whose burial uncovers the real body. Rain at the festival when Natsuru realizes he loves Rio. Rio's name, meaning "reason to live" (Wikipedia). Summer as a borrowed time that has to end.

**Traditions it echoes.** Stories of children keeping house alone after a parent leaves (the Japanese film Nobody Knows, 2004, on a similar real case; from general knowledge; to verify), and the long tradition of summer coming-of-age tales. It shares with Bible stories of orphans and widows the conviction that the community owes care to the fatherless.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian care is the moral: support systems are needed (Solrad). Duty ethics turns on promises kept and broken. Feminist readers note that the burden lands on a girl. Romance is present only as first love.

**Shifts across the series.** Chapters 1-2 (likely): the move, the kitten and the summer at Rio's house. Chapter 3 (likely): the festival, first love and the discovery of the body. Chapter 4 (likely): school, bullying and Rio seeing her father. Chapter 5: the runaway, the police, foster care and the closing phone call.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Natsuru promised to keep Rio's secret. Was it right to keep it? When should a friend tell an adult?
2. Why did Rio bury her grandfather? What was she afraid of?
3. What did the father's lie cost his children?
4. Natsuru fought the kids who spread rumors. What else could he have done?
5. What does the title mean to you?
