**Premise.** High schooler Ryo Watari knows he is a boy, though everyone around him sees a girl. He goes to school in gym clothes because he cannot face the girls' uniform, secretly paints graffiti, and cannot tell his best friend and crush Chika, or his mother, who keeps asking why he dresses like a boy (Kodansha; SOLRAD; Dauphin County Library). Jin Sato, a transfer student held back a year whom everyone takes for a delinquent, turns out to love the same streetwear; Ryo tearfully tells him he is a boy, and Jin simply accepts it (vol. 1; ANN). With the shy photographer Itsuka Todo they launch a clothing brand, Boys Run the Riot, using Ryo's mural art (vols. 1-2). A popular LGBTQ YouTuber, WING (Tsubasa), boosts their sales but outs Ryo to the world without asking; Ryo stays home, then stands before his class in a boy's uniform (vol. 3; Hayes; Bleeding Cool). In the last volume a veteran designer, Joe, mentors them toward an exhibition, and Ryo and Jin must decide whether to tell their families (vol. 4). The volume ends with the one-shot "Light," a harsher story of a bullied trans boy.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it cost to be seen as who you are, and who will stand with you when you are? Ryo has spent years hiding: gym clothes at school, graffiti at night, silence with his best friend and his mother. The series asks whether he can live openly, and what friends, work and art can do for a person whom almost no one yet sees correctly.

**The answer the story gives.** Be yourself, and build something with people who accept you. Jin's plain acceptance in vol. 1 is the turning point; the brand gives Ryo a voice in public; and when he is outed by someone else (vol. 3), he chooses to stand before his class in a boy's uniform rather than retreat. The story does not pretend the risk is over: family remains the last and hardest audience (vol. 4).

**Characters as arguments.** Jin argues that labels are lazy: people call him a delinquent too. Itsuka argues for quiet talent given a chance. WING is the most complex voice: a queer YouTuber who helps the boys' sales, believes visibility is good, and outs Ryo without asking; the story treats WING's choice as wrong but lets WING apologize and leave (vol. 4). Joe argues that a message needs craft and a concept, not just slogans. Ryo's mother, who means well but does not understand, stands for families who love their child and are not sure what to think.

**Symbols and motifs.** Clothes as armor and as voice (Dauphin County Library podcast). The gym uniform as a hiding place, the boy's uniform as a declaration. Graffiti murals turned into shirts. The word "riot" in a brand that reviewers find surprisingly gentle (SOLRAD).

**Traditions it echoes.** The coming-out story, the school outsider drama and the start-up tale. For Christian readers who disagree with its view of gender, its picture of loneliness and of a friend who simply stays may still echo the call to love the neighbor; for affirming readers it echoes the conviction that every person bears dignity as they are.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is the core and is affirmed. Progressivism is affirmed without debate. Communitarianism wins in the found family. Consumerism is questioned: the boys want to sell, but not to sell out.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-7): isolation, meeting Jin, coming out to him, starting the brand. Vol. 2 (ch. 8-16): money, a part-time job, new friends. Vol. 3 (ch. 17-26): WING, being outed, standing up. Vol. 4 (ch. 27-37): a mentor, the exhibition, families. Then the darker one-shot "Light."

**Discussion questions.**
1. What did Jin do that helped Ryo most? What could others in the class have done?
2. WING thought making Ryo's identity public would help the brand and other trans people. Was WING wrong? Why?
