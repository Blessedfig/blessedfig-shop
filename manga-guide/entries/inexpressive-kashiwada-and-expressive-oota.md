**Premise.** Oota, a mischievous junior high boy whose every feeling shows on his face, keeps setting up surprises and pranks to crack the poker face of his classmate Kashiwada. They always fail: his own face gives the joke away, and her expression never changes (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). The reader sees what others cannot: Kashiwada feels things as strongly as anyone, shown through narrator arrows and notes and through her twin ponytails (Japanese Wikipedia; Da Vinci News via Japanese Wikipedia). She has liked Oota since nursery school, when he pulled her into the group after she was left out; he moved away before she could thank him, and he does not remember it (Japanese Wikipedia). Around them are Tadokoro, a worrier who goes along with Oota's schemes; Sata, an honor student who studies their romance like a research subject; Tabuchi, the class president who adores Kashiwada and thinks Oota is bullying her; Odajima, who can read Kashiwada's feelings and slowly realizes she likes Oota herself; Oota's teasing older sister, who plays matchmaker; Kashiwada's doting older brother, whose smile hides his hostility to Oota; and the class teacher, Oniwarada, who scolds Oota daily but values him (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). Seasonal episodes follow: the pool, a gym storeroom, a date, Christmas and New Year, Valentine's Day and White Day, the beach, a test of courage, and a summer festival (anime episode titles, Wikipedia). The manga ends with the romance resolved (Mangashed; chapter to verify).

## Life message

**Feelings run deeper than faces show: paying close, kind attention to people is how you find out who cares about you, and care reaches people better than pranks.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you know what someone feels? Oota wears every emotion; Kashiwada shows none. Everyone assumes she feels nothing, and the joke is that she feels plenty.

**The answer the story gives.** By paying attention. The friends who look closely (Sata, Odajima) can read her; Oota, who is looking hardest, is too flustered to see it. The pranks are the wrong tool; kindness is the right one, and it was his kindness years earlier that won her in the first place (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Oota argues for feeling out loud, but also shows the cost of mischief. Kashiwada argues that quiet people are not empty. Tabuchi stands for protecting the vulnerable, even when she misreads the situation. Sata turns love into a study. Odajima shows that reading others well does not mean reading yourself well.

**Symbols and motifs.** The narrator's arrows and notes beside Kashiwada (Japanese Wikipedia); her ponytails as a mood gauge (Wikipedia); faces generally, as masks or open books.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese school romantic comedy of teasing and slow confession (*Teasing Master Takagi-san* is a close cousin, from general knowledge; to verify), and the old wisdom, found in many faiths, that people look at faces while the heart is seen by those who care (1 Samuel 16:7 for Christian and Jewish readers).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarian warmth runs throughout. Expression is explored without a verdict: both the loud and the quiet way of feeling are treated as good. No religious or political lens appears.

**Shifts across the series.** The first three volumes (chapters 1-37, the anime) are short gags around school seasons. Later chapters move toward confession and resolution (Mangashed; to verify). The side series continues the cast (from 2023).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do Oota's pranks never work?
2. How do Sata and Odajima know what Kashiwada feels when Oota doesn't?
3. Tabuchi thinks Oota is bullying Kashiwada. Is she wrong? When is teasing fun for both people?
4. At the festival, Oota gets Kashiwada away from a stranger. What else should a young person do in that situation?
5. Your family's view: at what age do you think dating or "going out" makes sense, and why?
