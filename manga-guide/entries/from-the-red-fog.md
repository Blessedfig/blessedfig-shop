**Premise.** England in the late nineteenth century (Yen Press; ANN places it mid-century). Ruwanda Bailey, twelve, has spent his life locked in a cellar by his mother Miranda, a beautiful serial killer who seduces and murders men and makes him help dispose of the bodies; he begins killing too, starting with the nannies hired to teach him (SKJAM; ANN). When intruders or police reach the house, he escapes into the world for the first time. On the streets he is abused again, including a rape by a wealthy man, mostly off the page (ANN). At an orphanage run by Will, a kind man who believes no one is born evil, Ruwanda kills most of the other children and then Will (ch. 1; SKJAM; MyAnimeList). He is taken up by killers working for Lord Midwinter, a nobleman who runs an assassins' guild called the Organization with an eye to power over society, meets the animal-killing girl Macalo and the rival boy-assassin Ivan, and carries out murders on commission, including a couple who tortured boys to death (vols. 1-3; Yen Press; But Why Tho; SKJAM). A girl named May stirs feelings he does not understand; he cannot kill her (ch. 11) and later weeps (ch. 19) (MyAnimeList). In vol. 4 he searches for his mother and uncovers the Organization's secrets; in vol. 5, having lost the only love he has known, he leaves the guild, grows into a young man after a time skip, and is drawn back by a string of murders in London to a final confrontation (Yen Press). Fan reviewers call the ending rushed and open (MyAnimeList).

## Life message

**It holds up no model life: it shows how a child raised in abuse and isolation becomes a killer, and asks, without a clear answer, whether connection can still reach him.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is a killer born or made? Ruwanda grew up in a cellar with a murderous mother; the first kind adult he meets, Will, tells him no one is born evil. Ruwanda kills him anyway. The series asks whether a child formed by such cruelty can ever feel, love or change.

**The answer the story gives.** It does not settle it. Ruwanda falters before killing Will (ch. 1), cannot kill May (ch. 11) and finally weeps (ch. 19), according to a MyAnimeList reviewer, but he keeps killing, and the ending is open (Yen Press; MyAnimeList). Critics felt the story leaned on shock rather than answering (ANN; But Why Tho, vol. 1).

**Characters as arguments.** Will argues for innate goodness and is killed. Miranda argues that children are tools. Midwinter argues that hidden violence can rule society. May argues, without words, that tenderness is possible. Ivan and Macalo are mirrors: other children who kill, offering rivalry and companionship.

**Symbols and motifs.** The red fog: blood spray that follows Ruwanda everywhere (But Why Tho). The cellar and the light. The rose with thorns (his mother; SKJAM). London's fog and its wealth beside its poverty.

**Traditions it echoes.** Victorian penny dreadfuls and the Jack the Ripper legend; Mary Ann Cotton, the Victorian poisoner (ANN); Dexter (SKJAM); Japanese gothic manga such as Black Butler. In moral terms it echoes the old debate between original sin and the blank slate, without siding with either.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nihilism and might-makes-right describe the world the story shows rather than what it praises; naturalism's nature-versus-nurture question is raised and left open. No faith lens is present; the humane, secular hope voiced by Will is defeated in the first chapter.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: escape, the orphanage massacre, abuse on the streets, recruitment. Vols. 2-3: the Organization, missions, Ivan, the 'gentlebeast' manor (Yen Press; But Why Tho). Vol. 4: the search for his mother and the Organization's secrets; a twist that recasts his past (MyAnimeList). Vol. 5: a time skip, new characters, the London murders and an open ending (Yen Press; MyAnimeList).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Will believes no one is born evil. Does the story prove him right or wrong?
2. Why might Ruwanda be unable to kill May?
3. The story shows killings from the killer's point of view. How does that change how a reader feels about the victims?
4. Who in this story was responsible for protecting Ruwanda, and how did they fail?
5. Some critics said the darkness was there only for shock. What would make a dark story worth reading?
