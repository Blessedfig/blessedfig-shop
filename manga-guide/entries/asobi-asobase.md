**Premise.** Hanako Honda (energetic, rich, with a butler), Olivia (raised in Japan by foreign parents, who pretends to be an American who speaks broken Japanese but knows no English) and Kasumi Nomura (who hates games after losing to her older sister and secretly writes boys' love stories) are second-year students at an all-girls middle school and the only members of the unofficial Pastimers Club (Wikipedia). Each chapter turns a simple pastime (rock-paper-scissors, a staring contest, a board game) into an absurd, petty war, with grotesque faces and gross-out gags. Recurring targets include a teacher they blackmail into advising the club, Hanako's butler Maeda (who fires lasers from his rear after alien probing), the Occult Research Club, a timid student council president, and classmate Tsugumi Aozora, whom the girls suspect is a boy (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). Later volumes widen the cast to art and newspaper club members; reviewers say the last stretch (from about ch. 108) turns to side characters and drama and that the two-chapter ending leaves Kasumi somewhat apart from the other two (MyAnimeList reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes three girls who do not much like each other at first into friends? The series' answer is games: pointless, petty, embarrassing games that let them fight, lose and laugh without real stakes.

**The answer the story gives.** Friendship is built from shared absurdity, not from being nice. The girls lie, scheme, bully and humiliate each other, and keep coming back. The story never moralizes; it trusts the reader to see that the club is the one place each of them belongs.

**Characters as arguments.** Hanako argues for play itself, wholehearted and childish. Kasumi argues for reason and dignity and keeps losing both. Olivia argues for keeping up an image, and her fake-English act is the long joke the series slowly lets go. The student council president stands for rules and secretly wants to join. Tsugumi turns the girls' curiosity back on them.

**Symbols and motifs.** The game of the chapter; the clubroom; Maeda's absurd inventions; Olivia's broken Japanese; the joint cover of the last two volumes (Comic Natalie).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese gag manga and the school-club comedy it parodies (ANN compares it to cute-girl club shows; Anime Feminist to Pop Team Epic); slapstick in the line of physical comedy everywhere.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Absurdism is the whole method and is celebrated. Postmodern irony about the genre runs underneath. Hedonism is present only as play, not as pleasure-seeking. Feminist readers praise girls allowed to be gross; no other lens is argued.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-5 (to ch. 48, the anime's range): the trio's games, the occult club, Tsugumi and the teacher. Middle volumes widen the cast with new clubs and side characters. The last stretch (from about ch. 108, vol. 13-15) centers on art- and newspaper-club characters and more dramatic stories, with a rushed two-chapter ending (MyAnimeList reviews; to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. The girls are often mean to each other. Why do they stay friends?
2. Olivia pretends to be someone she is not. Why, and what happens when people find out?
3. Is it fair to blackmail a teacher, even as a joke?
4. The club keeps trying to find out if Tsugumi is a boy. How would you feel if classmates did that to you?
5. Which jokes in the series are kind, and which are mean?
