**Premise.** High-schooler Kimihiro Watanuki, an orphan who lives alone, is plagued by spirits only he can see and that want to harm or eat him. He stumbles into a shop run by Yuko Ichihara, a powerful witch who grants wishes for a price of equal value. To pay for his wish to be rid of the spirits, he becomes her part-time cook and housekeeper, and she sends him on errands among spirits, curses and customers whose wishes usually go badly because of their desires and addictions (Wikipedia; Manga Bookshelf). He is joined by Himawari Kunogi, the girl he has a crush on, who turns out to bring bad luck to everyone near her, and Shizuka Domeki, a classmate who can repel spirits and whom Watanuki claims to detest (fan wiki). Crossovers with Tsubasa reveal that Yuko is alive only because her time was frozen, and that Watanuki was born to fill a gap left by Tsubasa's Syaoran. When the spell ends Yuko dies (vol. 15-16); Watanuki keeps his sight to take over the shop and grants wishes there, no longer aging, waiting to see her again. After a hundred years Yuko tells him in a dream that he may leave; he chooses to stay and keep waiting (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Every wish has a price and every choice shapes what becomes inevitable; you cannot buy happiness by throwing yourself away, because your life is bound up with the people who care about you.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Little of it. Spirits prey on the vulnerable; friendship and protection (Domeki guarding Watanuki) decide who survives. Watanuki, an orphan, finds a substitute family in the shop (Manga Bookshelf). |
| Pragmatic | Equivalent exchange is the law of the world: nothing comes free, and customers who try to cheat the price suffer (Wikipedia; Manga Bookshelf). Yuko explains the practical cost of every choice; Watanuki learns to count costs to others, not only to himself. |
| Religious | The world is steeped in Japanese folk belief: spirits (ayakashi), yokai, Shinto and Buddhist imagery, purification and curses. The organizing idea is hitsuzen, which Yuko sums up as no coincidence, only inevitability arising from everyone's choices (Manga Bookshelf). Manga Bookshelf's reviewer calls its morality non-Christian, built on action and reaction rather than good and evil. Yuko is a witch and a benevolent figure. |
| Socio-cultural | Wikipedia notes that CLAMP, as in Tokyo Babylon, writes about social pathologies: the '-holics' of the title, from a woman addicted to the internet who calls her husband and child 'other people' to a habitual liar (Wikipedia, Themes). A child medium is exploited by television and her mother (Kohane, fan wiki). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Watanuki grows from a self-neglecting boy into a calm shopkeeper loved by his friends, but Yuko dies and he chooses to wait for her in the shop for over a century; CLAMP says that for Watanuki this was happiness (Wikipedia).
Hope source: F — the way through is the bonds between people: Domeki and his descendants keep Watanuki company for a century, Himawari pays half the price to heal him, and Yuko teaches him that his life matters to others (Wikipedia; fan wiki); love and longing (L), his promise to wait for Yuko, is the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- CLAMP formed in the 1980s as a group of women manga artists; Nanase Ohkawa writes the scripts and the others consult her before drawing (Wikipedia). Founding year and members' biographies are (from general knowledge; to verify).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia): xxxHOLiC was conceived to link CLAMP's fantasy and supernatural series with a realistic one; the title was first going to be 'addicted'; like Tokyo Babylon it 'focuses on social pathologies'; CLAMP wanted the two protagonists of xxxHOLiC and Tsubasa to be revealed as sharing one existence. On the ending, CLAMP said that Watanuki's choice may seem sad but was happiness for him.
- Art draws on ukiyo-e prints and Alphonse Mucha; Tsubaki Nekoi draws male characters and spirits, Mokona female characters (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Weekly Young Magazine, seinen; later Bessatsu Shonen Magazine). English: Del Rey, then Kodansha USA (16+).
- No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a wish really cost, and what are we willing to pay for what we want? Each customer arrives wanting something, a fix, a cure, an escape, and Yuko names the price. Underneath is a second question for Watanuki himself: is his own life worth anything to him?

**The answer the story gives.** Nothing is free, and the price of throwing yourself away is paid by the people who love you. Watanuki starts out ready to sacrifice himself for anyone; he learns that when he is hurt, Himawari, Domeki and Yuko are hurt too (fan wiki). Choices, his and everyone's, add up to what becomes inevitable (hitsuzen, Manga Bookshelf).

**Characters as arguments.** Yuko is the voice of exchange and inevitability, generous and ruthless at once. Domeki argues for faithfulness without words. Himawari is love that must keep its distance. The customers are fables: the liar, the woman who calls her family 'other people' (Wikipedia, Themes), the cravers. Kohane is a child who blames herself for her mother's coldness and learns she is worth love. The adult Watanuki, smoking Yuko's pipe, is the boy who became his teacher.

**Symbols and motifs.** The shop that only those who need it can find; the price; the pipe and the sake; the eye shared between two friends; butterflies; dreams; the cage (ro) of the later title; a shop where time stops.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese ghost and yokai tales; Buddhist teaching on craving and cause and effect; Western fables of wishes gone wrong (the monkey's paw); CLAMP's own Tokyo Babylon. Christian readers may set equivalent exchange against grace, the gift that cannot be paid for.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult world is the setting and is treated with respect and danger. Buddhist-style warnings about craving shape the fables. Existential questions of identity and choice dominate the end. Hedonism is critiqued in the customers but lived by Yuko, which leaves the lesson mixed.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-8 are mostly episodic fables with comedy. Volumes 9-14 deepen into Watanuki's bonds, Kohane's story and the dream world. Volumes 15-16 bring Yuko's death; volumes 16-19 (xxxHOLiC Ro) follow the calm, older Watanuki running the shop and choosing to wait.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yuko says every wish has an equal price. Is there anything in real life that is truly free?
2. Watanuki kept getting hurt helping others. Why did his friends get upset instead of grateful?
3. Which customer's 'holic' felt most real to you? What would you have told them?
4. Yuko drinks and smokes, and Watanuki later takes up her pipe. Why do you think he does that? Is it a good way to remember someone?
5. Watanuki chooses to keep waiting for Yuko. Is that love, or being stuck?
