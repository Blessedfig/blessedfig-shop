**Premise.** Mariko "Mari" Kumakura is a shy, top-of-the-class high school student who eats lunch alone. Akiko "Akko" Oohashi, a stylish, outgoing classmate, decides to make her a best friend, makes her over, and brings her into a group with the party-loving Sugi (who drinks a lot and tends to strip when drunk) and the otaku Tamami (Wikipedia). After a group date, the two drink at Akko's apartment and Mari kisses Akko while she sleeps (end of vol. 1, Okazu). In their second year Mari dates a boy she knew from middle school to get over her feelings, kisses Akko again while drunk, and claims she has slept with the boy, which reviewers doubt and which both girls later admit was a lie about their experience (Gaffney). The story switches to Akko's view as she realizes she loves Mari; after a kiss at karaoke (end of vol. 3) and a class trip full of misunderstandings, they finally talk honestly at a heart-shaped stone said to bring eternal love (vol. 4, Okazu). In vol. 5 they become a couple, have sex for the first time (Gaffney; ANN; MangaUpdates), talk about coming out to friends and family, apply to college and graduate (ANN; Lesbrary; Okazu).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you risk when you tell your best friend you love her? Mari's first friend is also her first love, and she is afraid that saying so will cost her both. The series asks whether a friendship can survive the truth.

**The answer the story gives.** It can, and it grows. The early volumes are full of hiding: kisses given when Akko is asleep or when Mari is drunk, a boyfriend Mari does not love, lies about experience. The turning point is an honest conversation (vol. 4), after which the two become a couple and plan a future (vol. 5).

**Characters as arguments.** Mari argues that a shy person can change and find the courage to speak. Akko argues that kindness to a stranger can change a life, including your own. Sugi, the worldly one, is the first to see the truth and chooses to protect it quietly; in an extra chapter she envies their innocence (Wikipedia). Mari's boyfriend stands for the path expected of her.

**Symbols and motifs.** The makeover: the outside changing before the inside catches up. The heart-shaped stone on the class trip. Graduation as the threat of separation and the test of the relationship.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese yuri tradition, but without its usual convent-school contrivances (ANN); the coming-of-age school story; the friends-to-lovers romance.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is affirmed: being honest about who you love is the happy ending. Progressive acceptance of same-sex love is assumed rather than argued. Consumer culture (fashion, diets) is shown as normal teen life and gently satirized by reviewers rather than the story.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 are Mari's point of view, friendship and the first secret kisses. Vol. 3 shifts to Akko's view as she realizes her own feelings. Vol. 4 is the class trip and the honest talk. Vol. 5 brings the couple together, their first sexual experience, college and graduation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Akko made friends with Mari when Mari was alone. Have you ever done that, or wished someone would?
2. Mari kissed Akko while Akko was asleep. Was that okay? What would have been a better way?
3. Why do you think both girls lied about their experience? What happens when they stop pretending?
4. The story shows teenagers drinking as normal. Is it? What does it lead to here?
5. What makes a friendship strong enough to survive a hard truth?
