**Premise.** One spring, Satoko Tawada, a 30-year-old office worker at a Tokyo sporting-goods maker, finds Mashu Hayami, a 12-year-old elementary school boy, practicing soccer alone at night in a park, and starts coaching him (Wikipedia). She first steps in when a suspicious man is bothering him (TV Tropes; Renote; Gaffney). The same week her boss and former boyfriend, Fumitaka Shiikawa, invites her for drinks and introduces his fiancée; she cries in front of Mashu, who comforts her. Mashu's mother has died and his father is distant and controlling; Satoko starts seeing Mashu outside practice, takes him on outings and lets him into her apartment. When Mashu's father learns of it he demands that her company punish her, and she is transferred to Sendai (Wikipedia). Two years later, at 33, she turns down a marriage proposal from a school friend and, the same night, runs into Mashu (now 14) on a school trip; they begin exchanging messages, and she returns to Tokyo to join Shiikawa's project team. Mashu tells her he loves her and she turns him down because she is an adult and he is a child. With her sister Mayuko's help she learns that his home life has already improved, and realizes that her wish to "save" him was a pretext: she simply wanted to be by his side (English and Japanese Wikipedia). In later volumes Mashu is in high school (about 15-16); Satoko apologizes to his father and asks permission to see him (vol. 7); his classmate Nao, who loves him, confronts Satoko (vol. 8) (Anime UK News). The ending leaves their future open; reviewers report no physical relationship (MyAnimeList review; TV Tropes; MechaComic reader reviews).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hitomi Takano (story and art); earlier work mainly BL (Renote); later series Gene Bride (English Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: the series grew from a talk with her editor about an age-gap pair growing older together (she cited the film Boyhood); it began as a girl and a male photographer, and she swapped the genders, which let her draw the fear of aging and write the heroine's monologue (Japanese Wikipedia). She said she has never been especially drawn to boy characters and chose 30 and 12 because the gap makes readers stop and think (konomanga.jp, 2016).
- On adults and children: Japanese Wikipedia reports that in a 2018 Nlab interview she said an adult must never lay a hand on a child, whatever happens, and that this is why the story treats Satoko's involvement as a high bar. TV Tropes reports she said she had no romantic plan for the ending. In 2016 she said she was unsure whether the closeness would become romance, since she would then have to show what follows (konomanga.jp).
- Publishers: Futabasha (Monthly Action), then Kodansha (Weekly Young Magazine), which persuaded her that a male readership was a reason to move (Japanese Wikipedia). English: Vertical / Kodansha USA, 16+.
- Sean Gaffney criticized the volume 1 author notes for playing up the "older woman/young boy" angle (Manga Bookshelf). No other controversies were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is an adult's love for a lonely child, and what does she owe him? Satoko tells herself she is helping a neglected boy. The story slowly makes her, and the reader, look at what she really wants.

**The answer the story gives.** Honesty first. Satoko refuses Mashu's confession because she is an adult and he is a child, then admits that her wish to "save" him was a pretext for wanting to be near him (Wikipedia). She apologizes to his father and asks permission (vol. 7). The creator's line is that an adult must never act on such feelings; the ending, by design, does not say what their bond becomes.

**Characters as arguments.** Satoko argues for care and then for self-knowledge. Mashu, a child who comforts an adult, argues that neglected children reach for whoever is kind. The father is the case for a parent's right to protect, even when he is a poor parent. Shiikawa is manipulation in an adult relationship. Mayuko, the sister, is clear sight. Nao is ordinary first love between peers.

**Symbols and motifs.** The night soccer practice; Mashu's long hair, cut short in junior high; the turquoise color he likes because of Satoko; a scarf given in Sendai; the phone that keeps them "side by side" at the end (Japanese Wikipedia; MechaComic).

**Traditions it echoes.** The age-gap story told across time (the creator cites Boyhood), the Japanese "oneshota" pairing recast as drama, and literary novels about forbidden attachments. Anime UK News reads the bond through Plato's soulmates.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism dominates: loneliness, aging and self-deception. Duty ethics sets the line. Expressive individualism pulls the other way and is left unresolved.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: Tokyo, the park, the transfer. Vol. 4: the reunion after two years. Vols. 5-6: return to Tokyo, the confession and refusal. Vols. 7-9: Mashu in high school, the apology to his father, Nao and Shiikawa, and the open ending (Wikipedia; AIPT; Anime UK News).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Satoko first helps Mashu because he is alone at night. When did her help start to cross a line?
2. Mashu's father is angry when he finds out. Was he right?
3. Why does Satoko say no when Mashu tells her he loves her?
4. What are some warning signs when an adult wants a secret friendship with a child? Who would you tell?
5. Satoko realizes she wanted to "save" Mashu for her own sake. Have you ever helped someone partly for yourself?
