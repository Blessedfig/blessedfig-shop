**Premise.** Akihabara, summer 2010. Rintaro Okabe, 18, a university student who plays a self-styled mad scientist, runs a tiny "Future Gadget Laboratory" with his childhood friend Mayuri Shiina (16) and the hacker Itaru "Daru" Hashida (19). At a lecture Okabe finds the young neuroscientist Kurisu Makise (18) lying in blood, sends a text, and the world shifts: Kurisu is alive and their microwave-and-phone gadget can send texts into the past ("D-mails"). Their experiments change the present and draw the attention of SERN, a research body that has been killing test subjects in its own time-travel work. When SERN's agents raid the lab, Mayuri is killed; Okabe sends his memories back again and again but cannot save her. Undoing each D-mail to reach a timeline where she lives means returning to one where Kurisu dies. With help from Suzuha Amane, a time traveler from a future ruled by SERN, Okabe finally finds a third path, the "Steins Gate" world line, where both live (Wikipedia; Otaku USA; volume descriptions). The manga tells this in 23 chapters and cuts or condenses several side arcs (Kiri Kiri Basara).

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every tradition welcomes the story's moral core: responsibility for consequences and love that sacrifices for friends. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you could change the past, should you? Okabe begins by sending playful messages back in time and ends trapped in a loop where every change costs someone's life.

**The answer the story gives.** Power over time is too much for people to wield lightly, but despair is not the answer either. Okabe must undo his own meddling, accept losses he cannot reverse, and then, with help, find the one path that saves both people he loves. Beneath the Tangles reads this as humility before plans larger than our own.

**Characters as arguments.** Okabe argues for loyalty without limits and learns its cost. Kurisu argues for reason joined to compassion, and is willing to be erased to save a friend. Mayuri is the innocence the story protects. Nakabachi is ambition that turns on its own child. SERN is knowledge used to rule. Suzuha shows duty to a future others cannot see.

**Symbols and motifs.** The microwave "PhoneWave"; the divergence meter's numbers; Mayuri's pocket watch and her cheerful greeting; Okabe's lab coat and his fake phone calls to the "Organization"; Akihabara itself, a place of tinkerers.

**Traditions it echoes.** Time-loop and alternate-history fiction (YU-NO, the John Titor internet legend that the game uses), Greek tragedy's fate that returns no matter what, and the scientist's responsibility for invention (Frankenstein).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarian trading of lives is tested and refused. Authoritarian control of science is condemned. Secular humanism and friendship carry the hope, while Christian readers find providence and humility in the same events.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 is discovery and comedy with a dark hook (Kurisu in blood; Otaku USA). Vol. 2 turns to Mayuri's repeated deaths and Okabe's failures (library catalog description). Vol. 3 covers undoing the D-mails, the choice between Mayuri and Kurisu and the final rescue (ANN press release; Wikipedia). Kiri Kiri Basara notes that the manga condenses side arcs (Faris, Mr. Braun, Suzuha's search for her father).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Okabe changes small things at first. When did the experiments stop being harmless?
2. Kurisu tells Okabe to save Mayuri even though it erases her. What do you think of that choice?
3. Is it ever right to trade one life for another? How does the story answer?
4. Okabe acts like a mad scientist. Why does he need that act, and why does he drop it?
5. What would you send to your past self, and what might go wrong?
