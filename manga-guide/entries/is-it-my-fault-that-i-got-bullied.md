**Premise.** At a middle-school reunion, the 35-year-old former classmates meet Aizawa, whom they once bullied so badly that he stopped coming to school. Most apologize to protect themselves; only the ringleader, Suzuki, now a successful banker, says flatly that people get bullied for a reason. Aizawa is relieved that Suzuki has not changed: Aizawa is now a middle-school teacher, and Suzuki's beloved daughter Shiori is in his class (v1). Shiori is being bullied: her best friend Yumi turns on her, she is cut off from Manaka, the one classmate who still talks to her, and she begins to think it must be her own fault (v2). Manaka traces a past victim from Aizawa's school (v3), while Suzuki, about to be promoted, tries to end an affair with a woman named Yazaki (v3). A video of the bullying spreads online; the principal tries to protect the school's reputation; the ringleader Himekawa is shunned; Shiori reveals she posted the video herself (v4). Aizawa goes public, the media descend, and Aizawa's own past conduct toward another bullying victim is also scrutinized; Shiori and Yumi reconcile (v5). Aizawa tells Shiori that her father bullied him; she decides to break with her father, and Yazaki sends photos of the affair to Suzuki's bank, ending his career (v6) (ciatr; Penguin Random House; Mechacomic).

## Life message

**Bullying is never the victim's fault: the cruelty a bully sows comes back on him, and the young can break the cycle by facing the truth and reconciling, though revenge leaves its own scars.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** The title asks it: is the bully to blame, or the bullied? Suzuki says victims invite it (v1). The story tests that claim by putting his own beloved daughter in the victim's place.

**The answer the story gives.** The bully is to blame. Shiori's self-blame (v2) is shown as a lie bullying teaches, and Suzuki's cruelty and deceit catch up with him (v6). But the story also asks whether Aizawa's revenge is justice, and his own past comes under scrutiny (v5).

**Characters as arguments.** Suzuki argues for strength and blame-the-weak, and loses everything. Aizawa argues for payback and exposure, and must face questions himself. Shiori argues for truth and compassion, even toward her father's victim (v6). Yumi shows how fear makes friends into bullies, and how forgiveness can bring them back (v5). The principal shows how institutions fail.

**Symbols and motifs.** The reunion as the past returning; videos and photos as truth that cannot be denied; the science preparation room where Aizawa confesses (v6).

**Traditions it echoes.** The revenge thriller and the "sins of the father" story; Japanese school dramas about ijime (from general knowledge; to verify).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might-makes-right is condemned; duty and revenge are set against each other without a tidy answer; community appears mostly as a crowd that can turn either way.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2: setup and Shiori's isolation. Volume 3: investigation and Suzuki's secret. Volumes 4-5: exposure, media and reconciliation. Volume 6: Aizawa's confession and Suzuki's fall.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Suzuki says people get bullied for a reason. Why is that wrong? Why does Shiori start to believe it about herself?
2. Was Aizawa right to let events in his class unfold the way he did? What should a teacher do?
3. Shiori posted the video of her own bullying. Was that brave, risky, or both?
4. Yumi betrayed Shiori. Why might a friend do that, and what made forgiveness possible?
5. Does a child have to pay for a parent's wrongs? Does a child owe loyalty to a parent who did wrong?
