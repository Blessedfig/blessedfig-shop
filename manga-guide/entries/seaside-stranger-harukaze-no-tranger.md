**Premise.** Novelist Shun Hashimoto, disowned years ago when he came out at his own arranged wedding, returns from the Okinawan island where he met Mio Chibana to his family home in Hokkaido, because his father is said to be ill; Mio, now his boyfriend and orphaned himself, comes along (Seven Seas vol. 2; Wikipedia). Shun finds that his childhood friend and ex-fiancée Sakurako tricked him into the visit to make him reconcile, and that his parents adopted a boy, Fumi, from an orphanage while he was away. His father is cold and silent, his mother warmer; the father lets the couple live in the family's old shop on the property (ANN, first Harukaze volume). Fumi, a middle-school student in love with Sakurako, catches Shun and Mio having sex and struggles to accept them (vol. 3). At Fumi's school Shun meets Wada, the classmate he once loved, now married (vol. 4). Shun's first novel is published and the media brand him a "gay novelist" (Wikipedia). After a five-year time skip, Mio works as a mechanic, Shun has stopped writing, the couple feel their relationship going stale, and a teenage Fumi rebels and resents Shun for rejecting Sakurako (vol. 5). An accident sends them to Tokyo and their first home of their own (vol. 6), where a new editor pushes Shun to write again (vol. 7) (Penguin Random House blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens after the confession? The first book ended with Shun accepting Mio's love and deciding to go home. The sequel asks whether a family that rejected a son can make room for him, and whether a couple can survive ordinary life.

**The answer the story gives.** Slowly, partly, and only with work on all sides. The father offers a home but not approval; Fumi is told the truth and still struggles; Shun publishes a book and then stops writing; the couple drift and try to rekindle what they had (blurbs; ANN). The story does not promise full acceptance; it shows people learning to live together anyway.

**Characters as arguments.** Shun argues, at first, that his family will never accept him; ANN suggests the father's silence is discomfort rather than hatred. Mio argues for family itself: an orphan, he fears losing the one he has found (ANN, vol. 3). Fumi argues, as a child would, from loyalty and confusion; his rejection of Shun is tied to his love for Sakurako (Wikipedia). Sakurako argues that love can mean pushing someone home.

**Symbols and motifs.** The spring breeze of the Japanese title against the sea of the first book; the old family shop as a home inside the family's home; Shun's novel as his voice made public and then labeled; the time skip as the test of a relationship once the romance is won.

**Traditions it echoes.** The return of the prodigal son, here with a father slower to run out and meet him; the Japanese family drama of duty and the household; the BL romance, turned toward domestic realism (MyAnimeList reviewer).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and expressive individualism are held together: Shun becomes more himself by going home, not by leaving. Progressivism is affirmed through the couple's ordinary life rather than argument. Traditionalism is explored: filial duty is honored even as the family's expectations about marriage are set aside.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 2-4 (Japanese 1-3) are the homecoming: the father, Fumi's discovery, Wada and the novel. Vol. 5 (Japanese 4) jumps five years ahead to a settled, restless household and a rebellious teenage Fumi. Vols. 6-7 (Japanese 5-6) move the couple to Tokyo and their own home, with Shun's career and the relationship's spark in question.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Shun's father offers a place to live but not approval. Is that acceptance?
2. Why do you think Fumi reacts so strongly, and how do the adults handle it?
3. Mio lost his parents. What does Shun's family mean to him?
4. What keeps a couple together once the romance is no longer new?
5. Where would you draw the line between a family's expectations and your own life?
