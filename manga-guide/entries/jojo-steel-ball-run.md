**Premise.** In September 1890, jockeys from around the world gather in San Diego for the Steel Ball Run, a horse race across the United States to New York with a $50 million prize. Johnny Joestar, a former star jockey paralyzed from the waist down after being shot in a quarrel he provoked, enters after Gyro Zeppeli, a disgraced Neapolitan executioner, briefly makes his legs move with the mystical Spin of his steel balls. Gyro races to win a pardon for a boy he believes was wrongly condemned; Johnny follows him to learn the Spin. Rivals become friends as they fend off assassins, outlaws and the ambitious racer Diego Brando. The race is a cover: President Funny Valentine is gathering the scattered parts of a 1,900-year-old Saint's Corpse, heavily implied to be Jesus Christ, to make the United States invulnerable by pushing all misfortune onto the rest of the world. The promoter's 14-year-old wife, Lucy Steel, spies on him and becomes the Corpse's vessel. Valentine kills Diego, Hot Pants and Gyro (v20-21); Johnny masters the perfect Spin and kills Valentine after refusing a deal (ch. 85-89), loses the race to an alternate-world Diego who is destroyed by Lucy, regains his legs, and takes Gyro's body home to Italy (ch. 90-95) (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**A good life is the honest pursuit of one's own fulfillment and growth through hardship and friendship: getting from minus to zero by one's own effort, not by power taken from others. No nation, and no sacred object, justifies sacrificing the powerless.**

Christian readers weigh reverent awe toward the Saint against the treatment of Christ's body as a corpse that grants power, which differs from the Christian teaching of the Resurrection. |
| Socio-cultural | A Western set in the Gilded Age: the American president as villain, a Native American racer (Sandman) trying to buy back his people's land, and an Italian monarchy that executes the poor. Outlook: Bittersweet: Valentine is stopped and Johnny walks again, but Gyro dies, Johnny loses the race and the Corpse, and he sails home with his friend's body.
Hope source: E: effort and growing stronger. Johnny goes from minus to zero by mastering the Spin himself, and Araki describes the theme as people overcoming hardship through their own actions rather than through machines or gods (Wikipedia); friendship (F) with Gyro is the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki (story and art) grew up in Sendai, debuted in 1980 with the Western one-shot Poker Under Arms, and began JoJo's Bizarre Adventure in 1987; he was one of five artists invited by the Louvre in 2009 (Wikipedia, Hirohiko Araki).
- On-record intent: Araki described Part 7's theme as "seeking for satisfaction," and, like other parts, an affirmation of humanity's ability to grow and overcome hardship through its own strength and spirit, succeeding by its own actions rather than through machines or gods (Wikipedia). He made three research trips across the United States (Wikipedia).
- Johnny's disability was not planned from the start; it came from the focus on growth through hardship and a hero who must rely on others and on horses (Wikipedia).
- Valentine came from Araki's growing interest in why people do bad things: his patriotism and goals could seem just, and only his exploitation of powerless people makes him evil; the idea of a fighting president came from the film Independence Day (Wikipedia).
- Monthly serialization in the seinen Ultra Jump let him draw longer chapters with fewer restrictions than in a weekly shonen magazine (Wikipedia). No statements on religion or on the Saint's identity were found in sources opened. No controversies involving the creator were found.
- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Shonen Jump, then Ultra Jump); VIZ Media (English, Shonen Jump imprint, rated T+).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person who has fallen below zero, broken in body and spirit, climb back by his own effort? And can any good, even a nation's safety, justify taking that chance away from others? Johnny Joestar wants the Corpse to make him whole; Funny Valentine wants it to make his country invulnerable. Both seek a holy power; only one is willing to pay for it with other people's lives.

**The answer the story gives.** Growth must be earned and cannot be taken. Johnny wins by mastering Gyro's Spin, a craft learned through friendship and suffering (ch. 85), not by holding the relic; the Corpse leaves him in the end, and he walks anyway. Valentine's vision, in which misfortune is pushed onto others, is defeated, though the story gives his case a real hearing (ch. 88).

**Characters as arguments.** Johnny argues for self-made growth, and also shows its dark side: his willingness to kill for what he wants (ch. 64). Gyro argues for mercy and conscience over inherited duty. Valentine argues that a leader must sacrifice some for the many and that power belongs to whoever seizes it first. Diego argues for ambition without scruple. Lucy, a 14-year-old girl, shows courage and loyalty that outmaneuver presidents. Hot Pants argues for atonement.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Spin and the golden rectangle: perfection found in nature. The napkin at the table (ch. 61). The Saint's Corpse, gathered piece by piece. Valentine's handkerchief from his father. The race itself, a roundabout path that turns out to be the shortest (Gyro's last lesson, ch. 84-85; fan wiki).

**Traditions it echoes.** The American Western and the cross-country race film; the Grail quest and relic legends (Joseph of Arimathea); Christian readers may hear echoes of Simon the sorcerer, who tried to buy the Spirit's power. Araki's own JoJo theme of the human spirit overcoming hardship.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Nationalism in its expansionist form is the main target, made persuasive on purpose. Might makes right and utilitarian sacrifice are defeated. Existentialism and classical virtue are affirmed through Johnny and Gyro. The Christian lens is explored through awe at the Saint and condemnation of its misuse, without addressing the Resurrection.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4 (ch. 1-23, the Jump run): the race begins, Gyro and Johnny meet, the Boomboom family. Vols. 5-12 (to about ch. 48): the Corpse plot, Ringo's duel, the Rockies, Sandman. Vols. 13-19 (to about ch. 76): Lucy's infiltration, Valentine revealed, Philadelphia and the Lake Michigan battles. Vols. 20-24 (ch. 77-95): Love Train, Gyro's death, the final duel, the alternate Diego and the end of the race.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Johnny says he wants to climb from below zero back to zero. What does that mean, and what does it take?
2. Valentine says everything he did was for his country. Can loving your country ever justify hurting other people?
3. Gyro broke his family's rules to save a boy he thought was innocent. When is it right to disobey?
4. Johnny killed Valentine instead of accepting his offer. Was that justice, revenge or self-defense?
5. Lucy was 14 and very brave. How did the adults around her protect her, and how did some fail her?
