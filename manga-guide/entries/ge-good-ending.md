**Premise.** Seiji Utsumi, a shy high-school boy, has a crush on Shou Iketani, an older, cheerful member of the tennis club. Classmate Yuki Kurokawa catches him peeping at Shou and, instead of reporting him, makes him join the club and coaches him on how to win her (Wikipedia). When Shou starts dating someone else, Utsumi is crushed; he meets Risa Onuma at a part-time job and panics when Kurokawa goes on a date (Kodansha vol. 3). He falls for Kurokawa, who is guarded because of her past: an ex-boyfriend, Tohru, and a recording of them together that his friend secretly made and spread around school (TV Tropes). Utsumi and Kurokawa date and break up; Utsumi announces he is dating Shou, which upsets their friend Eri and splits the group (Kodansha vol. 12); he and Shou sleep together (ch. 120, TV Tropes). Tohru returns, violent and obsessive, and beats Utsumi before Kurokawa confronts him (ch. 148-151). Utsumi and Kurokawa reunite (ch. 155), and the last chapter jumps ten years ahead to show them married with children (ch. 156, TV Tropes).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you plan your way into love? Utsumi starts by watching from a distance and then by following a classmate's strategy. The series asks what happens when real people, with histories and wounds, refuse to follow the plan.

**The answer the story gives.** No: love requires honesty, patience and respect for the other person's past. Strategy produces lies (Utsumi's false announcement, vol. 12) and heartbreak; honesty and courage produce the "good ending" (ch. 155).

**Characters as arguments.** Utsumi argues for growth: a timid boy who learns to stand up (ch. 148-150). Kurokawa argues that trust has to be rebuilt after betrayal; her refusal to be rushed is vindicated. Shou is the case for warm impulse without depth. Tohru is the case against possessive love, and the friend who spread the recording is the case against treating a person as a trophy.

**Symbols and motifs.** The tennis club as the place where the game of love is played. The recording as the past that will not stay private. The title itself: the story keeps asking what a good ending is.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōnen romantic drama with love triangles and fan service; the coming-of-age story. Faith readers may notice the ending's turn to marriage and family after a story that treated teen sex as normal.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and virtue (honesty, courage) are endorsed; hedonism is explored with costs; strategy-as-romance is criticized.

**Shifts across the series.** Early volumes are comedy with fan service and a peeping premise; the middle volumes bring Kurokawa's past and the love polygon (ch. 80-120); the last volumes turn darker with Tohru's return (ch. 148-151) before the time-skip ending (ch. 156).

**Discussion questions.**
1. The story starts with Utsumi peeping. Is that funny, or is it a problem the story should have taken more seriously?
2. Why did Kurokawa ask Utsumi what she asked in ch. 87, and why was his refusal the right answer?
3. What made spreading the recording so harmful, and who was responsible?
4. Was Utsumi's strategy dating honest?
5. What makes an ending "good"?
