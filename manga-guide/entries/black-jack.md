**Premise.** Black Jack (real name Kuro Hazama) is a scarred, unlicensed genius surgeon who works for anyone who can pay his enormous fees and is thought heartless; most stories show him doing good that goes unrecognized, curing the poor for free or humbling the arrogant, and some end with a good person dying despite everything (English Wikipedia). Each story stands alone. Recurring threads: his childhood paralysis and recovery under his mentor Dr. Honma (v1, "Sometimes Like Pearls," "The Legs of an Ant"); the skin graft on his face from his half-African friend Takashi (v2, "Where Art Thou, Friend?"); his lost love Megumi Kisaragi, who after cancer surgery lives as a man and becomes a ship's doctor (v1, "Confluence"); his rival Dr. Kiriko, a former army doctor who performs euthanasia (v3, "Two Dark Doctors"; he later ends his own father's life, v5, "There Was a Valve!"); and Pinoko, a girl he assembles from a parasitic twin's organs inside a child-sized artificial body (v1, "Teratoid Cystoma"), who keeps house, assists in surgery, insists she is 18 and calls herself his wife as a running joke (English Wikipedia). Tezuka said he wrote it not to showcase medical technology but to portray doctors' dilemmas: whether prolonging life is the doctor's mission, and whether it makes the patient happy (Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Every life is worth fighting for with all one's skill, whatever the law, the price or the odds, yet medicine cannot conquer death, and pride, greed and cruelty are the real diseases.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Osamu Tezuka (1928-1989), the most influential figure in postwar manga, creator of Astro Boy, Phoenix, Buddha and Dororo; he studied medicine and held a medical license, but his medical knowledge dated from the late 1940s and early 1950s and he had little surgical experience, so some cases contain errors (Japanese Wikipedia; Vertical's page).
- On-record intent: Tezuka said he drew Black Jack to depict doctors' dilemmas, such as whether prolonging life is the doctor's mission and whether it makes the patient happy, not to introduce medical technology (Japanese Wikipedia).
- 1977 protest: ch. 153 used the word lobotomy for an electrical brain operation on a cerebral palsy patient; groups for people with cerebral palsy and for lobotomy victims protested, and Tezuka and Akita Shoten apologized in five national newspapers (February 10, 1977). The story was redrawn with a different illness; two other brain-surgery stories (ch. 41 "Vegetable," ch. 58 "The Seat of Pleasure") were left out of collections (Japanese Wikipedia; English Wikipedia's list of uncollected chapters).
- Tezuka asked that several stories be withheld; some were released decades later and three appear in Vertical's limited hardcovers (Vertical's page; English Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Akita Shoten (Weekly Shōnen Champion). Vertical Inc., later part of Kodansha USA (from general knowledge; to verify), published the English edition.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a doctor owe a patient, and what does anyone owe death? Tezuka framed it plainly: is prolonging life the doctor's mission, and does it make the patient happy (Japanese Wikipedia)?

**The answer the story gives.** Fight for life with everything you have, and accept that you will sometimes lose. Black Jack almost never gives up, and the stories usually honor that, but many end with death anyway, so the series refuses to make the surgeon a god.

**Characters as arguments.** Black Jack argues for life at any cost and for conscience over rules. Dr. Kiriko argues that ending suffering can be mercy; he is a rival rather than a villain and sometimes the two work together (Wikipedia). Dr. Honma argues for medicine as a lifelong vow. Biwamaru argues for the body's own healing and learns humility. Pinoko argues that love makes a home even for a doctor who claims to want none.

**Symbols and motifs.** The scar and the two-toned face (a friend's gift of skin); the black cape; the scalpel; huge fees that are often a test; Pinoko's assembled body as a picture of life made from fragments.

**Traditions it echoes.** The physician's oath to preserve life; the Good Samaritan for Christian readers and pikuach nefesh for Jewish readers; the Buddhist teaching on impermanence in stories where death cannot be beaten; postwar Japanese anti-war and anti-nuclear art.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism, virtue and duty are affirmed in Black Jack himself. The case for euthanasia is heard and usually rejected. Institutions are criticized for pride and profit. Supernatural stories are rare and, to reviewers, the weakest (Manga Bookshelf).

**Shifts across the series.** Vertical's volumes follow Tezuka's own deluxe-edition order rather than magazine order, so each mixes early and late stories. The early stories were darker (shelved as horror in Japan); later ones lean more humane (Japanese Wikipedia). The final volumes feature many identity-changing plastic surgeries (Experiments in Manga).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Black Jack charges huge fees but sometimes works for nothing. Why?
2. Dr. Kiriko ends lives to end suffering. What does Black Jack say to that, and what do you think?
3. Some patients die even when Black Jack does everything right. What do those stories teach?
4. Black Jack has no license. Is it ever right to break the rules to save a life?
5. Why does Pinoko matter so much to Black Jack, even though he acts like he does not care?
