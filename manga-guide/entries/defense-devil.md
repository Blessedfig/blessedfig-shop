**Premise.** Kucabara, the gentle third son of the great demon king, has been stripped of his powers and banished by his younger brother, Legato. To win back his strength he needs "dark matter," which he can earn by acting as a Defense Devil: a lawyer who proves that a soul sent toward hell is innocent before a shinigami (death god) drags it away. His cases include a motorcyclist whose brakes failed (ch. 1), a girl wrongly judged a suicide who was really tripped onto the tracks by a shinigami (ch. 2-6), and a hunter who took the blame for a death caused by children (ch. 7-14). Because he is kind, he keeps spending the dark matter to save people. With his dragon companion Bichula and Idamaria, an exorcist nun first sent to kill him (ch. 15-19), he trains at an orphanage run by Father Selma and enters the demon world to stop Legato (vol. 4 on), crossing hells where living humans are tortured for dark matter (vol. 5). The final volumes reveal that Legato wanted to destroy the angels who secretly rule hell; the fallen angel Elimona plotted to wake Lilith, the goddess of hell, inside Idamaria; God summons Kucabara to heaven, and the series ends with Kucabara recruiting his friends to defend God, who has been betrayed by his angels (vols. 9-10; Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who deserves a defense? Kucabara is a demon, the creature everyone expects to accuse and drag souls down, and he takes the opposite job: proving that the condemned are innocent. The series asks whether kindness is weakness, and whether the powers that judge us can be trusted.

**The answer the story gives.** Kindness is the real strength, and judgment without a fair hearing is corrupt. Case after case shows that the "sinner" was framed, mistaken or shielding someone else: a failed brake (ch. 1), a shinigami who tripped a girl onto the tracks (ch. 2-6), a hunter who took the blame for children (ch. 7-14). In the end the corruption reaches all the way up, to angels who rule hell and betray God (vols. 9-10).

**Characters as arguments.** Kucabara argues for mercy, even at the cost of his own power. Bichula argues for self-interest and keeps being won over. Idamaria, raised to kill demons, argues for duty and learns to trust. Legato argues that power belongs to whoever takes it. Samus, who was betrayed by every friend, argues that bonds are lies, and is answered when Kucabara saves her life (vol. 7). Father Selma, a killer given a second life, shows that people can change.

**Symbols and motifs.** Dark matter, the price of each rescue. The courtroom brought into the afterlife. The orphanage as home. Hell as a set of factories that torture the living for profit (vol. 5). Kucabara's smile, which reviewers single out.

**Traditions it echoes.** The courtroom drama and the "devil's advocate"; the scriptural picture of Satan as the accuser, reversed; the Buddhist image of a compassionate being who enters hell to rescue others; fallen-angel and Lilith legends from Christian and Jewish folklore.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Liberal ideas of due process and classical virtues of mercy and loyalty are affirmed. Christian imagery is used freely and reversed in places, with God honored at the end. Occult elements are the fantasy machinery. Authoritarian rule, whether demonic, angelic or ecclesiastical, is criticized.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-27) are case-of-the-week defense stories, the part most reviewers like best. Volumes 4-8 (ch. 28-78) become a journey through hell to rescue Jupiter's parents and confront Legato, with more battles and, per reviewers, more fan service from about ch. 50. Volumes 9-10 (ch. 79-100) bring Elimona's plot, Lilith, the angels and God, and end quickly; several reviewers call the finale rushed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kucabara keeps giving away the power he needs. Is that foolish or brave?
2. A girl was judged guilty of a death she did not choose (ch. 2-6). Why does the story care so much about getting the facts right?
3. Paul took the blame so that children would not carry it (ch. 7-14). Was that right?
4. In this story some angels are villains and a demon is a hero. Why might an author flip those roles, and what do you believe about them?
5. Samus decided all friendships were lies. What changed her mind?
