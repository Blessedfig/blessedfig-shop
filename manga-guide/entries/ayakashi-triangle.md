**Premise.** Matsuri Kazamaki, 15, is an exorcist ninja of the Kazamaki clan who has secretly guarded his childhood friend Suzu Kanade, an Ayakashi Medium whose overflowing life energy draws spirits (ayakashi) to her and makes many of them revere her (Wikipedia; fan wiki gives both as 15). The night before high school starts, the King of Ayakashi, Shirogane, tries to devour Suzu; Matsuri seals most of his power into a scroll, but Shirogane curses him into a girl's body so the two can never be a couple (ch. 1). Matsuri goes to Hokusai High School as a girl, where Suzu's friends Yayoi and Lucy do not know his secret, and the pair face friendly and malicious ayakashi, rival exorcist clans (Soga and Haya Ninokuru), the painter-ayakashi Garaku, and the Jinyo, human-shaped spirits born from negative thoughts (Sosuke Hinojiki, ch. 17-22, 62-67). Suzu briefly becomes King of Ayakashi (ch. 23), learns to fight with origami and a remote copy of herself, and wins back ayakashi Matsuri had driven away (Tanumaro). Matsuri regains his male body for a few hours in the Jinyo arc; they confess and kiss, but he will not date her until he is male again (TV Tropes; ch. 67-69). Suzu later gets the chance to undo the curse by changing the past and refuses, because the change made Matsuri grow (TV Tropes, ch. 87-88). The final arc reveals that the Gogyosen, founders of the Exorcist Ninja Association, are controlling Matsuri to destroy the Ayakashi Medium; defeating them may cost him his male body forever (PRH vol. 16 blurb; ch. 135-144).

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame finds something to admire in Matsuri's loyalty and Suzu's mercy toward spirits. The divide runs along two lines. First, the sexual comedy: constant nudity and groping involving 15-year-old characters, presented as fun, weighs as an endorsed message for most Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint and Historically Black Protestant readers (D or F), and as a consent and age problem for progressive, secular and Unitarian readers (C). Second, the gender-swap: progressive and some secular readers welcome its treatment of attraction beyond sex, while traditional and most religious frames read a playful change of sex as a concern in itself. Shinto, Pagan and Satanist frames read the spirit world and the identity theme most warmly (B).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who show mercy and loyalty (Suzu, Matsuri, the reconciled ayakashi) win; those who want to devour or control others (Sosuke, the Gogyosen) are destroyed, while Shirogane, the original villain, is tamed into a grumbling ally.

## Creator and publisher context

- Kentaro Yabuki (story and art), born February 4, 1980, in Okayama. He learned to draw manga from Akira Toriyama's Dragon Ball, assisted Takeshi Obata on Hikaru no Go at 18, debuted with Yamato Gensōki (1998), then created Black Cat (2000-2004, over 12 million copies) and illustrated To Love Ru and To Love Ru Darkness (with Saki Hasemi, over 16 million copies combined) and the Darling in the Franxx manga (2018-2020) (Wikipedia, Kentaro Yabuki).
- On-record intent: in July 2020 he said he planned several "relationship triangles" beyond romance; the premise and character designs come from his 2019 one-shot Leo × Leo; Omiko City is modeled on an area near Odawara (Wikipedia, Production). No interview on the fan service or the gender theme was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If your body changed overnight, would you still be you, and would the person who loves you still love you? Matsuri's curse is meant to end his romance with Suzu; instead it tests whether their bond depends on bodies at all (Wikipedia, Themes).

**The answer the story gives.** Love and identity outlast the body. Suzu loves Matsuri in both forms, and when she can erase the curse by changing the past she declines, judging the change made him grow (TV Tropes, ch. 87-88). Matsuri, for his part, keeps a male sense of self and holds back from dating until he is himself again, so the story honors both the constancy of the person and his wish to be whole.

**Characters as arguments.** Matsuri argues for duty and constancy. Suzu argues for compassion: she heals spirits, cries for enemies and wins back those Matsuri drove away. Shirogane argues for appetite and power, then shrinks into a comic housecat and an ally. Tanumaro argues that humans and spirits can never share a world and is answered by Suzu's kindness. Sosuke, a spirit born from human negativity, shows what devouring others makes of you. The Gogyosen argue for hidden control and lose.

**Symbols and motifs.** The triangle of the title: Matsuri, Suzu and Shirogane, and later the many "relationship triangles" Yabuki promised. The pinwheel that blows away possession without harming the host. The hairpin Suzu wears for their friendship. The three-pillar torii in Suzu's mind (TV Tropes). Reincarnated lovers who find each other again (ch. 57, 144).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese tales of shape-changing spirits and kami, Ranma ½-style gender-swap comedy (from general knowledge), Yabuki's own To Love Ru, and Russian folklore (Snegurochka, Ded Moroz). Religious readers will notice that spirits are treated as neighbors to befriend, which Shinto readers find natural and many Christian, Jewish and Muslim readers do not.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The spirit world (Pagan/occult) is affirmed as a place for friendship as well as danger. Identity and attraction beyond sex (Expressive individualism, Progressivism) are explored warmly. Hedonism is affirmed as comedy, and that is where most frames part ways with the series: groping and nudity of 15-year-olds are played for laughs. A hidden authority (the Gogyosen) is critiqued. Suzu's nonviolence is honored but not the story's whole rule.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-34) set up the curse, school life and the Jinyo and are what the anime covers. Volumes 5-8 (ch. 35-70) add Rochka and the Ayakashi Medium's past lives, and give Matsuri his male body for a few hours. Volumes 9-12 (ch. 71-106) include the skipped chapters and Suzu's choice not to undo the curse, the last story in Weekly Shōnen Jump. Volumes 13-16 (ch. 107-144) on Shōnen Jump+ bring the Gogyosen and the finale.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Matsuri says he is not really himself as a girl. What makes a person who they are?
2. Suzu could undo the curse and chooses not to. Was that fair to Matsuri?
3. The series makes groping a running joke. How would those scenes feel if they happened to a real friend?
4. Suzu tries to make peace with spirits that want to hurt her. When is mercy wise, and when is it dangerous?
5. Why do you think two chapters were skipped in English, and should publishers do that?
