**Premise.** Arikawa Yōichi, a cheerful, near-sighted third-year university student with many friends and a girlfriend, keeps running into Misaki Shōta, a quiet third-year agriculture student: once when Misaki trips with potted plants, once at a rainy station, where Arikawa picks up Misaki's flower necklace by mistake. He starts helping in Professor Tsujimura's plant lab, where Misaki works, and Misaki slowly lets his guard down. Misaki lost his parents in an accident in his first year of junior high and was raised by his grandfather, a plant scientist, who has since died. His past is tied to Kawabata Satoru, an older man who worked on the grandfather's research, tutored Misaki, became attached to him while Misaki was a high-school student, then dated and became engaged to a woman, Fukui, to seem "normal," while continuing to call and control Misaki for years. Arikawa breaks up with his girlfriend; when Kawabata confronts Misaki at a station, Arikawa confesses and the two become a couple (likely ch. 9, end of vol. 2). Volume 3 follows their new relationship, including sexual scenes between the two adult students, and Kawabata calls off his wedding (ch. 11) (TV Tropes; Mangapedia; Goodreads and MyAnimeList reader reviews).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can someone who has lost everyone, and been held too tightly by the one person who stayed, learn to trust love again? Misaki's parents died when he was young, his grandfather is gone, and Kawabata, who was meant to look after him, kept him close while living a public lie.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly, through love that is patient and honest. Arikawa is everything Kawabata was not: open about his feelings, willing to end his old relationship cleanly, and content to wait. By the end of vol. 2 Misaki can say what he feels; by the end of vol. 3 he is in his first real relationship (TV Tropes; Goodreads; vol. 3 blurb).

**Characters as arguments.** Arikawa argues that love should be spoken and free. Kawabata argues, by his life, that feelings can be hidden behind a respectable engagement and managed by control; the story shows that this hurts him, Misaki and his fiancée, and lets him change in ch. 11. Misaki carries grief and the habit of keeping distance. The professor and the grandfather stand for gentle care.

**Symbols and motifs.** Flowers and plants: the title, the lab, the greenhouse the grandfather left, the full-page kiss in a flower field (ch. 4). The flower necklace from Kawabata, which Misaki wants to be rid of but cannot throw away (Mangapedia), is the sign of an attachment he has not let go. Near-sighted Arikawa, who cannot recognize faces without glasses, learns to see one person clearly.

**Traditions it echoes.** The campus romance and the possessive older rival are boys' love staples. Readers from many traditions will recognize the contrast between love that seeks the other's good and love that possesses (1 Corinthians 13; Buddhist teaching on attachment), even where they disagree about the relationship itself.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism win: honesty about one's feelings is rewarded. Traditional expectation is shown mostly through Kawabata's pretense, so it appears as a source of harm rather than as a good. Virtue is in patience and truthfulness.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (likely ch. 1-4): meetings, the lab, the stolen kiss and the flower-field kiss. Vol. 2 (likely ch. 5-9): Kawabata's calls and the backstory, the break-up with the girlfriend, the confession at the station; a bonus story shows the two as children (Goodreads). Vol. 3 (likely ch. 10-12): the new relationship, sexual scenes and Kawabata's change of heart.
