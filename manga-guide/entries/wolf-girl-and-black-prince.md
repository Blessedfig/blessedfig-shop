**Premise.** High-school student Erika Shinohara wants to stay in a clique of girls who brag about boyfriends, so she invents one and shows a photo of a handsome stranger. He turns out to be Kyoya Sata, the most popular boy at her school, who seems princely in public. He agrees to pose as her boyfriend on one condition: she must act as his obedient "dog" (ch. 1). He is cynical and sharp-tongued in private, but he protects her from a boy who tries to use her for revenge (v1), and when the fake arrangement breaks down he makes her his real girlfriend (likely v4). Over three years of high school they face rivals (Kusakabe, Kamiya, Erika's cousin Lena, Kasai and Erika's old friend Terasaki, who slaps her and is beaten by Kyoya, ch. 43), Kyoya's estrangement from his mother after his parents' divorce (v6), and Erika's search for a calling, which becomes glassmaking in Kyoto (v14-15). They first sleep together in ch. 54 (v15), as high-school seniors (fan wiki). The epilogue (ch. 58, v16) shows them years later, married, with a daughter (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is love when it starts as a lie? Erika invents a boyfriend to belong, and the boy she picks agrees only if she becomes his obedient pet. The series asks whether something real can grow from a fake beginning.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, but only through honesty and change on both sides. Kyoya's cynicism gives way to protectiveness and then to plain tenderness; Erika stops lying, tells her friends how it began (v14) and finds a life of her own in glassmaking. The epilogue rewards them with marriage and a child (ch. 58). The story does not fully question its starting point; critics differ on whether the growth earns the romance (ANN).

**Characters as arguments.** Kyoya argues that the world is fake and people are shallow, and his own love disproves him. Erika argues for stubborn devotion. Kamiya argues for playing the field and changes his mind. Kasai argues that the relationship is dysfunctional; the story lets her speak, then answers her with Kyoya's loyalty. Terasaki shows what possessive "love" really looks like: he hits her (ch. 43).

**Symbols and motifs.** The leash and the dog, joked about to the very last volume's description. The photo of a stranger that starts the lie. Glass: fragile, shaped by heat and patience, like the relationship.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo "black prince" romance and the fake-dating trope; Beauty and the Beast in reverse (a cold prince softened by a devoted girl; my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism and virtue ethics win: friends and honesty save the couple. Traditionalism closes the story with marriage and family. Feminism appears mostly as a question critics ask of the series.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4 (ch. 1-14) are the fake relationship and its collapse, with the harshest treatment of Erika. Volumes 5-9 (ch. 15-33) are the couple's first year together and new rivals. Volumes 10-13 (ch. 34-47) bring the hot-springs trip, Terasaki and the class trip. Volumes 14-16 (ch. 48-58) turn to the future: careers, Kyoto, graduation, first night together (ch. 54) and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Erika lied to fit in. Why did it feel necessary, and what did it cost her?
2. Kyoya's deal was that Erika act like his dog. Is that ever an okay thing to ask of someone?
3. Kasai thought their relationship was dysfunctional. Was she right at the start? By the end?
4. Terasaki said he loved Erika, then hit her. How is his "love" different from Kyoya's?
5. The story ends in marriage. What does it say makes a relationship last?
