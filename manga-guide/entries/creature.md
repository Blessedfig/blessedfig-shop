**Premise.** Akira Takashiro, a second-year bench player on his Tachikawa (Tokyo) high school basketball team, has just made the starting lineup and agreed with his friend and rival Eiji that whoever scores more will confess to their childhood friend Miku. At lunchtime a huge earthquake knocks him out; he wakes to piles of bodies and monsters eating them. With class president Nao Shirasaki, the delinquent Tadokoro, a film-buff student, a salaryman and a burly gym teacher, Takeshige, he flees through a Tachikawa sealed off by a vast trench, searching for Miku, while a black-uniformed special force captures monsters and ignores survivors (Japanese Wikipedia; ANN; OASG). Part 1 has three arcs. Akira (vols. 1-4): escape from Tachikawa ends with their helicopter shot down. Miku (vols. 5-13): outside the wall, Miku and Eiji's younger brother Kenji try to expose the cover-up; monsters hit Kyoto, Odaiba and Shinjuku; humans fused with monsters ("Fuse" soldiers, among them Takeshige and Eiji) fight giant creatures; Eiji dies; Takeshige uproots Tokyo Skytree, wakes the giant "Emperor" and then seals it with his own body; Akira awakens as a new human, an "Evol." Five years later an Evol unit of young people (only those 18 and under are eligible) fights a last battle in which 38 of 42 die. Part 2 (vols. 14-21) follows Mio, the daughter of Akira and Nao, raised in an underground shelter town, "Cluster 7," where cannibal "Variants" hide among people. She goes to a ruined surface, finds her father guarding a cloning machine at Osaka Castle, loses him in battle, and faces a cloned Takeshige who reveals that the creatures are the Earth's own will to clear away humanity; he finally destroys the "Destruction Tree" that bears them. Fifteen years later, the creatures are dwindling, and Mio and her husband Ren hunt them with their children (Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**When the world ends, love and loyalty to the people beside you are what keep you human; those who rule by fear or treat people as tools destroy themselves, and humanity can survive even its own failures.**

| Pragmatic | Survival by planning and cooperation: the film buff's video becomes the bargaining chip for a helicopter ride; the government saves only those who can become soldiers. |
| Religious | No religion. A "savior" (Takeshige) is cloned and resurrected as a destroyer; a "Tree of Life" (the Destruction Tree) bears monsters. Religious readers may notice the inverted imagery. |
| Socio-cultural | Distrust of government: a secret military captures monsters and lets citizens die, the media is muzzled, and children are made into soldiers. The earthquake opening echoes Japanese disaster fears. ANN reviewers found the plot generic and the monster art the draw. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — humanity survives and the epilogue shows a new family fighting on, but nearly every Part 1 character and Akira himself die along the way (Japanese Wikipedia).
Hope source: P — the story's way through is protecting others: Nao dies shielding Mio, Akira dies protecting her, and even Takeshige ends by saving the young; family (F) is the close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do people become when the world ends and every rule falls away? Akira keeps looking for one girl; Takeshige, a teacher with a hidden past, becomes a tyrant; the government decides some people are worth more than others.

**The answer the story gives.** Love and loyalty keep people human; fear and domination turn them into monsters. Those who sacrifice for others (Eiji, Nao, Akira) are honored; those who use people (the army, Saotome, Takeshige) are destroyed or must sacrifice to redeem themselves (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Akira argues for never giving up on the people you love. Takeshige is the argument that a man who preys on the weak becomes a monster in fact, yet even he ends by saving the young. Nao is calm courage. The army is the case against cold triage. Mio, in Part 2, moves from revenge to protection.

**Symbols and motifs.** Matching wristbands Miku gave the boys; Eiji's arm with the wristband (ch. 1); the trench around Tachikawa; Skytree as a spike sealing the Emperor; the Destruction Tree bearing monsters; clones of the dead.

**Traditions it echoes.** Kaiju and tokusatsu films, zombie-survival horror, Gantz and Attack on Titan (MyAnimeList reviewers), and the eco-apocalypse in which nature strikes back.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Might makes right and authoritarianism are shown and condemned; transhumanism is used without much reflection; environmentalism arrives as a late reveal; communitarian loyalty is what wins.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4: survival horror in Tachikawa. Vols. 5-13: conspiracy and city-wide kaiju war, ending in a five-year time skip. Vols. 14-21 (Part 2): a new hero, a shelter town hiding cannibals, a ruined Osaka and the final reveal, then a 15-year epilogue (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Akira keeps looking for Miku even when it seems hopeless. Why does that matter to the story?
2. Takeshige seems helpful at first. What were the warning signs that he was dangerous?
3. The army saves monsters for study but not people. Who should decide who gets saved?
4. Several characters die to save friends. What do their choices say about love?
5. The story says the Earth sent the monsters. What do you think humans owe the planet?
