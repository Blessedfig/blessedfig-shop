**Premise.** In 1999, 15-year-old Kamui Shiro, an esper, returns to Tokyo after six years away to keep his dying mother's wish that he change fate. Two groups of seven gather. The Dragons of Heaven, guided by Princess Hinoto, a dreamseer whose hidden chamber is in the National Diet Building, guard the kekkai: spiritual barriers, in the form of landmarks such as Tokyo Tower, that hold back the end of the world. The Dragons of Earth, gathered by Hinoto's sister Kanoe, want to destroy the barriers so that earthquakes can rid the planet of humanity, which they see as killing it. Kamui must choose a side. He chooses Heaven because his only wish is to protect his childhood friends Kotori and Fuma Monou; at that moment Fuma becomes his opposite, the Kamui of the Dragons of Earth, and kills Kotori (vol. 8). Kamui falls into catatonia, is drawn back by Subaru Sumeragi (vol. 9), and decides his wish is to bring the old Fuma back. Across the later volumes barrier after barrier falls and Tokyo collapses and floods; Subaru kills Seishiro Sakurazuka, the man he loved and who killed his sister (vol. 16), and then joins the Dragons of Earth (vols. 17-18.5). The published story stops at the final duel between Kamui and Fuma (Wikipedia; List of X chapters).

## Life message

**The future is not fixed: each person chooses, by what they most truly wish, whether to protect or destroy, and love for particular people is the reason to save the world, even when it costs everything.**

Kinship and bonded pairs (Kamui and Fuma, Sorata and Arashi, Subaru and Seishiro) decide loyalties; the Dragons of Earth argue that humans are a plague on the planet's body. Mothers die bearing sacred swords (vols. 5-7). |
| Pragmatic | Strategy is mostly defense of landmarks: each kekkai battle (Sunshine 60, Shinjuku, Yamanote Line, Rainbow Bridge) is a test of who will protect people at the cost of themselves (vols. 11-17). Practical problem-solving matters less than will and wish. |
| Religious | A deliberate blend: the Book of Revelation (Tokyo as Babylon; seven seals; seven angels), Kamui as a messiah figure, an Ise Shrine priestess, a Shingon monk from Mount Kōya, onmyōji (yin-yang diviners), a Catholic heroine and Kabbalah imagery (Wikipedia). Animerica called it a "surreal blend of Taoist, Shinto, and even European mysticism" (via Wikipedia). The apocalypse is environmental, not divine judgment. Faith readers will weigh sorcery used by heroes and the reworking of Scripture. |
| Socio-cultural | A shōjo-magazine epic about Japan's state of affairs, its environment and family bonds; Wikipedia notes the themes go unspoken rather than stated. Serialization was paused after real disasters and crimes, and editors balked at the planned ending's graphic nature (Wikipedia; Garrity). Same-sex love (Subaru and Seishiro) and a sex worker (Karen) are drawn sympathetically. |

Outlook: Bleak — the published story ends with most of Tokyo sunk, many heroes dead or turned, and the final duel unresolved; Kotori's last message, that the future is still undecided, is the thread of hope.
Hope source: F — bonds of family and friendship: the Dragons of Heaven draw strength from their ties to one another (Garrity, ANN), and Kamui's wish is to bring his friend back; protecting others (P) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** The divide is over the spiritual world and over sexuality. Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness and Christian Science readings treat heroic sorcery and a rewritten Armageddon as the message itself (F), and most other Christian, Jewish, Muslim and Bahá'í readings weigh that, plus sex outside marriage and the sympathetic sex worker, against strong themes of sacrifice and choice (C or D). Shinto, Buddhist, Pagan, progressive and secular readers find a pluralist tragedy about choice, love and the planet (A or B), while progressive readers question tying Arashi's power to her virginity. Everyone weighs the extreme gore as a depiction concern.

## Creator and publisher context

- CLAMP, a four-member group: Nanase Ohkawa (story), Mokona (lead art), Tsubaki Nekoi and Satsuki Igarashi; their earlier and later works include Tokyo Babylon (whose characters Subaru and Seishiro return here), Magic Knight Rayearth, Cardcaptor Sakura, xxxHolic and Tsubasa (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, citing Ohkawa): the story grew from an end-of-the-world idea she had in middle school; Go Nagai's Devilman shaped its themes of good, evil and fear; she is fascinated by dualism, the good and bad in every person; mankind saves the Earth to save itself, and she asks who will risk themselves to save the Earth; she believes individuals choose their destiny as they choose right and wrong.
- CLAMP worried about the amount of gore, especially Kotori's death, but feared softening it would harm the work; Ohkawa said the deaths were meant to be cruel (Wikipedia). The planned ending was judged too graphic by Monthly Asuka's editors in 2003, and CLAMP chose to stop rather than censor it (Garrity, ANN). **Status: Needs more info on the creators** (no interview opened directly).
- Publisher: Kadokawa Shoten (Monthly Asuka, shōjo). VIZ Media (English), rated T+ (Older Teen) for the 3-in-1 edition.
- Controversies: hiatuses after the 1995 Kobe earthquake and the 1997 Kobe child murders, when CLAMP was criticized for similarities between fictional and real killings (Wikipedia). No controversies involving the creators personally were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If the end of the world were foretold, and you held the deciding vote, would you save humanity or the planet, and could you change what has been prophesied? Kamui is told his choice decides everything, and that whichever side he picks, his best friend will take the other.

**The answer the story gives.** Choose by what you truly love, and accept the cost. Kamui chooses Heaven to protect Kotori and Fuma, and loses both; yet the story insists, through Tokiko, Kotori and Ohkawa's own comments, that the future is not fixed (Wikipedia). Because the manga stops before its ending, the final answer is withheld.

**Characters as arguments.** Kamui argues for particular love over abstract duty. Fuma argues that people should get what they truly wish, even death. Kusanagi argues for the planet without hatred of people; Satsuki argues that there is no reason human life is worth more than the Earth's. Sorata embraces his fate and chooses whom to die for. Subaru shows a love that wants its own destruction. Karen, a Catholic and a sex worker, argues for compassion without condition.

**Symbols and motifs.** Twin stars and two Kamuis; sacred swords born from women's bodies; the cross and crucifixion (the title itself is a cross shape); kekkai as invisible walls holding the world together; dreams as the place where the future is seen and fought over; Tokyo as Babylon.

**Traditions it echoes.** Devilman's two friends destined to fight at Armageddon, the Hakkenden's band of destined warriors, Teito Monogatari's occult Tokyo and the Book of Revelation (Wikipedia). Readers of many faiths will hear their own scriptures borrowed and changed.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Environmentalism is taken seriously but its extreme form, wiping out humanity, is the villains' creed. Existential choice is the heroes' faith. The spirit world is real and is how people fight. Nihilism and fatalism belong to the Dragons of Earth and to the despairing, while hope belongs to those who keep their bonds.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-8 gather the two sides and build to Kotori's death; ANN noted the story turns after the eighth volume (Wikipedia). Vols. 9-15 are barrier battles and side chapters on each character's past. Vols. 16-18.5 bring the deaths of Seishiro, Nataku and Kanoe, Arashi's loss of power, and the start of the final duel, where the story stops.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kamui chose to protect two people instead of choosing for the whole world. Was that selfish or the right reason?
2. The Dragons of Earth say humans are destroying the planet. Where are they right, and where do they go wrong?
3. Fuma grants people their "true wish," even death. Why is that frightening?
4. Arashi loses her power after she and Sorata are together. What do you think of that rule, and why might the authors include it?
5. If you were told your future, would you try to change it?
