**Premise.** Maruo Kaido, a teenage manga fan beaten by his father as a child, copied the brutal training of manga heroes until at seven he could break rocks and beat his father; his strength gets him expelled and makes ordinary life impossible (fan wiki; ANN). He rescues Urara, an oni who looks like a young girl, from a gang of ayashimon (shape-shifting yokai) yakuza, and joins her one-woman gang by a blood-exchange sakazuki pledge (ch. 1-2). In Tokyo's Kabukicho, where yokai gangs settle disputes by ritual duels, Urara wants revenge on whoever killed her father Kio, the first chairman of the Enma Syndicate, and to win back his crest. Maruo fights a border guard, a scam gang (freeing the timid yokai Ten), and the Enma Syndicate's new chairman, Doppo Akari, who turns living victims into horror art and beats Maruo badly (ch. 6-10). The three recruit the Todoroki biker gang (ch. 11-18) and storm the Kori Hotel of the tanuki and kitsune (ch. 19-25). The cancelled run jumps ahead and stops just before the final fight with Doppo (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Strength is for protecting someone, and family is the people who pledge themselves to each other and keep their word; a boy hurt by his father finds a home by fighting for others instead of for himself.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is strength for? Maruo made himself superhuman to escape a father who beat him, but when he finally beat his father he felt nothing, and his strength cost him school and every job. Ayashimon asks what a boy built for fighting should do with himself.

**The answer the story gives.** Use it for someone. Maruo's first instinct is to defend Urara from a gang (ch. 1), and his strength gains meaning only when it is pledged to her. The sakazuki makes them family (ch. 11). The series says this playfully, through a yakuza fantasy, so the "family" it offers is an outlaw gang; the values it rewards (loyalty, keeping one's word, protecting the weak) are older than the setting.

**Characters as arguments.** Maruo argues that a life can be built from the stories you love. Urara argues for legacy and revenge, then learns that using people is not leadership and apologizes to her two followers (ch. 11). Ten argues that cowards can change. Doppo is the case against power that feeds on fear: he treats people as material for art (ch. 6-7). Tamagawa argues for business and for loyalty to his own boss, at the cost of himself (ch. 23).

**Symbols and motifs.** The sakazuki cup and the blood exchange. The irezumi drawn first in marker, then inked (ch. 11). Kamehameha as a prayer to fiction. The Kori Hotel, a luxurious trap that turns out to be a folklore joke (ch. 21). The ritual duel, which makes violence rule-bound.

**Traditions it echoes.** Yakuza cinema's honor code and the ninkyo ideal of the chivalrous outlaw (from general knowledge; to verify); the shonen tradition of training and rivals; Japanese yokai folklore. Readers from honor cultures will recognize the pledge and the duel; readers from faith traditions will weigh the gang setting against the loyalty.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Honor-code and communitarian lenses are affirmed. Might makes right is explored: strength decides, but the cruel use of strength (Doppo) is condemned. Postmodern play with fiction runs through the whole book. Occult elements are folklore color.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-7) sets up Maruo, Urara and the duel system and introduces Doppo's cruelty. Volume 2 (ch. 8-16) has Maruo's first defeat, the pledge and the biker gang. Volume 3 (ch. 17-25) is the Kori Hotel and a rushed ending that stops before the final fight.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Maruo trained to escape his father. What else could he have done with that strength?
2. Why does Urara apologize in ch. 11, and why does it matter?
3. Is a yakuza gang a family? What would make any group a family?
4. Doppo calls cruelty art. How does the story show he is wrong?
5. What is the difference between living like a manga hero and being a good person?
