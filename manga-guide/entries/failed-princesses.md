**Premise.** Fujishiro Nanaki is popular, fashionable and proud of being cute; she has no time for plain, bookish classmates like Kurokawa Kanade, a quiet manga and anime fan. When Nanaki learns that her boyfriend is cheating on her, and that she is the second girlfriend, Kanade sees the breakup and quietly offers her a handkerchief (ANN, vol. 1). Surprised by the kindness, Nanaki pushes her way into a friendship and gives Kanade a makeover. The new Kanade turns heads, Nanaki is jealous without knowing why, and Nanaki's old clique turns hostile; teachers are unfair to both (Okazu, vols. 1-2). Nanaki breaks with the clique and joins Kanade's welcoming circle of fan friends, Izumi and the transfer student Iroha, for the school trip; Izumi confesses her feelings to Nanaki (vols. 3-4; Okazu). A childhood friend of Kanade's reappears (vol. 5; PRH). In vol. 6 Nanaki confesses; Kanade, who feels she does not deserve to be loved, struggles before the two become a couple, and an epilogue shows them and their friends at university (Goodreads reader reviews; PRH).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a person worth when you take away her looks and her rank? Nanaki has built her life on being the cutest girl in the room; Kanade has given up trying to be seen at all. A cheating boyfriend shows Nanaki that her rank did not protect her, and a plain girl's handkerchief shows her a kind of worth she had never counted.

**The answer the story gives.** Worth is not earned by appearance and not granted by a crowd; it is seen and spoken by people who know you. ANN sums it up as the girls learning that they are worth something whatever others think. The makeover is the test case: it makes Kanade popular but not happy, and it makes Nanaki jealous of the person she built (vol. 2). What heals both is honest friendship, then love (vol. 6).

**Characters as arguments.** Nanaki argues first for image and then for loyalty: she gives up the clique to stand by Kanade (vol. 3). Kanade argues that some people simply live in different worlds, and the story spends six volumes proving her wrong. The clique argues for rank kept by mockery and loses. Izumi argues for honesty about one's feelings, confessing even though she knows the answer (vol. 4). Iroha, who sees Kanade as her savior (Okazu), shows how a little kindness can matter more than the giver knows.

**Symbols and motifs.** The handkerchief. The makeover (the Pygmalion and Galatea story, as Okazu frames it). Color-coded surnames, which Okazu notes (Kuro- "black," Ao- "blue," Aka- "red"). The "failed princess" of the title: girls who do not fit the fairy-tale role and are loved anyway.

**Traditions it echoes.** The school yuri romance (Okazu compares it to Morinaga Milk's GIRL FRIENDS) and the "opposites attract" romantic comedy (SLJ). For Christian readers the arc echoes the reversal of the proud and the lowly (Luke 1:52) and the warning against judging by appearance (1 Samuel 16:7); for Buddhist readers, freedom from craving for approval.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: being true to yourself and to whom you love is the happy ending. Consumerism is explored rather than condemned: fashion stays a joy for Nanaki, but it cannot give worth. Communitarianism appears in the fan circle that takes Nanaki in. Faith is absent.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 is the meeting and the makeover. Vols. 2-3 are the cost: jealousy, a hostile clique, unfair teachers and Nanaki's break with her old friends. Vol. 4 turns to Izumi's confession and Iroha. Vol. 5 adds Kanade's childhood friend as a complication. Vol. 6 brings Nanaki's confession, Kanade's struggle to believe she is worth loving, and the ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nanaki scorned Kanade, and Kanade helped her anyway. Why do you think Kanade did that?
