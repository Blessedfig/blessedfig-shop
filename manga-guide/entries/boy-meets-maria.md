**Premise.** Taiga Hirosawa starts high school wanting to become an actor and a hero like the ones on TV. At a dance exhibition he sees "Maria," known as the Madonna of the drama club, falls for her at first sight, confesses and is turned down (MangaUpdates; Publishers Weekly). Then he learns that Maria is Arima, a boy who performs female roles. Taiga's feelings do not change. Rehearsing a school play together, the two slowly reveal what they have hidden: Taiga's distant, strained relationship with his father, and Arima's recovery from a childhood sexual assault by a trusted teacher, which surfaces as flashbacks and panic attacks when Arima must perform as a boy (Publishers Weekly; SOLRAD). Classmates bully Taiga with homophobic insults and Arima with transphobic ones, including the claim that Arima deceived them. Asked directly about gender, Arima answers that he has not decided (SOLRAD); Publishers Weekly notes the shifting pronouns. A teacher who feels unqualified to help calls for more support, and the book ends hopeful but open (SOLRAD).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you love someone before you know who they are, and can they let you? Taiga falls for Maria the way heroes fall for heroines on TV. Arima is not the heroine he imagined, and is carrying something no one has seen.

**The answer the story gives.** Love that stays is what makes honesty possible. Taiga keeps loving the person once the costume is gone; Arima slowly lets the past be known. Healing is begun, not finished: SOLRAD notes that the trauma cannot be resolved within one school year, and a teacher has to admit she needs more help.

**Characters as arguments.** Taiga argues for simple, stubborn kindness and learns that a hero is someone who stays. Arima argues that roles can be a refuge after harm, and also a trap. The bullies argue that people must fit the categories they expect, and the story sides against them. The teacher who abused Arima stands for the worst betrayal of trust.

**Symbols and motifs.** The stage and the mirror (SOLRAD); the name Maria and the "Madonna of the drama club"; a bedroom drawn in colors that echo the trans flag (SOLRAD); panels that fall apart during panic.

**Traditions it echoes.** The romantic comedy of mistaken identity, Japanese school club stories, and boys' love. Its handling of trauma recalls survivor narratives more than BL fantasy.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressive acceptance are affirmed. Existential questions about roles and a real self are explored without a settled answer. Critical theory appears mainly in the critics' reading (SOLRAD), not as the book's own argument.

**Shifts across the series.** A single volume: comedy of first love in the early chapters, then rehearsals, disclosures and the stage crisis, then an open, hopeful ending. Exact chapter breaks were not available in sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Taiga wanted to be a hero. How did his idea of a hero change?
2. Why do you think Arima felt safer as Maria?
3. What should a student do if an adult they trust hurts them? Who could they tell?
4. How did the classmates' words hurt Arima and Taiga? What could a bystander have done?
5. The teacher said she was not the right person to help. Was that a good thing to admit?
