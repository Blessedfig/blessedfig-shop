**Premise.** Five childhood friends, Shintaro, Tsubaki, Akira, Ryu and Haruka (written "Haruko" in The Manga Critic's review; to verify against the Kodansha edition), killed a man the summer they were eleven and buried him on a mountain; every summer they dig up the body and renew their vow of silence (Kodansha). The man was Shintaro's father, who was abusing him; Akira proposed the killing and Tsubaki struck the final blow (Honcierge, vol. 2-5; TV Tropes). In their second year of high school the skeleton is gone and an anonymous caller blackmails them: they must dismember a stranger's body found in a bathroom and bury the pieces (vol. 1), undress for him (vol. 2), and dismember a headless body that turns out to be Akira's (vol. 2). The four survivors hide Akira's death, split over whether to confess, and hunt the blackmailer through a homeless woman, their old elementary school and its teachers (vol. 3-5). A former teacher is found murdered (vol. 6), and in vol. 7 Shintaro names the killer among the four. The truth: a former teacher who preyed on children knew their secret, and Tsubaki had carried the burden of keeping him silent (TV Tropes). The friends confess; three serve two years in juvenile detention and Tsubaki serves four and a half years of an 8-to-13-year sentence before parole for remorse (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A crime buried to protect someone does not stay buried: secrecy breeds more crimes and destroys the friendships it was meant to save, and only telling the truth and accepting the penalty lets the friends begin again.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Children defend one of their own against an abusive adult by killing; the group then guards itself like a band under threat. Rivalry and pairing among teens (a love triangle, coerced sex) strain the band from inside. |
| Pragmatic | Each cover-up creates a bigger problem: a stolen skeleton, a second body, a third. Shintaro finally sees that killing cannot fix what killing started (vol. 5, Honcierge). |
| Religious | No religion appears. The yearly vow at the grave is a private ritual. Faith readers see a story of sin hidden and found out, ending in confession and penalty, set beside sexual content involving teens that most traditions reject. |
| Socio-cultural | Adults fail every child in the story: an abusive father, a predatory teacher, police who come too late. The Japanese Honcierge writer frames it as erotic-grotesque horror and also as a story about how a harsh environment marks children. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Akira and two adults are dead and the friends lose years of their youth, but they finally confess, serve their sentences, and the epilogue hints at new beginnings (TV Tropes).
Hope source: R — the way through is redemption: confession, punishment and parole for remorse end the cycle of secrets (TV Tropes); the friends' loyalty (F) is the close second, though it is also what trapped them.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a wrong done to stop a greater wrong be kept secret forever? Five eleven-year-olds kill an abusive father to save their friend. Five years later, someone knows.

**The answer the story gives.** No. Every cover-up demands another crime, the friends turn on each other, one of them dies, and the truth comes out anyway. Shintaro's realization that people must not kill, whatever the hatred behind it (vol. 5), is the hinge. The ending, confession and sentences with parole for remorse, is the only way out (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Shintaro and Tsubaki argue for silence to protect each other; Ryu, Haruka and, before his death, Akira argue for confession. Tsubaki, who struck the final blow, carries the most and suffers the most. The adults who prey on children are the story's real villains, and its answer to why the children acted as they did.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bones that will not rot; the summer vow at the grave; the ringing phone; the scar on Haruka's shoulder that only a few people know about (vol. 2).

**Traditions it echoes.** *I Know What You Did Last Summer* (The Manga Critic suggests the author study it), Stephen King's *It* and *Stand by Me* (from general knowledge; to verify), and the Japanese ero-guro (erotic-grotesque) tradition that the Honcierge writer names.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Utilitarian reasoning (kill one to save one, hide to save many) is shown to fail; duty-based ethics wins in the end; group loyalty is both the friends' love and their prison.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1-2: the blackmail, two dismemberments, Akira's death. Vol. 3-4: hiding his death, the hunt, the love triangle. Vol. 5-6: the school, the teachers, a murdered teacher. Vol. 7: the reveal, confession and epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. What could the children have done at eleven instead of killing Shintaro's father? Who should have helped?
2. Why does each cover-up make things worse?
3. Ryu wanted to confess and Shintaro didn't. Who was right, and when?
4. Is it fair that Tsubaki got the longest sentence?
5. The story shows teen characters in sexual situations. Why might a publisher and a parent see that differently?
