**Premise.** Kazuya Kagami, a middle school student at Kamioka East Middle School, always carries the kimono sash (obi) his late mother left him. In ch. 1 the sash becomes Kiriha, a tsukumogami, who saves him from an amasogi, a spirit born from a person's twisted wish (fan wiki, Chapter 1; Wikipedia). Kazuya turns out to be a "Taboo Child" who draws curse toward himself, and he becomes the town's exorcist under Kukuri, the poor and humble land god of Hakusan Shrine, and her raven familiar Kokuyou, who move into his family's house (fan wiki). Arcs follow a swordswoman, Sunao Sumeragi, who must marry the boy who beat her in a duel to inherit her school (ch. 20-34); a revolt by the tsukumogami of Mayoiga, in which Kazuya spares the flute spirit Kyōka (ch. 49-71); the memories of his mother Kanaka, a powerful exorcist who was possessed and died, and Kiriha's destruction (ch. 72-85); Kazuya's imprisonment at Tsuzura Temple with other exorcists who treat tsukumogami as partners rather than tools, and their escape (ch. 86-113); an other-world arc (ch. 114-127); and later arcs about restoring a god and fighting curse (ch. 133 onward) (fan wiki arc pages; Wikipedia). Alongside this plot the series is a harem comedy, and its comedy and its spirit-power system both rely on sexual content involving Kazuya, who is a minor (see Content scores).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do we owe the things and beings that have shared our lives? A boy's dead mother left him a sash, and the sash turns out to have a soul. The series asks whether spirits, objects and partners are tools to be used or companions to be cherished.

**The answer the story gives.** Cherish them. Kazuya wins by treating his tsukumogami as partners: he spares the flute spirit Kyōka (ch. 49-71) and is jailed alongside others who refuse to treat spirits as tools, then frees them (ch. 86-113). The same story, however, treats people's bodies and consent as material for jokes, which undercuts its own message about respect.

**Characters as arguments.** Kazuya argues for responsibility: he blames himself for the curse he draws and pays for it with his own safety. Kiriha argues for fierce, possessive loyalty. Kukuri, a god who lives on donations and scrubs dishes, argues for humble service. Kanaka, a gifted exorcist overtaken by the very curse she fought, is the warning that power without care can be turned against you. The temple captors argue that spirits are tools, and lose.

**Symbols and motifs.** The cherry-blossom obi that still carries his mother's scent (Wikipedia); the "Taboo Child" who attracts curse; amasogi, spirits born of twisted wishes; the debt-ridden shrine; a mirror world that becomes a home (Mimane).

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese tsukumogami tales and the Hyakki Yagyō (night parade of a hundred demons) (from general knowledge; to verify); exorcist and shrine-maiden action manga; and the ecchi harem comedy, whose conventions ANN's reviewers called dated.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The pagan and occult world is affirmed as real and mostly benevolent. Hedonism is affirmed through the comedy and the power system. Communitarianism wins in the household of partners. Religious readers from Abrahamic faiths will find both the spirit service and the sexual content in direct conflict with their teaching; secular readers who enjoy the folklore will still face the sexualization of a minor.

**Shifts across the series.** Ch. 1-48: setup, the land god, Sunao, the counselling club and a gender-swap arc, with the most episodic comedy (anime season 1 covers ch. 1-30). Ch. 49-113: the series' serious core, from the Mayoiga revolt to Kanaka's past and the Tsuzura Temple imprisonment, where the sexual power technique is introduced. Ch. 114 onward: an other-world arc, a god's resurrection and the curse allies, still ongoing.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kiriha was once his mother's sash. Do you own anything that feels like it carries someone's memory?
2. Kazuya spares an enemy who fought to protect her friends. Why does mercy turn her into an ally?
3. The temple treats spirits as tools. What is the difference between using someone and working with them?
4. Reviewers said some of the jokes are mean. When does teasing stop being funny?
5. Why might a family decide a story with good themes is still not right for their home?
