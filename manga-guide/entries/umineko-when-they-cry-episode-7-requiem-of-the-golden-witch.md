**Premise.** In a world where the Golden Witch never existed, the witch Bernkastel summons Willard H. Wright (Will), a retired detective of the inquisitors' SSVD named after S. S. Van Dine, to answer one question: who killed Beatrice? His partner is Lion Ushiromiya, Krauss and Natsuhi's eldest child and heir, whose gender the story deliberately leaves unstated (Manga Bookshelf; Mizuno's vol. 3 afterword). Will questions the family at Beatrice's funeral: Rosa tells how, as a girl in 1967, she found a hidden mansion (Kuwadorian) and a young woman named Beatrice who fell from a cliff (ch. 1-3); patriarch Kinzo tells how, as a navy officer in 1944, he met Beatrice Castiglioni, an Italian interpreter who arrived on a submarine carrying ten tons of gold, and how a gunfight broke out on the island (ch. 4-8). Will learns that the Kuwadorian Beatrice was Kinzo's daughter by Castiglioni, that Kinzo fathered a child by her, and that Lion is that child, raised by Natsuhi in the one world where she did not throw the baby from a cliff (ch. 15-20; Wikipedia). The rest of the arc (ch. 20-39) is the culprit's own story, told through Clair Vaux Bernardus, a figure Bernkastel creates for the purpose: a servant girl nicknamed Yasu, bullied by other maids, who invents the witch Beatrice, falls in love, solves the epitaph's riddle and is presented to Kinzo as Beatrice (vols. 4-7; Yen Press and Square Enix blurbs). Will names the culprit, and Clair asks him to end her (ch. 37-39). In the closing "tea party" (ch. 40-47), Bernkastel stages a story with no game master for Lion and Ange: the gold is found, the adults turn guns on one another, Kyrie and Rudolf kill most of the family, Eva survives; Lion is nearly shot and is saved by Will, and Ange is devastated (Umineko fan wiki; TV Tropes; The Fandom Post).

## Life message

**A crime is not understood until you understand the heart behind it: the "magic" of the witch was a wounded person's way of surviving, and the truth should be sought with both reason and pity.**

| Pragmatic | The detective's method works: Will solves the earlier games by fair-play reasoning under Van Dine's rules (ANN, omnibus 1). But the story argues that method alone is not enough; the prologue has Will insist that motive, the heart, must be weighed beside who and how (Umineko fan wiki summary). |
| Religious | Witches, demons and magic are revealed as human invention: the witch Beatrice was made up by a lonely servant, and ANN reads the series as treating magic as a product of the human mind. Kinzo's gold and the 'Golden Land' play the role of a false paradise. Faith readers weigh occult imagery used as metaphor, and a patriarch's grave sin with only a pathetic remorse, against a strong call to judge with compassion. |
| Socio-cultural | A wealthy head family built on wartime gold and secrecy; servants treated as household 'furniture'; Kinzo's sexism (Manga Bookshelf) and a cover-up of his crimes by loyal servants (ch. 17-20). The WWII flashback shows a pointless gunfight between allies (TV Tropes recap). |

Outlook: Bittersweet, close to bleak — the culprit's story is understood and pitied and Lion is saved from death, but the arc ends with Bernkastel's grim staging of the massacre, a devastated Ange and the answer to how it all ends held over for Episode 8 (The Fandom Post; TV Tropes).

**Where traditions disagree.** Most traditions welcome the arc's demand to understand the heart before judging and its pity for a bullied, lonely child. The split is over its language and its darkness: Bible-centered churches, Jehovah's Witnesses, Adventists, Muslims and Orthodox Jews weigh the witches and magic, the incest backstory and the family bloodshed heavily (C to F), while secular, Atheist and Buddhist frames read the unmasking of magic as human invention very warmly. Progressive readers welcome the critique of an abusive patriarch but share Sean Gaffney's objection that some characters excuse him.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Reason joined with pity wins the argument: Will solves the case and lets the culprit tell her story, while the patriarch's power is exposed as the root of the harm; but in the tea party Bernkastel's cruel 'truth' wins the moment, and no one in the family escapes.

## Creator and publisher context

- Ryukishi07 (story), pen name of the lead member of 07th Expansion, born November 19, 1973; creator of the When They Cry visual novels (Higurashi, Umineko, Ciconia) and writer of Silent Hill f (Wikipedia, Ryukishi07).
- Eita Mizuno (art), Japanese manga artist best known for Spiral: The Bonds of Reasoning (story by Kyo Shirodaira); later drew the Otherside Picnic manga (Wikipedia, Eita Mizuno). Gaffney notes the artist was told to keep Lion's gender hidden (Manga Bookshelf).
- On-record intent (afterwords, 07th Expansion fan wiki): in vol. 1 Ryukishi07 recalls the strain of checking for logic errors and deciding how much to reveal, and set three conditions for the artist: "Will had to be cool! Lion had to be androgynous!" In vol. 3 Mizuno says the volume touches Lion's gender but that some things you don't need to know. In vol. 9 Ryukishi07 says the arc gave many answers while holding some back to see readers' theories, and calls Episode 7 one of the hardest to show in pictures.
- On the culprit's body and gender (Umineko fan wiki, citing Ryukishi07's answers in 'Answer to the Golden Witch' and a later interview): he said the phrase 'a body that cannot love' points to some imperfection on a sexual level that he obscured as a very adult topic, and when asked whether the character is transgender he preferred to leave it to readers. ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who killed Beatrice, and who was she? After six arcs in which Battler fought a witch over whether magic was real, this arc turns the question around: if the witch was invented, who invented her, and why? Will's first argument, in the prologue, is that a mystery solved without the heart is only half solved.

**The answer the story gives.** The witch was made by a hurt person. Behind her stands a chain of harm: a patriarch who could not let go of a dead lover, a daughter he wronged, a baby thrown from a cliff in most worlds, a child servant bullied and lonely who made up a witch to have power and company, and a promise forgotten by the boy she loved (ch. 17-31). The story asks readers to understand her without excusing the killings, and gives the last word in this arc to Bernkastel's cruel view that nothing could have changed.

**Characters as arguments.** Will is the case for reason joined with pity. Lion is the case for what love and acceptance can make of the same child; Bern says the chance of that world is one in millions (Umineko fan wiki). Kinzo is the case against unchecked power and obsession, and his groveling before 'Beatrice' (ch. 35) is meant to be pathetic. Natsuhi in Lion's world shows that a choice to love changes everything. Bernkastel is truth used as a weapon. Genji shows how loyalty can protect a sin.

**Symbols and motifs.** The portrait and the epitaph; the gold; the cliff (Beatrice's fall, the baby's fall); the hidden mansion Kuwadorian; the funeral and coffin of a witch; the theater seats of the tea party. Names carry meaning: Beatrice (Dante's guide), Will (after S. S. Van Dine), and the Eva, Ange, Lion pun Gaffney notes.

**Traditions it echoes.** The golden-age detective story and Van Dine's twenty rules; Dante's Beatrice (ANN notes the name's link and pronunciation); the gothic family saga with a hidden child. For some Christian readers, the Lord's warning about the sins of fathers falling on children and the hope of breaking that chain; for Buddhists, craving as the root of suffering.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism wins the argument: no magic survives Will's questions. Postmodernism shapes the form: stories within stories, a narrator invented for the purpose. Pagan and occult imagery is kept as metaphor. Nihilism is the villain's view and is not yet answered. Traditional authority is shown at its worst in Kinzo.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-20): the funeral, Kinzo's wartime story and the secret of Lion's birth. Volumes 4-7 (ch. 21-39): the culprit's story, from bullied child to witch, to the solved epitaph and the unmasking. Volumes 8-9 (ch. 40-47): the tea party, a staged massacre with no game master, and Ange's despair.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Will says the reason someone did something matters as much as how they did it. Do you agree? When?
2. Lion and Yasu are, in a sense, the same child raised in two different ways. What made the difference?
3. Some servants keep Kinzo's secret out of loyalty. When does loyalty become wrong?
4. Yasu made up a witch to cope with loneliness and bullying. What do people do today to cope, and what can help?
5. Bernkastel says nothing could have changed. What would you say back to her?
