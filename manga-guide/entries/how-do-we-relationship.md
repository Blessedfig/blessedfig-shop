**Premise.** Miwa Inuzuka, a shy young woman from a small town, starts college in Tokyo and joins the light music (band) club, where she befriends the outgoing guitarist Saeko Sawatari. Over drinks the two come out to each other as lesbians, and Saeko suggests they date, since neither knows other options; Miwa agrees, curious what a relationship is like (Wikipedia; ANN; VIZ vol. 1). The series follows them through their college years: a happy sexual relationship that sours over mismatched needs and poor communication (vols. 2-4), a breakup, Miwa's confession to her high school crush Shiho (vol. 5), a friends-with-benefits stretch (vols. 6-7), new girlfriends (Tamaki for Miwa, the hairdresser Yuria for Saeko, vols. 7-12), everyday homophobia from friends and family (vols. 8-9), depression, a coercive sexual encounter (vol. 11), more breakups (vol. 12), and a renewed closeness as graduation and job hunting arrive (vols. 13-14) (VIZ synopses; Okazu; The Outer Haven). Both leads are about eighteen at the start (The Beat) and twenty-one by vol. 12 (Okazu).

## Life message

**Love is not a fairy tale but a practice: two people have to learn honest communication, respect for each other's needs and consent, and the courage to be who they are, even when that means letting a relationship go.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does a relationship actually take? Most romance stories end when the couple gets together; this one starts there (Dickinson blog; Aftermath). Miwa and Saeko agree to date almost as an experiment, and the series asks what turns an arrangement into love, and what it means when love is not enough.

**The answer the story gives.** Honesty, communication, and respect for the other person's needs, including in sex. The first relationship fails because Saeko hides behind a facade and Miwa cannot say what she wants (Okazu vol. 4). Later relationships fail for different mismatches (vols. 10-12). The series counts it as growth when Miwa ends a harmful relationship instead of clinging (Okazu vol. 12). Love, in this story, is something people learn.

**Characters as arguments.** Miwa argues for romantic hope, and also shows how self-blame can make a person accept too much. Saeko argues for bravado as protection and learns to set it down. Tamaki shows that bluntness can be honest or cruel; her coercion of Miwa (vol. 11) is the story's clearest picture of what love must not be. Yuria shows that needing space is not rejection. Tamaki's friends and Saeko's mother show that prejudice often comes from people who mean well.

**Symbols and motifs.** The band: making music together as a picture of partnership, with Saeko writing the songs. The playground where couples talk and part (Okazu vol. 12). Cooking fried rice together, a dish they could never repeat (Okazu vol. 9). Hair: Yuria the hairdresser restyling Saeko.

**Traditions it echoes.** The josei relationship drama and modern yuri; Okazu compares a difficult scene to one in Yuri Is My Job! Its insistence on communication and consent echoes modern relationship advice more than any religious or philosophical school.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed throughout. Hedonism is explored and shown to cost something. Pragmatism, the work of compromise, is the practical wisdom the story offers. Traditional expectations appear as pressure, drawn with sympathy for the people who hold them.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4: the first relationship, from joyful start to breakup. Volumes 5-7: Shiho, depression, friends with benefits. Volumes 7-12: new girlfriends, homophobia, depression and a traumatic encounter, ending in two breakups. Volumes 13-14: friendship renewed, graduation, and a turn back toward each other.

1. Miwa and Saeko start dating because it is convenient. Can a relationship that starts that way become real love?
2. What does the story show about communication when people want different things?
3. Why do you think Miwa blames herself when relationships fail? Is she right?
4. How do Tamaki's friends hurt her even while saying they support her?
5. What does it mean to respect someone's "no," and where does the story show that well or badly?
