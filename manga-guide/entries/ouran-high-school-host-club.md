**Premise.** Haruhi Fujioka, a scholarship student at the ultra-rich Ouran Academy in Tokyo, wanders into the Third Music Room looking for a quiet place to study. It is the home of the Host Club: six wealthy boys (the theatrical president Tamaki Suoh, the calculating Kyoya Ootori, the twins Hikaru and Kaoru Hitachiin, the sweet-loving Hunny and the silent Mori) who entertain female classmates with tea, compliments and costume parties. Haruhi breaks a vase worth 8 million yen, and because her short hair and plain clothes make them think she is a boy, she is made to work off the debt as a host. She turns out to be a natural (v1). The comedy parodies shojo romance and boys'-love tropes while Haruhi's "commoner" eyes deflate the boys' wealth. Over 18 volumes it becomes a story about each member's family: Haruhi's crossdressing father Ranka, the twins' isolation, Kyoya's pressure to please his father, and Tamaki, the illegitimate son of the school's chairman, kept from his French mother and rejected by his grandmother. In the final arc Tamaki quits the club to win his grandmother over, the club reunites him with his mother, Haruhi confesses her love, and she leaves to study in the United States with Tamaki and, it turns out, the whole club following (ch. 72-83) (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Creator and publisher context

- Bisco Hatori (story and art), born 1975 in Saitama; debuted in LaLa DX; won an Outstanding Debut prize at the 26th Hakusensha Newcomers' Awards for Millennium Snow; later wrote Behind the Scenes!! (2014-2018) (Wikipedia, Bisco Hatori). She names Please Save My Earth and Slam Dunk as influences.
- On-record intent (Anime Expo 2019, via Wikipedia): Ouran was always meant as a parody of yaoi (boys' love) manga; Haruhi was first a boy, changed at an editor's suggestion, and was written as a girl who wears what she likes without subtext; Hatori is not concerned with genre or gender categories because every person should be proud of being unique while staying true to themselves; she stressed it is a story about family and friendship and did not set out to found the "fujoshi comedy" genre.
- No statements on religion were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Hakusensha (LaLa, a shojo magazine). VIZ Media published it under Shojo Beat, rated Teen.
- No controversies involving the creator were found; criticism concerns translation terms and stereotypes in the work (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who are you when you stop performing? Every host plays a type: the prince, the cool type, the mischievous twins, the cute boy, the strong silent one. Haruhi refuses to play anything; she is simply herself, and she does not mind whether people think she is a boy. The series asks what is left when the act ends.

**The answer the story gives.** Honest people heal families. The comedy of the early volumes, in which the hosts sell a fantasy, gives way to stories in which each member's act turns out to hide a family wound. Haruhi's plain honesty, and finally Tamaki's, mend them: Tamaki's grandmother accepts him, his mother is found, and his father and grandmother agree to speak honestly with each other (ch. 80-81). Hatori calls it a story about family and friendship (via Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Haruhi argues that worth does not depend on money or labels. Tamaki argues for openhearted idealism, and learns that hiding his feelings to protect others can hurt them. Kyoya argues for calculation, and is shown to care more than he admits. The twins argue for a closed world of two, and learn to open it. Ranka argues that a loving parent need not fit a mold. The grandmother argues for the family name, and changes.

**Symbols and motifs.** The broken vase and the debt. The Third Music Room as a stage. Haruhi's plain clothes versus the hosts' costumes. Instant coffee as a "commoner" treasure. The airport where Tamaki is reunited with his mother and Haruhi confesses (ch. 80-81).

**Traditions it echoes.** Shojo manga's long history of crossdressing heroines, from The Rose of Versailles, and of boys'-love tropes from The Heart of Thomas (Darlington, via Wikipedia); the comedy of manners; and, for many religious readers, the reversal in which the poor girl is wiser than the rich.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and found family are affirmed. Postmodern parody is the series' voice. Consumerism is mocked. Progressive readings of gender find much to welcome, though critics note dated slurs and a lesbian stereotype. Tradition is critiqued and then reconciled: the old family is not overthrown but made more honest.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 (ch. 1-27) are mostly episodic comedy and parody. Volumes 7-13 (ch. 28-61) deepen each member's backstory (Kyoya and Tamaki's first meeting, the twins, Hunny and his brother). Volumes 14-18 (ch. 62-83) turn to romance and Tamaki's family, ending with the club following Haruhi abroad.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Each host plays a character for the customers. What role do you sometimes play, and why?
2. Haruhi does not care if people think she is a boy. Why does that bother the others more than her?
3. Tamaki quit the club to win his grandmother over. Was that right? What did Haruhi want him to do instead?
4. Which jokes in the series would you tell differently today?
5. What can money not buy in this story?
