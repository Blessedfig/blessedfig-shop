**Premise.** Edo-period Japan. Sōjirō Yui is the second son of a samurai house, so he will not inherit; he is a renowned swordsman of the Ittō-ryū school, promoted by his lord, who teaches children at the family kendo dojo, and he is kind, principled and sure he is not good enough. A month ago he married Chiyo in a match arranged to repay a debt her family owed his (Mangapedia). Chiyo is beautiful, capable and trained as an assistant instructor in martial arts, and she smiles politely at him every day. Sōjirō reads the smile as dutiful unhappiness and resolves to become a husband worthy of her. In fact Chiyo fell for him at first sight at the wedding and is fighting to keep her delighted grins and her very un-ladylike desire for him from showing (TOKYOPOP; Mangapedia). Each short chapter is a misunderstanding: jealousy, family visits, his stern elder brother Heizaburō, her younger sister Wakayo, rivals and ruffians whom one or both of them beat. Late in the web run Chiyo finally tells him how she feels, and the marriage, still unconsummated for most of the story (MangaUpdates; pixiv tags), becomes a real one before the final chapter (pixiv, October to December 2022, January 2023).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a marriage you did not choose become the love of your life? Sōjirō and Chiyo already have the answer, they just cannot see it: each is sure the other is merely doing their duty.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if both keep trying to deserve each other and finally say so. The joke of every chapter is that their love is complete except for words. When Chiyo at last tells him how she feels (pixiv, October 2022) and goes to him at night (December 2022), the marriage they already had becomes the one they wanted.

**Characters as arguments.** Sōjirō argues for humility, perhaps too much of it: a good man who thinks he must earn what he has already been given. Chiyo argues for propriety holding back passion, and the story both laughs at and admires her restraint. Heizaburō, the stern elder brother, turns out to be a doting husband too, suggesting that the samurai's gruff face hides the same heart.

**Symbols and motifs.** The fake smile, which is not fake at all but a lid on a bigger smile; the sword, used only to protect; the dojo full of children; mochi and New Year games, the rhythm of a household year.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese romantic comedy of mutual misunderstanding (both love, neither says); Edo-period household drama; for Jewish and Christian readers, Isaac and Rebecca, whose arranged marriage became love (Genesis 24:67), and Proverbs on delighting in one's spouse.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Traditionalism and Confucian propriety are affirmed and gently teased. The honor code is the background of a gentle swordsman. Expressive individualism appears only as the need to speak one's heart inside a marriage.

**Shifts across the series.** The early chapters are one-joke misunderstandings; the middle adds family, rivals and jealousy; the last chapters bring Chiyo's confession and the consummation of the marriage (pixiv dates; Kodansha volume placement not confirmed).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Sōjirō think Chiyo is unhappy? What does he miss?
2. Chiyo hides her feelings for the sake of propriety. When is holding back good, and when does it hurt?
3. Their marriage was arranged to repay a debt. Does that matter by the end? Why or why not?
4. What does each of them do to try to deserve the other?
5. What finally changes between them, and what made it possible?
