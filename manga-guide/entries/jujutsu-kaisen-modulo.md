**Premise.** In 2086, 68 years after the Culling Game, the spaceship Naunax arrives over Nevada carrying about 50,000 Simurians, three-eyed aliens with their own cursed energy, seeking a new home; the United States hands the talks to Japan, home of jujutsu sorcerers (ch. 1). The Simurian envoy Marulu (Maru) is paired with Tsurugi Okkotsu (17) and Yuka Okkotsu (16), grandchildren of Yuta Okkotsu and Maki Zen'in, on missions: rogue curse users abducting children as cursed-energy 'batteries' (ch. 1), a forest-swimming shark (ch. 3), and an elderly curse user who has posed as the same first-grader for years and killed families (ch. 4-6). Maru's twin Cross secretly hopes for war, and Yuka learns she has a terminal brain tumor (ch. 7). Flashbacks show the twins' tribe, the Rumelians, driven from their land, and their mentor Dura killed in a duel by the champion Dabura, forced to fight because his sister is a hostage (ch. 8-9). The Simurian council votes for coexistence (ch. 10), but when sorcerers exorcising curses in Tokyo shoot Cross, the Simurians, who see curses as their sacred Kalyans, demand Tokyo and a duel of champions (ch. 12-14). Yuka summons Mahoraga in a ritual that will cost her life to fight Dabura, while Tsurugi and Maru fight their own duel (ch. 15-21). Tsurugi lays down his sword rather than win (ch. 21); Maru, helped by the ageless Yuji Itadori, performs a Ritual of Harmony that changes cursed energy itself so curses stop forming, heals Yuka, Tsurugi and Cross, and lets humans and Simurians live as neighbors (ch. 22-25) (fan wiki; Wikipedia; Beebom).

## Life message

**Living as neighbors with people who are strange to you takes patience, listening and sacrifice; peace is made by people willing to lose rather than win the wrong way, and the old cycle of fear and revenge can be ended.**

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame welcomes the story's core: strangers made into neighbors, trafficking condemned, sacrifice honored and a refusal to win the wrong way. The divide is the setting. Bible-centered frames that treat sorcery and spirits as spiritually real (Pentecostal, Jehovah's Witness, many Baptist and Adventist families) weigh a world built on curses, shikigami and a soul-altering ritual as an endorsed occult framework (C or D), while mainline, Reform Jewish, secular, Atheist, Agnostic and Nothing in particular frames read it as fantasy and grade the refugee message highly (A). Peace churches note the sacrifice and Tsurugi's forfeit but also that duels and one revenge killing settle the plot. Conservative readers may ask whether a story about newcomers demanding territory ends too easily.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who reach across the divide (Maru, Tsurugi, Yuka, Usami, Cross after his change of heart) are healed and rewarded; those who exploit others (traffickers, Masaki, the Deskunte chief) are defeated or killed, and the fearful shooter Yakumaru is maimed and apparently left unhealed.

## Creator and publisher context

- Gege Akutami (story) created Jujutsu Kaisen (Weekly Shonen Jump, 2018-2024). Wikipedia says Akutami was at first hesitant about spin-offs, fearing they would not match the original, and set Modulo in a parallel line of the future to expand the world without compromising its themes.
- On-record intent: at Jump Festa 2026 Akutami said the idea began as 'sorcerers vs. aliens' and that the team 'faced modern challenges' within the limit of a short serialization (fan translation reported by ComicBook.com). ## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can two peoples who fear each other become neighbors, and what has to be given up for that to happen? The Simurians arrive as refugees with powers that terrify their hosts; the humans kill beings the newcomers hold sacred without knowing it. Every chapter asks what it takes to live next to someone you do not understand.

**The answer the story gives.** Peace needs people who will lose for it. Dura dies asking his enemies to treat his tribe as neighbors (ch. 9); Usami begs Dabura to take his life instead of his subordinates' (ch. 13); Yuka offers her life (ch. 15); Tsurugi drops his sword at the decisive moment because a future where he won 'wasn't right' (ch. 21). The final peace comes from Maru's ritual, which changes cursed energy itself (ch. 23-24), a supernatural answer that ComicBook.com finds too easy but that the story frames as healing rather than extermination.

**Characters as arguments.** Maru argues for openness; Cross for the resentment of a people who were wronged, until he votes for peace (ch. 10). Osuki argues for taking what was taken from them (ch. 10). Yakumaru shows fear acting first (ch. 13). Dabura is strength without freedom, bound by a hostage, who finds a warrior's purpose in protecting others (ch. 18-19) and then frees his people by killing their tyrant (ch. 24). Yuji, an ageless survivor of the old story, argues that ignoring the present also loses the past and future (ch. 25).

**Symbols and motifs.** Neighbors, the word that stops Maru's rampage (ch. 6). The canal joining two tribes (ch. 8). A father's ordinary rock, treasured as if precious (ch. 8, 14). The third eye that can weep to death. Yuta's ring, carrying a dead girl's will. Curses rising as light, resting rather than destroyed (ch. 24). A game of catch (ch. 25).

**Traditions it echoes.** Refugee and first-contact stories; the biblical command to love the stranger and the parable of the Good Samaritan, for Christian and Jewish readers; Buddhist teaching that hatred breeds more hatred, which Jujutsu Kaisen's curses (born of negative emotion) already dramatize; the shonen tournament duel, here turned toward a forfeit.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism wins: the tribes and species become one community. Pragmatism earns respect but cannot finish the job. The warrior code is examined: it binds Dabura, wounds Maru, and is finally redefined as protection. Pacifism is honored in key choices but not as the whole rule. Might makes right is defeated. The occult system is the setting's physics and is ended by the plot itself.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-7) is case-of-the-week investigations with a light buddy tone, ending on Yuka's diagnosis. Volume 2 (ch. 8-16) gives the Rumelians' history, the vote, the shooting and the start of the duels. Volume 3 (ch. 17-25) is the battle, Tsurugi's forfeit, the ritual and the epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The Simurians arrived needing a home. What did the humans fear, and which fears turned out to be fair?
2. Yakumaru shot Cross because he thought Cross was drawing a sword. How do fear and quick judgments start conflicts?
3. Tsurugi gave up a win he could have had. Why did he think winning 'wasn't right'?
4. Dura wanted the tribes to be neighbors, and Dabura took revenge on the chief. Do both count as justice?
5. The ending solves the conflict with a ritual. What would real people need to do instead?
