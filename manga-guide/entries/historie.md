**Premise.** Fourth century BC. Eumenes, a clever young man sitting by a boat near the ruins of Troy, helps the philosopher Aristotle escape Persian pursuers (ch. 1). The story then goes back to his boyhood in the Greek city of Cardia, where he is the gifted son of the leading citizen Hieronymus, until a violent slave escape by the Scythian Thrax (ch. 12-15) leaves Hieronymus dead and reveals that Eumenes was born a Scythian, taken as a baby in a slave raid in which his mother was killed. He is sold into slavery; a mutiny and shipwreck leave him in Paphlagonia, where he lives in the village of Boa, loves the villager Satura, and leads the village's defense against the Greek city of Tios (ch. 24-36), then gives Satura up to protect the village. Returning to besieged Cardia, he meets King Philip II of Macedon, who had visited in disguise, and becomes his secretary (ch. 39-44). In Macedon he serves Philip in war (the Scythian campaign, the Triballi ambush, Chaeronea), meets the young Alexander, who has a second personality called Hephaestion, and his mother Olympias, and watches the court plots that end in Pausanias's assassination of Philip (ch. 45-101) (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Wit, learning and adaptability let an outsider survive and rise in a cruel world, but who a person is cannot be fixed by birth, nation or master, and freedom is worth more than position.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A Darwinian world of raids, slavery, war and dynastic marriage: Philip takes many wives for alliances (Eurydice is his seventh, fan wiki), and succession drives the court plots. Iwaaki, the author of Parasyte, shows humans as animals capable of great cruelty and great intelligence. |
| Pragmatic | Eumenes survives by thinking: a boat mechanism (ch. 1), a defense of Boa using a hidden dike (ch. 31-34), a chess variant he invents (fan wiki), tactics that save Philip's army from the Triballi. He lies when lying protects people (fan wiki). The story rewards competence over birth. |
| Religious | Greek gods and rituals are the cultural background; Eumenes's mother's amulet bears a sign like Ahura Mazda's (ch. 1); Olympias pursues hidden aims and takes Pausanias's heart, presumably for ritual use (fan wiki). Aristotle's empirical study (the Lesbos biological institute, ch. 37-38) is portrayed admiringly. Faith readers will meet a pre-Christian world drawn without endorsement of its cults. |
| Socio-cultural | Greek versus "barbarian" is the central tension: Eumenes is a Scythian raised Greek, enslaved when his origin is revealed, and later meets Macedonian prejudice against non-Macedonians (Attalus, fan wiki). Slavery is shown from inside; Charon, a freed slave, tells Eumenes to "live free" (ch. 86, paraphrased). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Eumenes keeps winning his freedom and his place by his wits, but he loses families, a love and a home along the way, and the court around him is murderous; history (and the coming anime) point to wars after Alexander that Eumenes did not survive (from general knowledge).
Hope source: K — the way through is knowledge and inquiry: Eumenes's learning, Aristotle's curiosity and the library of his childhood carry him through slavery, shipwreck and war; being free and true to himself (S) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame welcomes the story's honest picture of slavery and prejudice and its love of learning. The split is over how much a dark, pre-Christian world without moral resolution suits a family: most Christian, Jewish and Muslim frames grade the message B (the story condemns cruelty but rewards a hero who deceives, and offers no hope beyond this life), with Pentecostal, Anabaptist and Jehovah's Witness readers lower because war and pagan rites are the substance. Secular, Atheist and Satanist frames read its reason-centered realism warmly. Almost every frame marks the gore and sexual coercion as a significant depiction concern.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hitoshi Iwaaki, born 1960 in Tokyo, began drawing under the influence of Osamu Tezuka, assisted Kazuo Kamimura, and has worked mainly for Kodansha's Afternoon; his Parasyte (1989-1994) won the Kodansha Manga Award (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent: Historie is a story he had wanted to tell since before his professional debut (Kodansha K Manga blurb via AniList). On the 2022 hiatus he said he had to think about how to make sure the whole story reaches its conclusion (Wikipedia, paraphrased).
- The Mainichi Shimbun called his invention of Eumenes's largely unknown youth bold and unique (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Kodansha (Monthly Afternoon, a seinen magazine); English digital on K Manga, print by Kodansha USA from 2027. No age rating found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a person who they are: birth, upbringing, or choice? Eumenes believes he is a Greek of Cardia until a slave revolt reveals he was born a Scythian, a "barbarian," and he is sold as a slave by the people who raised him. Historie follows how a mind survives that.

**The answer the story gives.** Neither birth nor status decides. Eumenes is "unconstrained by any one value system" (fan wiki) and rebuilds himself in a Paphlagonian village and at the Macedonian court by intelligence and adaptability. Freedom is the deepest good: Charon, his old slave-tutor, blesses him as a free man, his only son (ch. 86). But the story also says that the world rewards cleverness and power more than goodness.

**Characters as arguments.** Eumenes argues for wit and openness. Aristotle argues for observation and questions. Thrax argues the enslaved's case with terrible violence and spares the boy who offered him a hand. Philip argues for pragmatic power. Olympias argues for hidden, ritual power that reshapes her son into two people. Pausanias, emotionless and used by others, finds a purpose only in killing a king. Satura and Boa show a peaceful life Eumenes chooses to give up.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bronze snake on the beach (ch. 1) and Alexander's snake-shaped scar. His mother's amulet with an Ahura Mazda-like sign. The recurring dream of a woman fighting soldiers. The library of his childhood. The heart: Pausanias's seat of the soul debate (ch. 88) and the heart Olympias takes (ch. 101).

**Traditions it echoes.** Herodotus (whose word historie means "inquiry"), Plutarch's lives of Eumenes and Alexander, Homer's Odyssey (Eumenes calls his journey an odyssey; ch. 36 is "Odysseus"), Tezuka's historical epics and Iwaaki's own Parasyte in its cool look at human cruelty.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism is the hero's method and largely vindicated. Might makes right describes the world but is not praised. Classical virtue and Aristotle's reason are admired. Nationalism and ethnic prejudice are examined from an outsider's view and found wanting. Religion appears as culture and as a tool of power, never as an answer.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 opens with Aristotle at Troy. Vols. 1-3 are Cardia: childhood, Thrax's revolt, slavery. Vols. 3-5 are Paphlagonia and Boa. Vols. 5-6 return to Cardia and Philip. Vols. 6-12 and later chapters are Macedon: the court, Alexander and Hephaestion, wars, Olympias's exile and Philip's assassination (ch. 101).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Eumenes is sold as a slave by the people who raised him when they learn where he was born. Why?
2. Thrax kills many people, but spares Eumenes. How should we judge him?
3. Eumenes lies to Satura to protect her village. Was that right?
4. What does Aristotle see in Eumenes, and what does curiosity have to do with freedom?
5. Charon tells Eumenes to live free. What does freedom mean in this story?
