**Premise.** Two years after the "Fifth Moon" incident, the traveling priest Nicholas D. Wolfwood finds Vash the Stampede living quietly as "Ericks" with a 12-year-old girl, Lina, and her grandmother, and draws him back out to stop his twin brother Knives (ch. 1-2). The two gunmen travel together while the surviving Gung-Ho Guns, now ordered by Legato Bluesummers to make Vash's life miserable rather than kill him, attack one by one: Rai-Dei the Blade, whom Wolfwood kills after Vash has won (ch. 8-10); Leonof the Puppet-Master and Gray the Ninelives, who kill 170 of the 480 people on the floating ship Vash calls home (ch. 12-19); and Midvalley, Zazie and Hoppered, a survivor of the destroyed city of July (ch. 22-33). Flashbacks reveal that Vash and Knives are "independent Plants," the living power sources humans have used for 150 years, and that Knives's hatred began when the twins found what researchers had done to another Plant, Tesla (ch. 39-42). Wolfwood turns out to be an agent of the Eye of Michael, a Plant-worshipping order of assassins serving Knives; he betrays Knives to save Vash and dies after a last stand at his childhood orphanage (ch. 49-65). Knives launches the Ark, absorbing the planet's Plants and pushing humanity toward extinction (ch. 46-47), as a ship from Earth arrives (ch. 66-72). Vash kills Legato to save Wolfwood's friend Livio (ch. 90-91), frees the absorbed Plants and saves Knives from Earth's weapons; Knives, dying, grows a fruit tree to feed his weakened brother, and Vash continues his travels with Meryl and Milly (ch. 92-97; Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Every life, even an enemy's, is worth saving, and love can still win after failure and loss; but the story admits that holding to that ideal in a violent world has a real and painful cost.**

| Pragmatic | Wolfwood is the pragmatist: killing one now saves many later, and he proves it repeatedly (Rai-Dei, ch. 10). Vash answers that you cannot know someone would not have stopped. The ending gives each a point: Vash must kill Legato to save Livio (ch. 91), yet his refusal to kill Knives saves the planet. |
| Religious | A priest who kills (Wolfwood), an assassin order founded by a church that worships Plants (Eye of Michael), a servant who treats his master as a god and quotes scripture (Legato), Knives's "ark" read as a manufactured flood, and a fruit tree at the end (Women Write About Comics). Vash sits and prays in a church (ch. 38). Nightow has said he follows no religion (Beneath the Tangles, cited in this site's Trigun entry). |
| Socio-cultural | A space western grown into a seinen war story: refugees, militias, bounty hunters, a colonizing Earth fleet and an exploited energy class (the Plants). Readers compare Vash's pacifism with Rurouni Kenshin and Batman (Thompson, via Wikipedia), and a scholar compares the Angel Arm to the atomic bomb (Aponte, via Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — humanity survives, the Plants are freed and the brothers reconcile, but Wolfwood dies (ch. 64), Vash kills for the first time (ch. 91), Knives dies (ch. 96-97) and many towns are lost along the way.
Hope source: R — the way through is forgiveness and redemption: Vash spares and saves Knives, who answers with a fruit tree to feed him; Wolfwood confesses and dies at peace (Beneath the Tangles reads both as grace received); protecting others (P) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every frame welcomes the ending's mercy and Vash's refusal to answer hatred with hatred, so grades are high. The divide is Vash's one killing (ch. 91): Jewish, Muslim, Sikh, Catholic just-war and conservative readers see it as a rightful defense of an innocent and a sign the story is morally realistic, while Anabaptist and some pacifist readers see the vow's breaking as a concession. Depiction concerns are heavier than in Trigun: graphic violence, body horror, a drug overdose and a flashback of sexual abuse of a teenager move most religious frames to Significant.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Mercy wins the war but not without loss: the hero's refusal to hate saves his brother and the planet, while those who lived by killing (Legato, the Eye of Michael) are destroyed, and the friend who argued for killing dies at peace with the hero.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yasuhiro Nightow, born 1967 in Yokohama, sold apartments for Sekisui House before drawing full-time; Trigun was his first serial (Wikipedia; this site's Trigun entry).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia): he made Vash a pacifist because he did not want his lead to be a murderer; he asked Young King OURs to let him finish Trigun rather than start a new work, and said Maximum is the same story. Vash's hair turning black, a running mystery, is revealed in the climax as a side effect of overusing his powers. The Angel Arms take a feminine form and can create matter.
- Faith: asked in 2009, Nightow said through an interpreter that he does not follow a religion (Beneath the Tangles, via this site's Trigun entry); Women Write About Comics found no evidence for rumors that he is Catholic.
- Publisher: Shōnen Gahōsha (Young King OURs, a seinen magazine). Dark Horse publishes it in English and rates the Deluxe Edition 16+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's statements about Maximum's ending.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person keep the rule never to kill when keeping it costs lives, and what happens to him if he breaks it? Trigun Maximum takes the pacifist hero of Trigun and puts him beside the one man who can argue back: Wolfwood, a priest trained as an assassin, who believes someone always has to die.

**The answer the story gives.** Mercy is right, and it is not free. Vash spares every assassin, and many people die in the attacks that follow (ch. 19). Wolfwood kills to protect him and his orphans and is often proved right (ch. 10, 31). When Legato holds Livio hostage and demands to be killed, Vash shoots (ch. 91), and the story treats it as a wound. Yet the war ends because Vash will not kill his brother: he frees the absorbed Plants, saves Knives from Earth's weapons, and Knives's last act is to feed him with a fruit tree (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Vash argues for mercy and the worth of every life. Wolfwood argues that love sometimes requires a gun, and dies with a confession on his lips (Beneath the Tangles). Knives argues that humans, who tortured Tesla and live off enslaved Plants, deserve to end (ch. 39-46). Legato, abused as a slave and saved by Knives's massacre, argues that power is worship and death is release (ch. 79-91). Livio, whose abuse split his mind in two, shows that a killer can be freed (ch. 52-63). Rem, in flashback, is the source of Vash's creed.

**Symbols and motifs.** The cross Wolfwood carries, which is also a gun. The coin case filled with the halves carried by each Gung-Ho Gun. Vash's hair turning black as he spends his life. The Ark. The fruit tree. Red geraniums, Rem's flower (ch. 5).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Western gunfighter who will not draw; Rurouni Kenshin's vow and Batman's rule (Thompson, via Wikipedia); for Christian readers the Sermon on the Mount, Noah's ark and the tree of knowledge (Women Write About Comics); for Buddhist readers the refusal to answer hatred with hatred; for Jewish readers the law of the pursuer in ch. 91.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pacifism is affirmed but not made easy; utilitarian killing is given its best case and partly conceded; nihilism and the rule of the strong are defeated; Christian imagery is serious and sympathetic without a sermon. Environmental and anti-exploitation themes grow stronger as the Plants' story is revealed.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-20): road story and the floating ship; vols. 4-5 (ch. 21-33): July's survivors and Legato's cruelty; vols. 6-7 (ch. 34-45): the twins' past; vols. 8-10 (ch. 46-65): the Ark and Wolfwood's last stand; vols. 11-14 (ch. 66-97): the Earth fleet, Legato and the brothers' end.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Wolfwood kills Rai-Dei after Vash has already won. Was he protecting Vash or doing something wrong?
2. Vash kills Legato to save Livio. Did he break his rule, or keep its deeper meaning?
3. Knives hates humans because of what was done to Tesla. Is his anger understandable even if his plan is evil?
4. Why does Vash save Knives at the end, and what does Knives's last act show?
5. Livio and Legato were both abused as children. Why does one find freedom and the other does not?
