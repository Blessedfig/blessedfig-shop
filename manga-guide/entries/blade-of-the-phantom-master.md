**Premise.** Jushin, a kingdom modeled on Joseon-era Korea, once had secret royal inspectors (amhaeng-eosa, "angyo onshi" in Japanese) who traveled in disguise to expose and punish corrupt officials, each carrying a medallion that lets a true believer in justice summon phantom soldiers. Jushin has fallen into fiefdoms ruled by tyrants. Munsu, a former general and the last high-ranking inspector, wanders with his bodyguard Sando (Chun Hyang), a fierce warrior he freed from a corrupt lord who had brainwashed her, and later with a hapless servant, Bang Ja. His episodic missions punish cruel lords, but he is really hunting Aji Tae, the demon-sorcerer who killed Jushin's king, Munsu's best friend Hae Mo Su, and took his form. Munsu is an anti-hero who lies, steals and kills without mercy, and he blames himself for the kingdom's fall and for the death of his lover, Kye Wol Hyang. In the end Munsu storms Aji Tae's castle with old comrades, dies in the battle, and Sando kills Aji Tae; Sando and Bang Ja go on fighting for the inspectors' ideals, and Munsu is shown reunited in death with his lover and friends (Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Creator and publisher context

- Youn In-wan (story) and Yang Kyung-il (art) are South Korean creators who made the series for a Japanese magazine while living in Incheon; Youn learned Japanese as the series went on (Wikipedia). Many chapters carry author's notes explaining Korean folktales and historical figures, and where Youn departed from accepted versions (Wikipedia).
- Influences: the tragic version of The Legend of Chun Hyang; the real inspector Park Mun-su; the heroine Non Gae (Ju Non-gae), who gave her life to kill an invading general; Natsuki Sumeragi's manga Richō Angyōki (Wikipedia). Youn found that roles like the secret inspector exist in many cultures, which showed him that fighting corruption is a universal theme (Wikipedia).
- On Hwanung (vol. 4), Youn worried his portrayal of the sage as a summoned creature in fetish gear might mislead readers, noting the figure's very high status in Korea (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Monthly Sunday Gene-X, seinen) and Daewon C.I. in Korea; in English only the Webtoon digital release. The volume 8 bonus booklet included an interview between Youn and the manga group Clamp (Wikipedia).
- No controversies involving the creators were found beyond some Korean readers' criticism of the dark retelling of Chun Hyang (Wikipedia). **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you fight a corrupt world with clean hands? Munsu is the last guardian of a justice system that no longer exists. To punish tyrants he lies, steals and kills, and the people who travel with him keep asking whether he is any better than the men he destroys.

**The answer the story gives.** Justice is worth fighting for, and loyalty to those you have lost can carry you through, but the methods leave scars. Munsu's guilt is the center of the story: he blames himself for the king's death and his lover's. The end gives him a soldier's death and a reunion with the dead, not a clean victory; his companions carry the ideals forward in better form.

**Characters as arguments.** Munsu argues for results. Bang Ja argues for the inspectors' ideals and grows from a coward into someone brave enough to keep them. Sando argues for conscience, and her brainwashing and recovery show how evil uses people. Hong Gildong argues for righteous crime and is told that crimes remain crimes. Aji Tae argues that power makes a god, and is destroyed. Kye Wol Hyang's suicide argues that love can sacrifice itself to break evil's hold.

**Symbols and motifs.** The inspector's medallion, which works only for those with a true conviction for justice (Wikipedia), worn by a man who seems to have none. Munsu's breathing charm, a cursed inheritance from the woman he loved. Phantom armies of the dead. The fallen kingdom as a ghost the living serve.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Korean amhaeng-eosa tales, the Robin Hood figure Hong Gildong, the Chun Hyang love story told as tragedy, and the dark-fantasy anti-hero (readers compare it to Berserk). Faith readers may hear Romans 12:19 on vengeance and John 15:13 on laying down one's life for friends.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Confucian loyalty and the ideal of the upright official are honored; might-makes-right is condemned in its villains; utilitarian means are questioned; the occult is the world's furniture. No religion is preached, but the afterlife reunion gives the ending its peace.
