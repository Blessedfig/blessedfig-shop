**Premise.** Twelve-year-old Mitsuki Koyama has a throat tumor (sarcoma); surgery could cost her voice, and her grandmother Fuzuki, who blames music for taking everyone she loved, forbids her to sing. Mitsuki wants to become a singer so that her first love, Eichi, who left the orphanage where they met for America, will hear her. Two shinigami (death spirits) who only she can see, Takuto and Meroko, tell her she has a year to live. Takuto agrees to let her turn into a healthy 16-year-old so she can audition, and she debuts as the singer "Full Moon" and leaves home to live with her manager. She learns that every shinigami was once a person who died by suicide; Takuto was a singer who tried to take his life after losing his voice to the same illness and has lain in a coma since. Mitsuki has known all along that Eichi died in a plane crash (ch. 12-13), and for a time she wants to die to join him until Takuto's regret turns her back. She chooses surgery and life; a shinigami official tries to take her soul, and Takuto, Meroko and Izumi save her in the underworld (v6-7). In the epilogue Mitsuki, now about 16 in her own body, sings again, Takuto wakes from his coma with his voice restored, they become a couple, and Eichi's soul, which had been watching over her, goes to heaven (ch. 30) (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; fan wiki).

## Life message

**Life is a gift worth choosing even in grief; love for the living and the dead can coexist, and dreams are pursued by deciding to live.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A story of survival against a hereditary illness that runs in the family (TV Tropes) and of pair-bonding: first love (Eichi), second love (Takuto), a love triangle with Meroko, and adult couples (Oshige and Wakaoji; Madoka and Nachi) paired off at the end. Kin care is strong: a grandmother's fear for the last of her family drives the conflict. |
| Pragmatic | Mitsuki's bargain with death is practical: one year, one dream. The turning point is a medical decision, surgery, made once she has reasons to live; the magic only buys time. Oshige's deal with the record-company president shows the cost of getting results by any means. |
| Religious | A Japanese folk-and-Buddhist-tinged afterlife: shinigami in a "pediatrics ward" of heaven, a Death Master who rules the underworld, souls that linger out of attachment and then ascend to heaven (TV Tropes; Wikipedia). No real religion is preached. Faith readers weigh the strong choose-life message and the regret attached to suicide against an invented afterlife in which suicides become reapers, and against the romance of a 12-year-old with an older youth. |
| Socio-cultural | A shojo idol story: the music industry, a rival who had plastic surgery to be pretty enough for fame, a manager who drinks and sleeps with her married boss to advance her singer's career, and a grandmother who controls a granddaughter out of grief. Families are healed by understanding, not punishment. |

Outlook: Hopeful — Mitsuki lives, keeps her voice, reconciles with her grandmother and reunites with Takuto; the one bittersweet note is Eichi's final farewell as his soul goes to heaven (TV Tropes calls it bittersweet but upbeat).
Hope source: L — love between two carries the story's way through: Takuto's love and the memory of Eichi's love give Mitsuki reasons to live, and the epilogue is a reunion and confession; family reconciliation (F) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Nearly every tradition welcomes the story's central choice: a grieving child chooses life, and every suicide is mourned. The divide is the afterlife: Adventist and Witness readings treat conscious spirits of the dead as a serious error (D), while Shinto, Pagan, Buddhist, Hindu and "Nothing in particular" readers find the departed soul who watches over and then lets go a gentle and familiar idea. Christian, Jewish and Muslim frames add a concern that the story's reaper system judges people who died by suicide, and many families across frames will want to discuss the romance between a 12-13-year-old and an older-looking youth, which Sevakis (ANN) also questioned.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Everyone who chooses to live, forgive or let go ends well: Mitsuki lives and sings, Fuzuki reconciles, Izumi and Meroko find peace, and Eichi ascends. Despair and suicide are shown as losses that the characters regret.

## Creator and publisher context

- Arina Tanemura (born 1978) is a shojo manga artist long associated with Ribon; her earlier series Time Stranger Kyoko was cut short, which she attributes to a heroine with too strong a personality for her editors and readers, so she gave Mitsuki a quieter personality and made her sickly to explain her shyness (Wikipedia).
- On-record intent (Wikipedia, citing Ribon and a 2012 panel report): she is a fan of idol singers and made the series because she wanted to write lyrics; Mitsuki is modeled on an assistant, with a hairstyle from singer Ai Kago; Oshige drinks because Tanemura's editors like to drink; Jonathan's design came from her sketch of Peeves from Harry Potter.
- ANN's 2007 interview says her characters come from stories she has heard and from her friends; it does not discuss suicide, death or the ending. No statement on why suicide is the origin of the shinigami was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's intent about suicide.**
- Publisher: Shueisha (Ribon, a girls' magazine). VIZ Media published it under Shojo Beat, rated Teen.
- TV Tropes notes that in 2015 the artist published an adults-only doujinshi about the adult-looking characters Izumi and Meroko under a different pen name; it is a separate work, not part of this series (TV Tropes; to verify). No controversies about the series itself were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you do with a life that is about to end, and what if it doesn't have to? Mitsuki is 12, ill, and forbidden to sing. When death spirits tell her she has a year, she spends it on her dream. The series then turns the question around: every death spirit chose to end a life that was not over, and each regrets it.

**The answer the story gives.** Choose life, even through grief. Mitsuki's darkest point comes not from the tumor but from mourning Eichi; she wants to die to be with him (fan wiki). Takuto, who tried to end his own life, persuades her to live. Her final choice, surgery, is a choice for a future she cannot see, and the story rewards it.

**Characters as arguments.** Mitsuki argues for courage and hope. Takuto is the cautionary witness: a singer who lost his voice and his will to live, then finds a reason in her. Fuzuki argues for safety by control and learns to let go. Izumi, a child who died trying to make an abusive mother smile, argues that love can be misdirected; he finds peace by understanding her. Oshige shows an adult trying to cope through drink and a bad bargain, then choosing a healthier love. Sheldan stands for rules of fate kept without mercy, and ends by sacrificing himself to heal Takuto (Wikipedia).

**Symbols and motifs.** The full moon (Mitsuki's name means "full moon"; Eichi loved the moon). The voice as life itself. Wings and animal-plush forms for death spirits who work with children. The transformation into a 16-year-old: a borrowed future. The concert as a promise kept to someone who is listening from beyond.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese shinigami of folklore and modern manga; the Buddhist idea that attachment holds the dead to this world; the Western guardian-angel story; and the "dying girl" melodrama, which reviewers say Tanemura keeps from becoming too sappy (Manga Report).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is honored but balanced by family; existential choice is the climax; the afterlife functions as comfort and as warning. Secular readers find a medical cure and human love; religious readers find a strong defense of life wrapped in an invented spiritual system.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 are a comic magical-girl idol story; from chapter 11 (v3) the drama deepens with Eichi's death and the shinigami's pasts (TV Tropes); volumes 4-5 are the heaviest, with Mitsuki's despair and the suicides in backstory; volumes 6-7 are the underworld rescue and a happy epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mitsuki has one year. What would you want to do with yours, and who would you want to hear from?
2. Why does Takuto's regret matter so much to Mitsuki's choice?
3. Fuzuki forbids music to protect Mitsuki. Was she right about the danger, wrong about the answer, or both?
4. If a friend talked about wanting to die, what would you do and who would you tell?
5. Takuto and Mitsuki become a couple only years later. Why do you think the story waits?
