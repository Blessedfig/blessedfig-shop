**Premise.** In the near-future city of Tsukumo, every citizen must hold a rights card under a Human Rights Protection Act; without one you cannot ride trains, use hospitals or pay electronically, and citizens lose their rights at 85 and are moved to "elderly special zones." People who give up or lose their cards become mujina (the Japanese word for badger, a shape-shifting trickster in folklore): they live on rooftops, pay cash, find doctors and housing on the black market and mostly work as hired killers or as influencers (Mangapedia; The Outer Haven). Ubume, 30, is a legendary mujina assassin with an electrified katana and no memory of her teenage years. A job goes wrong when the influencer-assassin Tenko interferes, and she is badly wounded. She ends up in the flat of Terumi Morgan, a divorced, burned-out head of a small game studio who has thought about suicide, together with Juno Oshima, 15, a runaway who was bullied at home and was tricked by a prostitution ring that took her rights card; Ubume rescued her (Mangapedia; The Outer Haven, vol. 1). A new card for Juno costs 800 million yen, so Ubume works off the debt by taking jobs from the broker Nive, while Terumi arranges for Juno to be registered as his daughter (vol. 2). Public Safety officers of "Division 9" hunt dangerous mujina (vol. 2); the men who exploited Juno come back for her and beat Terumi, and Ubume kills them (vols. 3-4). Ubume's memories begin to return, tied to a man in a tanuki suit and to what an ultra-elite did on an island (vol. 4) (The Outer Haven).

## Life message

**When a society puts human rights up for sale, the people it throws away can still choose to protect one another; a found family can give back the humanity a cruel system takes.**

| Socio-cultural | A satire of a Japan that ranks people by usefulness: rights expire at 85, girls are exploited, influencers turn killing into content, and the media fake audio to smear a politician (vol. 4). ## Creator and publisher context

- Inio Asano, born 1980 in Ishioka, Ibaraki; won the 2001 GX competition for young artists; known for character-driven, realist stories from slice of life to psychological horror; Dead Dead Demon's Dededede Destruction won the 66th Shogakukan Manga Award (2021). The Yomiuri Shimbun called him "one of the voices of his generation" (2010) (Wikipedia, Inio Asano).
- On-record intent (Automaton, July 2026, from his Weekly Ochiai appearance): the series began as a grounded story about a hopeless middle-aged man in a shopping street and became an action manga after he built the town in 3D; his recent work is inspired by open-world games like GTA. He builds over 90% of backgrounds himself in Blender and Unreal Engine, and said publishers do not currently allow AI tools, one reason among several for the pause until 2027.
- No statement by Asano on the series' sexual content or on religion was found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's intent.**
- Publisher: Shogakukan (Big Comic Superior, a seinen magazine). VIZ Media (owned by Shogakukan and Shueisha; from general knowledge) publishes it under VIZ Signature, rated Mature, and marks several digital chapters 18+.
- No controversies involving the creator's work on this series were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a person worth when worth has a price? In Tsukumo, rights are a card you can lose, sell or buy back, and at 85 they simply expire. The mujina are those who fell or stepped outside. The series asks whether people stripped of every legal protection still owe each other anything.

**The answer the story gives.** They do, and it is the only thing that saves them. Ubume begins with two rules, keep emotions out and never lose (VIZ vol. 4 synopsis); Terumi begins having given up on life. Taking in Juno, a girl adults have already used, forces both to care. The price of her rights becomes a debt Ubume pays in blood.

**Characters as arguments.** Ubume argues that skill is the only protection, then learns it is not enough. Terumi argues for withdrawal, then becomes a father. Nive argues that everything, including people, has a price, yet shows concern for Terumi. Tenko argues for fame, thrill and freedom without attachments. Mai shows what happens when someone sells her rights for love (vol. 2). Kashio, a police officer saved once by a mujina, argues that the law and its victims are not simple opposites (vol. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The rights card; rooftops as the mujina's country; the wrist health monitor that watches Terumi's despair; the 800-million-yen price tag; the man in the tanuki suit in Ubume's returning dreams (vol. 4).

**Traditions it echoes.** Cyberpunk dystopia, the assassin-and-child story, and Asano's own studies of alienated Japanese adults. Readers from many traditions will hear an old claim inside it: that human dignity is given, not granted, and that a society is judged by how it treats the weakest. The series makes that case negatively, by showing a world that has forgotten it.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Rights-based liberalism is the unspoken standard the world fails; authoritarian control and a market in persons are the villains. Strength rules is the mujina's way of life, and the story counts its cost. Communitarian care, in the found family, is the answer. No religious lens appears.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 sets up the world, the rescue and the household. Vol. 2 widens to Nive, Mai and Division 9. Vol. 3 brings back Juno's exploiters and Kashio's past. Vol. 4 turns to Ubume's memories and a political plot. Season 1 ended in April 2026 with a "final battle"; season 2 is due in 2027.

**Discussion questions.**
1. In this world, rights can be bought, sold and lost. Can a right really belong to someone if it can be taken away?
2. Why do you think the law takes rights away at 85? What does that say about how the society values people?
3. Ubume's rule was to keep emotions out. What changed it?
4. Terumi takes in two strangers. What does he get, and what does it cost him?
5. Tenko turns violence into content for followers. Where do you see that in real life?
