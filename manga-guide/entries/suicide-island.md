**Premise.** In a near-future Japan where citizens are managed by government ID, Sei, 19, a young man who has repeatedly tried to end his life by overdose and wrist-cutting, signs a form renouncing his "duty to live." He wakes on a remote island with other repeat survivors of suicide attempts. A government sign says they have been stripped of their IDs and their rights and duties as citizens, and may not leave. On the first day a group jumps from a building, and the survivors see what a failed fall does to a body. Sei and others decide that if they cannot die they must live. Led by the warm Ryou and the cool, clever Kai, the "mountain group" fishes, makes salt, grows rice and repairs an abandoned school, while Sei teaches himself to make a bow and hunt deer, with a puppy, Ikiru ("to live"), from a hermit who survived the island's earlier days as a penal colony. Kai turns out to be steering despairing people toward death; a rival "sea group" under the drug dealer Sawada rules by fear and sex; and the two groups go to war before the island is finally reopened as a place of recovery (Japanese Wikipedia; English Wikipedia; Dark Horse).

## Life message

**Even people who have tried to die can choose life again: meaning grows from hard work with your own hands, from caring for others and letting them care for you, and from passing life on to the next generation, while despair, domination and the voice that says death is a relief all destroy.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Kouji Mori (story and art), born November 28, 1966, in Tokyo; Nihon University fine arts. Works: Holyland (2000-2008), Suicide Island (2008-2016), Destroy and Revolution (2010-2016), Muhōtō (2019-2022), Sōsei no Taiga (2017-), D.Diver (2023-) (Wikipedia, Kouji Mori).
- A high-school friend of Kentaro Miura; since Miura's death in 2021 Mori has supervised the continuation of Berserk, from volume 42 (2023) (Wikipedia, Kouji Mori; Dark Horse cites Berserk vol. 42).
- On-record intent (Da Vinci News interview, 2016, near the series' end): he made the series because the subject weighed on him personally and he wanted to make something that might help; he does not claim to understand the depth of despair or to be able to stop anyone, but speaks to people who want to live yet sometimes think of dying, urging them to look to the people in front of them; he values entertainment and decided the last chapter before starting.
- Research: Mori made repeated trips to southern islands to spear-fish and went into the mountains with hunters in Kyoto, and, as in Holyland, explains survival methods with diagrams (Japanese Wikipedia).
- The volume 1 launch in 2009 included a talk with psychiatrist Yasufumi Nagoshi, who works with suicidal patients (Comic Natalie).
- Publisher: Hakusensha (Young Animal, seinen). English: Dark Horse, rated 18+. No controversies involving the creator were found. No statements on religion or politics were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you have already tried to die, is there any reason to live? Suicide Island takes people who have each answered no, removes every support (family, money, law, medicine) and asks whether a reason can be found in what is left: food, other people and the work of staying alive.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, and it is found in doing. Sei finds his place in the forest with a homemade bow; Minoru finds his in a rice field; Boushi in the tools he makes; Tae in nursing. The story's conclusion, reached through deaths and a birth, is that life is something passed on, and that suicide is not good (Japanese Wikipedia, final arc). Mori said he wanted to speak to people who want to live but sometimes think of dying (Da Vinci News).

**Characters as arguments.** Sei argues for life found in the body and the land. Ryou argues for living for others while carrying grief, and dies having done so. Kai is the argument for death made gently: kind words, offers to go together, and finally murder; his last confession that he acted from feeling left behind turns the philosophy into wounded envy. Sawada is the argument for instinct and force; he is revealed as a frightened man. Liv and Nao argue that people who were used by others can still choose life, and Tomo that a person rejected by her father can be accepted by friends.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bow and arrow, a tool both for feeding the group and for killing. The dog Ikiru ("to live") and the name Liv, which Sei chose to echo "live" and his own name. The rice harvest, food made from patience. The school building, a place of youth reclaimed. Ryou's bandana, passed to Sei and then to his son.

**Traditions it echoes.** Lord of the Flies and Battle Royale (Dark Horse's comparison), the castaway story (Robinson Crusoe), and Japanese survival manga. Its conclusion echoes "choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19) for Jewish and Christian readers, and the precept against taking life for Buddhists, though the story gets there without religion.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and communitarianism are affirmed together: meaning is chosen, but it is chosen with others. Nihilism and might-makes-right are given real voices (Kai, Sawada) and defeated. Secular humanism carries the ending: a human policy change, not a miracle, opens the island.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-4: arrival, first deaths, learning to fish, hunt and make salt, Ryou's failed raft escape. Volumes 5-12: Sawada's sea group appears, Nao flees to the mountain group, Kai's hidden killings are exposed, Sei and Liv fall in love. Volumes 13-15: open war; Minoru dies; Sawada is killed. Volumes 16-17: Kai kills Ryou and is killed; a birth; the island is reopened as a place of recovery (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. On the first day, why did the group decide to live after seeing the jump?
2. Kai seemed kind. What made his kindness dangerous?
3. Why do people who have work to do on the island get better?
4. What does Sei mean by passing on life, and do you agree?
5. If you knew a friend was thinking about dying, who could you tell? (Parents: have a crisis line ready, such as 988 in the US.)
