**Premise.** Makio Kōdai, 35, is a reclusive novelist who avoids people and has not spoken to her family in years. When her older sister Minori and Minori's partner die in a car crash, their daughter Asa, 15, is passed around at the funeral as a burden; Makio, who disliked her sister, takes Asa in anyway, while telling her plainly that she may never love her the way a parent would but will never treat her as unwanted (ep. 1; Wikipedia). Makio suggests Asa keep a journal. The two very different people learn to live together: Asa, outgoing and raised strictly, works through anger and grief and finds her voice in a school band; Makio, socially anxious and prone to low moods, relies on her old friend Nana, her ex-boyfriend Kasamachi and the guardianship lawyer Tōno. Asa's friend Emiri comes out as a lesbian with a girlfriend (vols. 7-9). Asa learns about her mother from a diary meant for her 20th birthday (vols. 4-6). In the final volumes Asa worries about her future, Makio begins to share her own past and tells Asa directly how much she cares, and the story ends at Asa's graduation with a glimpse of Asa as an adult (ciatr; BookLive; Wikipedia).

## Life message

**People never fully understand one another, but they can still live together with honesty, respect for each other's feelings and steady care; grief is not solved, it is lived with.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can two people who do not and perhaps cannot understand each other share a life? Makio, who prefers the people in her novels, and Asa, who grew up being told what to want, are thrown together by a death neither chose.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if understanding is replaced by respect. Makio tells Asa she need not feel what others expect, and that her feelings belong to her (Quick Japan via Wikipedia). Yamashita states the theme directly: people can never truly understand each other (Wikipedia). Love, when it is finally spoken (vols. 10-11), comes after years of kept promises.

**Characters as arguments.** Makio argues for honesty and boundaries, even when they hurt. Asa's mother, seen through Asa's memories and her diary, argues for order and high standards; the story is fair to her (vols. 4-6). Kasamachi and Tōno show men stepping away from expectations (vols. 7-9). Emiri argues for being oneself with one's closest friend. Nana shows friendship lasting decades (ep. 3).

**Symbols and motifs.** The journal; Makio's precise explanations of words and kanji (AniList review); the "strange land" of the title, each person as a foreign country; bento lunches; Asa's song lyrics; the diary meant for Asa's 20th birthday.

**Traditions it echoes.** Josei family drama; the found-family story; grief narratives such as Housekeeping and Fun Home, to which Anime Feminist compares it.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and liberal respect for boundaries are affirmed; duty is affirmed in Makio's kept promise; secular humanism frames grief. Traditional family authority is questioned, not mocked.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3: moving in, funeral aftermath, school begins. Vols. 4-6: Makio's essay about her "roommate," Minori's diary, Asa's first year. Vols. 7-9: Emiri's coming out, Kasamachi and Tōno, Makio's writer's block. Vols. 10-11: Asa's future, Makio's past, graduation and Asa as an adult (ciatr).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Makio says she may never love Asa like a parent but will never treat her as unwanted. Is that a kind thing to say?
2. Can you care for someone you do not understand? How?
3. Why does Makio refuse to tell Asa how she should feel about her parents' deaths?
4. What would you want written in a diary your parent left for you?
5. Emiri was afraid to tell Asa something important. What makes a friend safe to tell?
