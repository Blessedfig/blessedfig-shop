**Premise.** In 2011 Florida, 19-year-old Jolyne Cujoh, the estranged daughter of Jotaro Kujo (hero of Part 3), takes the fall for her boyfriend's hit-and-run and is sentenced to 15 years in Green Dolphin Street Prison. The trial was rigged. The prison chaplain, Father Enrico Pucci, is the last disciple of the vampire DIO. He framed her to lure Jotaro, whose memories hold DIO's plan to reach "Heaven." Pucci's Stand, Whitesnake, pulls memories and powers out of people as discs. Jolyne gains her own Stand, gathers fellow inmates as allies, and fights to save her father. Pucci revives DIO's bone, which absorbs prisoners' souls and becomes a "Green Baby"; he fuses with it, escapes, and at Cape Canaveral his Stand evolves into Made in Heaven, which speeds up time until the universe ends and restarts so that every person knows their fate in advance. Nearly all the heroes die. A boy, Emporio, kills Pucci, and the new universe that forms holds altered versions of Jolyne and her friends who do not remember him (Wikipedia; jojowiki).

## Life message

**A good life is chosen, not fated: loyalty to family and friends, courage under unjust power, and sacrifice for the weak are worth more than the peace of a destiny imposed from above, and hope in an unknown future is part of being human.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | A prison is a closed world of rank, alliances and threat, and Jolyne survives by building a coalition. The Joestar "bloodline" and the star birthmark carry the story: Araki says a legacy passes on "regardless of whether their recipient is male or female" (Bunko vol. 11 interview). Romance is mostly Anasui's one-sided pursuit of Jolyne. Pucci's crimes start from a sibling-protection impulse (separating his sister from a man who is secretly their brother, ch. 127-129). |
| Pragmatic | The heroes win by wit, not strength. ANN's reviewer: "physical strength is no substitute for ingenuity and resilience." | Religious | The main villain is a Catholic priest and prison chaplain (jojowiki). He speaks in religious terms: humans are defined by "the desire to 'go to Heaven'" (ch. 34); "Our Lord Jesus Christ was fated to be put on the cross" (ch. 143); he tells DIO "I love you as I love God" (ch. 94) and prays to dedicate himself to "your very will" (ch. 148). He keeps a confession secret (ch. 127-129), yet his "Heaven" is DIO's plan, not the Church's. Araki said Pucci's priesthood was "just a facade, and not to impart some religious message" (jojowiki, Creation and Development). The ending is a universe reset with altered versions of the dead, which reads to some as rebirth or reincarnation (jojowiki lists Irene as Jolyne's "reincarnation"). |
| Socio-cultural | A Japanese artist's view of the American prison system. Anime Feminist reads it as "critique of the carceral state" with guards who assault prisoners. Pucci's backstory involves a Ku Klux Klan "detective agency" lynching his twin, who was raised by a mixed-race family, in the American South (ch. 128). Jolyne was the first female JoJo lead; Araki refused an editor's suggestion to make her male (Wikipedia). |

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, most frames see a false "Heaven" defeated and free choice, love and sacrifice affirmed, so mid-to-high grades are common. The dividing lines are three. First, a Catholic priest as villain: Araki says it was a facade, and the story never attacks the Church, but some families will want to discuss it. Second, fate: traditions with strong teaching on divine decree (Reformed, Muslim, Sikh) can use Pucci to explain how their teaching differs from fatalism. Third, the souls, ghosts and reset universe weigh most for traditions that are careful about spiritism and the state of the dead (Adventist, Jehovah's Witness, Pentecostal).

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Pucci's fate-acceptance theology wins almost everything and then loses at the last step to a child's choice. The heroes die, but their sacrifice keeps the future open.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** In a 2016 Buzz Plus poll of JoJo fans on final villains, Pucci placed 2nd, behind only Part 1 Dio (Otaku USA). TV Tropes' audience-reaction page calls him "easily the most well-intentioned and genuinely caring Big Bad in the franchise," notes fans who claim he "won," and records a "Draco in Leather Pants" reading that overlooks his killings. He is one of the most-memed JoJo villains, including a fan nickname about his look and his subtext with DIO (TV Tropes YMMV). The ending splits fans between a moving send-off and a "cop-out" (TV Tropes YMMV); ANN's This Week in Anime writers called it "the strongest part thematically." TV Tropes also says Stone Ocean is among the least popular Parts in Japan but has a larger fanbase in the US.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987. On Pucci (via jojowiki, Creation and Development): Araki "initially came up with the concept of Pucci being a priest as just a facade, and not to impart some religious message"; he "wondered if such complex villains belonged in a shonen manga"; he considers Pucci the strongest villain in some respects.
- On the Part (Bunko vol. 11 interview, February 2009, jojowiki translation): the title "symbolizes Jolyne's stone will, as well as the femininity of the ocean," and the prison as "an ocean of stone." The central idea is a "season of being attacked." He felt "there's nothing left to draw" and changed the ending at the last minute so that "time, the protagonists, and the bloodline itself can make a full cycle of the universe and return to their origin," and spoke of a return to nature. He calls Jolyne "the toughest" lead and the one he "wanted to be happiest," and says a legacy passes "regardless of whether their recipient is male or female." He visited a Florida prison for research and felt claustrophobic there.
- On the female lead (Wikipedia, Jolyne Cujoh): editor Hideto Azuma suggested making Jolyne a man; Araki refused. He had earlier shelved a female-led one-shot (Gorgeous Irene) believing readers would not accept it.
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media publishes the English edition; Viz is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). The anime was produced by David Production with Warner Bros. Japan, as a Netflix release (Wikipedia).
- Controversies: (1) Netflix's batch release drew criticism from ANN reviewers and fans. (2) Fans objected that the anime removed the Klan robes from the lynching scene (TV Tropes YMMV). (3) Fan debate over Pucci's ethnicity and his family's race (TV Tropes YMMV; The Mary Sue). (4) The manga's "queer" slur was dropped from the anime (TV Tropes YMMV). No widely reported controversy over the priest-villain or the religious content was found in the pages opened.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Would you rather know your fate or keep your freedom? Pucci believes the pain of human life comes from not knowing what will happen. If everyone knew their future in advance, he says, they could prepare for it, accept it, and be at peace: "One's resolution eradicates despair!" (ch. 157). Stone Ocean puts this question to a young woman locked in a prison for a crime she did not commit, which is to say, a person whose fate has already been decided for her.

**The answer the story gives.** The story answers through action rather than argument. Jolyne will not accept the sentence given to her by a rigged court, and she will not accept Pucci's fate either. She dies to save Emporio (ch. 155). Emporio, the last one left, uses his friends' gifts to change his own fate and kill Pucci. In jojowiki's summary of ch. 157-158, he replies that the path of fate is the path of justice. ANN's This Week in Anime writers read the ending as a rejection of Pucci's fate in favor of "hope and choice." The new universe that results is gentler, but it costs everything: the people Emporio loved exist again only as strangers.

**Characters as arguments.** Pucci is the argument for peace through certainty. He is also, in his own telling, a believer: humans are defined by "the desire to 'go to Heaven'" (ch. 34). But his god is DIO, whom he loves "as I love God" (ch. 94), and SYFY Wire calls him "certainly not the devoted man of the cloth he pretends to be." Wes, his twin, is the argument from the other side of fate. He was stolen at birth, lynched by Klansmen his own brother hired, and kept alive by a power that would not let him die (ch. 127-129). Jolyne is will and hope. Jotaro is a father's late love. Anasui is love as obsession, a man who killed a former lover now devoted to Jolyne. Emporio is the child who inherits everyone's sacrifice.

**Symbols and motifs.** The prison, "an ocean of stone" (Araki). Discs: memory and selfhood that can be stolen. Gravity: Pucci's word for fate and attraction between people (ch. 103, 140). The new moon and the launch site at Cape Canaveral: the place where a new heaven is supposed to begin. Prime numbers, which Pucci counts for strength because they are "solitary" (ch. 49). The star birthmark: identity that survives even the end of the universe (ch. 158).

**Traditions it echoes.** Debates over predestination and free will in Christianity, Islam and Judaism (Pucci's line that Christ was "fated" to the cross, ch. 143, is the clearest point of contact). Stoic acceptance of fate (amor fati). Greek tragedy, where defiance of fate brings ruin, which ANN's writers note. Cyclical cosmologies in Hindu and Buddhist thought. The American prison film and the history of lynching in the South. (The links to Stoicism and cyclical cosmology are my own framing.)

**How each lens is portrayed.** Christianity appears almost only through a corrupted priest. The story does not argue with Christian doctrine; it shows a man using its language for DIO's plan, and Araki says no religious message was meant. Fatalism and authoritarian "care" are critiqued. Existential choice, loyalty and sacrifice are affirmed. Feminist and carceral readings are prominent in reviews (Anime Feminist; ANN). Racism is shown at its most violent (ch. 128).

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-45) are a prison thriller: survival, framing and recovering Jotaro's discs. Volumes 6-11 (ch. 46-99) turn to DIO's bone, the Green Baby and Pucci's escape. Volumes 12-15 (ch. 100-135) bring DIO's sons and the Weather Report backstory. Volumes 16-17 (ch. 136-158) become a cosmic battle at Cape Canaveral, with the deaths of the heroes, the end of the universe and its reset.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Pucci says knowing your future would make you happy (ch. 157). Would it? What would you lose?
2. How does Pucci's idea of "Heaven" (ch. 34) differ from what your family believes about heaven?
3. Pucci says Christ was "fated" to be crucified (ch. 143). How does your tradition talk about God's plan and human choice?
4. Jolyne gives her life for Emporio (ch. 155). Is a sacrifice still worth it if the world is reset afterward?
5. Anasui killed a former girlfriend and then becomes a hero who loves Jolyne. Should the story have treated his past differently?
