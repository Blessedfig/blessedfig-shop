**Premise.** Seiichi Osabe is a quiet second-year middle schooler (about 13-14; Japanese Wikipedia gives a 1981 birth date) in a green suburban town in Gunma, doted on by his young, beautiful mother Seiko while his salaryman father Ichirō keeps his distance. On a summer hike with relatives, his cousin Shigeru jokingly shoves him near a cliff and Seiko pulls him back. Moments later, Seiko pushes Shigeru off a higher cliff and smiles at her son, the only witness. Seiichi backs her story to the police (Japan News/Yomiuri review). What follows is told entirely from Seiichi's point of view: Seiko's possessive control tightens, she forbids his friendship with classmate Yuiko Fukiishi, and Seiichi's stammer and grip on reality worsen. The truth eventually comes out, Seiko is taken into custody, Seiichi recovers a memory of his mother throwing him from a hill as a small child, and in a ritual meant to sever himself from her he kills Shigeru (vols. 9-12, publisher synopses). Twenty years later Seiichi lives alone and empty, plans to end his life after his father's funeral, and is stopped by a police call: his aging mother has been found confused and taken into protective custody. The last volumes follow the long night in which he learns where her darkness began, and his decision to take her in until the end (vols. 13-17, publisher synopses).

## Life message

**The series holds up no ideal good life. It argues that love which possesses another person destroys both, that a child must somehow become a separate self to live at all, and that the "tracks" of harm passed from parent to child can at least be seen, understood and allowed to stop, even when they cannot be undone.** Anime Collective's reviewer reads the ending as "a quiet decision to stop the cycle" (opened, paraphrase).

## Creator and publisher context

- Shūzō Oshimi was born in 1981 and grew up in Kiryū, Gunma (see The Flowers of Evil entry). Blood on the Tracks is also set in Gunma, and the protagonist shares his birth year (ja.wikipedia gives Seiichi's birth date as March 19, 1981).
- **On the mother and autobiography (ORICON NEWS, February 27, 2019, interview with Fumi Fumiko, opened; translations ours):** He calls the work semi-autobiographical and says untrue things may be mixed in because he is "drawing to explore the essence." His aim: "I want to grasp the true nature of my relationship with my parents." Before he started drawing, he "hated" his mother, but "gradually I came to realize I really love her." He tries not to call her a "toxic parent" himself, did not want "an obvious monster parent," and describes "the anguish of not being able to make parents completely villainous." He wants readers "to vicariously experience the protagonist's subjective world." He said drawing it worsened his stutter to the point of disrupting daily life. At that time he did not know how the relationship would settle.
- **ANN, October 16, 2024 (opened):** He drew it "strictly from the main character's perspective" and drew the mother "as beautifully as possible, as if it's almost scary." He relates closely to Seiichi, knew the climax in advance but not the ending, calls his protagonists "projections of myself," and writes for "myself when I was 14."
- **Anime Feminist, December 6, 2024 (opened):** He bases stories on "personal memories and thoughts" and is "putting everything into these personal themes"; his characters must discover that their sense of self "is actually fragile and easy to shatter." The interviewers, not Oshimi, call the mother "autobiographical" and describe the afterwords as expressing resentment born of growing up with an abusive mother.
- **Final-volume afterword (August 2023), as summarized on Moegirlpedia (opened; afterword not read):** he drew only what needed drawing and finished the story; the ending is a kind of happiness for him but cruel for others; he apologizes for making the work and says he drew it in order to keep living; he thanks his editor and family. Anime Collective reports the afterword shows a photo of him and his mother with the faces cut out (opened; to verify).
- No statement by Oshimi on religion or politics was found. This entry reports only what he has said on the record about his family and does not go beyond it. No widely reported personal controversy was found.
- Publishers: Shogakukan (Japan). Vertical (English), part of Kodansha USA (from general knowledge; to verify); distributed by Penguin Random House.

**Profile notes for parents:** A literary psychological horror about a mother's control of her son, told entirely from inside the son's mind, that follows him into middle age. It argues for no ideology, but it asks hard questions about love, possession, what children owe parents and whether harm can stop with one generation. Families reading it with older teens often talk about recognizing emotional abuse, where to get help, and suicidal thoughts.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a mother's love when it leaves no room for the child to exist apart from her? And can a person who was never allowed to become a self still become one? Seiichi begins the story content inside his mother's devotion. One smile on a cliff edge shows him that the devotion has a second face, and the rest of the series is his slow, painful discovery of where his mother ends and he begins.

**The answer the story gives.** The first half answers bleakly: enmeshment does not loosen on its own. Seiichi's attempts to please his mother, keep her secret and keep his promises only draw him deeper, until the only escape he can imagine is a ritual killing meant "to kill himself" and sever the bond (vol. 12 synopsis). The second half answers more quietly. Twenty years of emptiness follow. Then the mother returns as a frail, confused old woman, and the son learns that she too was a child no one loved (Japan News). He cannot hurt her (vol. 15), and in the end he takes her in. The final conversation happens in a dream, and the publisher says the horrors of the past fade (vol. 17). The story offers release and understanding, not a clean reconciliation.

**Characters as arguments.** Seiko is the argument that love grounded in fear becomes possession, and that possession can kill. Oshimi refused to make her "an obvious monster parent" (ORICON), so she is also the argument that abusers were often harmed first. Ichirō is the argument that a parent who does not look is part of the harm. Fukiishi, herself abused by her father, is the argument that another wounded person can still show a way out. Seiichi carries both sides: the victim and, by vol. 12, a person who does terrible harm.

**Symbols and motifs.** The title's "tracks" are ruts left by wheels, harm pressed into a path that the next generation follows. The cliff and the hill are where the mother nearly ended two children's lives. Butterflies and surreal close-ups mark Seiichi's dissociation (Anime Collective). The mother is drawn "as beautifully as possible, as if it's almost scary" (ANN 2024). Faces matter throughout: chapter titles include "Face," "Eye Contact," "Expression" and "That Face."

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese I-novel and confessional fiction; psychoanalytic stories of mother and son; Western domestic horror. Reviewers compare it to *Mommie Dearest* (The Manga Critic), Pink Floyd's *The Wall* (Anime UK News) and Poe's "The Fall of the House of Usher" (The Beat). Filial piety, central to Confucian-influenced cultures, is the duty the story tests without naming.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism shapes the core: a self must be claimed. A Dharmic reading finds attachment and inherited suffering, and release through seeing. Nihilism appears in the adult Seiichi's emptiness and is not argued down, but life continues. Confucian filial duty is neither praised nor rejected; it is lived out in its hardest form. No religious lens is present.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-3 (ch. 1-24): the cliff, the cover-up, the love letter, dread inside the home. Vols. 4-8 (ch. 25-68): Fukiishi, running away, the return home, Shigeru's waking, rising suspicion. Vols. 9-12 (ch. 69-108): the truth comes out, the recovered memory, the killing, custody and the courtroom. Vols. 13-14 (ch. 109-127): twenty years later, emptiness, the father's death and a suicide plan. Vols. 15-17 (ch. 128-153): the mother found, her past told, life together, decline and the dream conversation.

**Discussion questions.**
1. At the start Seiichi thinks his mother's attention is ordinary love. What are the signs that it is something else, and how could a friend or adult have noticed?
2. Seiko was not loved by her own parents. Does understanding her past change how we judge what she did?
3. Seiichi tries to free himself by harming someone else. Why does that not work, and what does?
4. What does a child owe a parent who did great harm? What do your family's tradition and sources say?
5. Adult Seiichi plans to end his life and a phone call stops him. If someone you know felt that empty, who could they talk to?
