**Premise.** England, around 1900. Hanako Kujō, a former teacher from Japan who hopes to become a novelist, travels to London to meet her favorite author, Victor Franks, and is turned away by his publisher. Alice Douglas, a young noblewoman who overhears, hires her as her personal maid and promises an introduction to Franks on one condition: that one day Hanako will kill her (vol. 1; Wikipedia; Seven Seas). Hanako learns that Alice is engaged to Edward, a kind nobleman she does not love, who hopes the marriage will quiet rumors that Alice once loved her governess, Eliza McGovern; the trials and imprisonment of Oscar Wilde hang over the household as a warning (vol. 1; ANN; The Beat). Alice tells Hanako of her love for Eliza and its sad end; when Hanako falls ill, Alice learns Hanako's own secret, and the two find they have something unexpected in common (vol. 2; Seven Seas; Okazu). Alice reveals that she herself is Victor Franks. Hanako confesses her love and they kiss; Alice agrees to marry Edward and Hanako to leave, but at the church wedding Alice declares her love for Hanako before the congregation, leaves, gives up her title, and the two go to make a life together (vol. 3; Seven Seas; Anime Feminist).

## Life message

**Love does not have to hide or end in despair: leaving a beautiful but confining life to live honestly with the person you love is worth what it costs, and the wish to die can give way to a life chosen together.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Dr. Pepperco (story and art) first met yuri through Kaoru Kurimoto's Rose of Unter den Linden; drew on photographs, picture books, a book on Victorian jewelry and a Harrods catalog; used dip pen and ink for the first time on this series; and asked the editor to check historic stamps and postmarks for a letter scene (Wikipedia, citing Natalie and Magcomi interviews).
- On-record intent: Pepperco conceived Hanako as a 'New Woman' (an independent woman of the era) and Alice as someone who must hide her true self to keep her reputation; literature, they said, helps people keep going despite life's struggles, so the two leads' relationship begins with books (Wikipedia). The series alludes to Oscar Wilde, Kate Chopin and Sarah Orne Jewett; 'Victor Franks' nods to Mary Shelley's Frankenstein.
- No statements by the creator on religion or politics beyond the above were found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator** (a German interview on manga-passion.de was found but not opened).
- Publisher: Mag Garden (MAGxiv web magazine). Seven Seas publishes it in English under its girls' love line, rated Teen.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can a person live honestly in a world that punishes who she is? Alice's answer, at first, is no: better to be killed than disgraced. The story sets out to find another answer.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, if someone refuses to accept that the person is the problem. Hanako will not kill Alice; she tries to understand her, sees the world's cruelty rather than Alice's sickness (Hopper, ANN), and in the end the two leave the garden together (vol. 3).

**Characters as arguments.** Alice argues from despair that appearance is everything. Hanako, an outsider and a reader, argues that love is free and that literature shows other ways to live. Edward argues, kindly, that marriage can protect Alice from rumor; the story rejects that protection as another kind of confinement. Eliza, who left for Japan, is the love that history did not allow.

**Symbols and motifs.** The rose garden, beautiful and pruned to uniformity. Books and pen names: 'Victor Franks' as a hidden author like Frankenstein's hidden maker. Oscar Wilde's fate. Letters and stamps. Hands, which Pepperco drew to show feeling (Wikipedia).

**Traditions it echoes.** Victorian and Edwardian romance, the 'New Woman' novel, Sarah Orne Jewett's 'Martha's Lady' (Dee, Anime Feminist), Japanese Class S fiction, and the real Ladies of Llangollen, whom Friedman compares to the ending (Okazu).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed: the happy ending is living openly. Traditionalism is critiqued as a garden that prunes people. Liberalism appears in the insistence that marriage should be chosen. Feminism shapes Hanako's independence and Alice's escape.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the strange bargain, the rumors and Wilde. Vol. 2: Eliza's story, Hanako's secret, Victor Franks. Vol. 3: Alice revealed as Franks, the confession, the wedding and the departure.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Alice ask Hanako to kill her? What does that tell you about the pressure she is under?
2. Hanako says no, and stays. What does real help look like when someone is in despair?
3. Alice is engaged to a kind man she does not love. Is that unfair to him, to her, or both?
4. Many stories from Alice's era ended in tragedy for people like her. Why do you think this author chose a happy ending?
5. What do you think your family's tradition would say about Alice's choice? Where do you agree or disagree?
