**Premise.** Risa Amakawa, a cool, studious high-school girl, carries a heavy bag ready for every emergency and never asks anyone for help; ANN's reviewer ties this to the pressure of living with a single mother. One rainy evening she patches up Zen Ohira, a delinquent beaten and left under a tree in a park, and refuses any repayment. Zen, leader of one of two rival groups at an all-boys school, keeps turning up to help her, and saving him draws the attention of his rivals (vol. 1; Kodansha blurb; ANN). She tutors him and his friends (vol. 2), the two confess their feelings when he visits her sick bed (vol. 3), and she stays a night with his lively family when locked out (vol. 4). Zen takes a beating to protect her from the rival gang of Fuumin (vol. 5); Zen's past with a girl called Kuma unsettles her (vol. 6); Fuumin joins her cram school, studies hard and falls for her too (vols. 7-8) (Kodansha USA and Apple Books blurbs).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it strength or fear that makes someone refuse all help? Risa Amakawa has a bag for every emergency and never needs anyone, or so she says. When she rescues a battered delinquent who will not let a kindness go unpaid, the series asks what she is really protecting herself from.

**The answer the story gives.** Learning to lean on others is not weakness. Each time Zen steps in, Risa's walls drop a little, until she lets herself cry on his shoulder (vol. 2), admits her feelings (vol. 3) and is welcomed by his family (vol. 4). The story treats mutual dependence as the mature form of strength.

**Characters as arguments.** Risa argues for self-sufficiency, and the story gently answers her. Zen argues that loyalty and repaying kindness matter more than reputation. Fuumin, the rival gang leader, turns out to be a hard worker studying for exams (vol. 7), complicating the picture of delinquents as simply bad.

**Symbols and motifs.** Risa's heavy bag of supplies; rain at their first meeting; the umbrella and first-aid kit; the shared study table.

**Traditions it echoes.** The good-girl/bad-boy shojo romance (ANN) and the yanki genre of rough boys with soft hearts. Faith readers may hear the Good Samaritan in the opening scene.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism is affirmed. The delinquent honor code is admired when it protects and shown as dangerous when it feuds. Feminist readers find a capable heroine and a familiar protector trope.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2 (stories 1-8): meeting, rescues, tutoring. Vols. 3-4 (9-16): confession, dating, Zen's family. Vols. 5-6 (17-24): rival gangs and rival loves, Kuma. Vols. 7-9 (25-36): the relationship deepens, cram school, Fuumin's rivalry and exams (blurbs; later chapters uncollected and unchecked).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think Risa never asks for help? Is that a strength?
2. Zen insists on repaying Risa. Why does that matter to him?
3. Zen kisses Risa by surprise. What should he have done instead?
4. Zen fights to protect Risa. Are there better ways to protect someone?
5. Who in your life would you call if you were "locked out"?
