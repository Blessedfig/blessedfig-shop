**Premise.** In a sand-buried, post-apocalyptic world, Gordon, a gruff middle-aged cook, drives a food truck with a cannon on the roof and almost no customers. He nearly runs over Arisa, a naked girl asleep in the middle of the desert, gives her clothes and food, and finds that an armed militia is hunting her (VIZ; Shinchosha). Arisa eats enormously, fights with super-strength and heals in a day (TV Tropes). The pair hunt giant squid, free a beer-brewing town from a cruel colonel (v1), visit a sauna, cook escargot and curry, and meet Gordon's former subordinate Kyle, who is traveling with Arisa's "little sister" Myna (v2). The truth comes out in volume 3: Gordon is a former general of Vald, the world's ruling military, who faked his death, and Arisa is a "Doll," a clone grown from the cells of Colonel Sarah, who needs regular injections to live. Arisa chooses freedom with Gordon over a return to the lab; the two become lovers, and the army hunts them down (TV Tropes; Goodreads reviews; Calvin's Canon).

## Life message

**Freedom to live as you choose, eating good food beside someone who cares for you, is worth more than a long life under someone else's control.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a life worth if someone else controls it? Gordon was a general who faked his death to escape the army; Arisa is a clone who escaped a lab. Both are hunted by the same power.

**The answer the story gives.** Freedom, food and companionship are worth dying for. When Arisa learns that she will die without the lab's injections, she chooses to stay with Gordon anyway; the army kills them, and their friends carry on their way of life (TV Tropes; Calvin's Canon). ANN found the ending an abrupt downer; The Slings and Arrows found it poignant.

**Characters as arguments.** Gordon argues that walking away from power is possible. Arisa argues that a person made as a weapon can choose her own life, though the story also uses her as a pin-up (The Manga Critic). Kyle and Sarah argue for duty, then choose loyalty to a friend. Colonel Diego shows what rule by force becomes.

**Symbols and motifs.** The food truck as a moving home and a small free state; the cannon on top of it; appetite as life; the injection as the leash; the sacred eel in a tank beside Arisa in a tank (v3).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Mad Max road movie, the food manga, and the "mysterious girl weapon" story (Outlaw Star and others, per Goodreads reviewers). Its live-free-or-die ending echoes American frontier ideals. A Goodreads reader says it also repeats the old born-sexy-yesterday pattern: a childlike, naive girl drawn as an object of desire (Goodreads reviewer).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Libertarian freedom is celebrated; authoritarian militarism and the Doll program are condemned; hedonism is cheerful. The story does not apply its anti-control message to its own treatment of Arisa's body, which is where nearly all critics object.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-4) is episodic: meeting, squid hunt, Blue Lake City, backstory hints. Volume 2 (ch. 5-10) slows into sauna, snail and curry chapters, with Kyle and Myna, and ends with Arisa's capture. Volume 3 (ch. 11-16) turns to rescue, revelation, romance and death (ANN; The Slings and Arrows).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Gordon left the army by faking his death. What did he gain, and what did he lose?
2. Arisa chooses freedom over a longer life. Was that a good choice?
3. Vald treats Arisa as equipment. How does the book itself treat her? Are those different?
4. Many readers objected to Gordon and Arisa's relationship. Why is the age difference a problem even in a fictional world?
5. How does sharing food build trust between people?
