**Premise.** Hime Sakuragaike has loved her childhood friend Akira Yonezawa for years, and since they were 12 she has been the only one who knew Akira is a girl (Comics Beat). When they start high school, Akira begins living openly as a girl and wears the girls' uniform. Classmates mock her (Kirkus), and Hime, a fierce tomboy who protected Akira from bullies as children, starts wearing the boys' uniform in solidarity and defends Akira aggressively, which backfires on them both (vol. 1). New friends, the fashion-loving Anzu and the gyaru Yukka, and a calm teacher help Hime question her own feelings and assumptions (ANN). In vol. 2 Akira's father refuses to let her grow her hair (Cannonball Read; Kirkus via Wikipedia), Yukka shares being bullied for being a lesbian, and Akira joins the soccer team as manager because of a crush on a boy, widening the rift with Hime. In vol. 3 a boy's interest in Hime makes Akira jealous, and both question what being a girl means to them. In vol. 4 they reconcile and Hime confesses her love (Penguin Random House blurbs). Akira turns her down; after a time skip Hime marries a man, and Akira, with long hair at last, catches the bouquet at the wedding (MyAnimeList user review).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** How do you love someone well while they are becoming who they are? Hime has loved Akira since childhood and was the first to know she is a girl. When Akira steps into the open, Hime's love turns into a fight with the whole school, and it is not what Akira needs.

**The answer the story gives.** Listen. Let the other person lead. The best help Hime gives is not the boys' uniform but honesty, apology and patience (vols. 3-4). And love does not always become romance: Hime's confession is turned down, the friendship survives, and each finds her own life (MyAnimeList user review).

**Characters as arguments.** Akira argues for the right to live as herself and, just as much, to be ordinary and unremarkable at school. Hime argues for protective loyalty and learns its limits. Yukka and Anzu argue that style and cuteness belong to anyone. The teacher argues that society's categories are conveniences and identity is chosen. Akira's father argues for fixed rules and is the story's sharpest pain (vol. 2).

**Symbols and motifs.** School uniforms traded and swapped; Akira's hair, forbidden in vol. 2 and long at the end; a bouquet caught at a wedding.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo friendship-romance, the coming-out story, and Japanese manga about gender such as Wandering Son (from general knowledge; to verify). Traditional religious readers will recognize the questions but answer them differently; the story itself does not engage religion.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and progressivism are affirmed throughout; postmodern questioning of categories is voiced by the teacher; traditionalism appears only as the father's rules and is shown as hurtful. Communitarianism wins at the end in the repaired friendship.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: coming out at school, the uniform swap. Vol. 2: the rift, the father, Yukka's story, Akira's crush. Vol. 3: the silence between them and each girl's question about gender. Vol. 4: confession, reconciliation and a time skip to adulthood.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hime meant well when she wore the boys' uniform. Why did it make things harder for Akira?
2. What is the difference between speaking up for a friend and speaking for them?
3. Akira's father makes a rule about her hair. What do you think he was afraid of? How do your family's beliefs shape how you see him?
4. Hime's love is not returned, but the friendship survives. How?
5. How should we treat classmates whose lives or beliefs are different from ours?
