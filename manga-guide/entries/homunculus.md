**Premise.** Susumu Nakoshi, 34, a former employee of a foreign bank, lives in his car in Shinjuku, moving between a luxury hotel and a park full of homeless people. Manabu Ito, a 22-year-old medical student from a hospital-owning family, offers him 700,000 yen to undergo trepanation, the drilling of a hole in the skull, which Ito believes may awaken a "sixth sense" (he says he wants to study the mind and disprove the occult). Nakoshi refuses until his car is towed. Afterwards, when he covers his right eye, his left eye shows him people's "homunculi": grotesque figures of their hidden trauma, such as a yakuza boss who appears as a boy inside a robot and a schoolgirl made of symbols. Each time he "resolves" someone's trauma, part of their homunculus appears on his own body. The story gradually turns inward: the homunculi may be projections of Nakoshi's own mind, and his own lost identity (he has had plastic surgery and cannot remember his old face) becomes the real subject (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; Penguin Random House; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**The series does not hold up a model of a good life. It shows people hiding guilt and trauma behind masks (money, rank, surgery, lies), and asks whether a person who has no self underneath the mask can be healed at all. Its answer, as summaries read it, is bleak: Nakoshi's search for himself ends in self-mutilation, a woman's death and a world in which every face is his own.**

These are each tradition's views, not the site's. **Grade** measures message fit only: whether the work endorses or merely depicts, whether evil is framed as evil, whether there are consequences and room for redemption, and how the sacred is treated. Intensity alone never lowers a grade. **Depiction concern** is how the tradition views the explicitness itself. On message, Homunculus shows the cost of lies, vanity and self-absorption plainly: Nakoshi's search ends in mutilation, a woman's death and madness, and one character finds peace by confessing and atoning. ## Creator and publisher context

- Hideo Yamamoto (born June 23, 1968, Tokorozawa, Saitama) won the Tetsuya Chiba Award in 1988. Works include Okama Report, Voyeur, Ichi the Killer, Homunculus and Hikari-Man. Wikipedia names his recurring themes as "crime, sexual deviations, and the human mind" (Wikipedia, opened). Ito's stated birthday (June 23, 1981) matches Yamamoto's own birth date (Japanese Wikipedia; English Wikipedia); no source explains this.
- He announced the end of the series on his Twitter account in February 2011 (ANN news, opened).
- **No on-record statement by Yamamoto on the intent, themes, religion or politics of Homunculus was found** in the pages opened. A 2021 Real Sound interview about the film features the director and lead actor, not Yamamoto. This entry says nothing about his private life. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- No widely reported controversy involving the creator was found (not exhaustively searched).
- Publishers: Shogakukan (Japan). Seven Seas Entertainment (English), distributed by Penguin Random House (sell sheet). The 2021 film was produced by Booster Project and distributed by Avex Pictures (Japanese Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is there a real self beneath the masks people wear, and can seeing it heal anyone? Nakoshi is given, or believes he is given, the power to see other people's hidden trauma. The series asks what that power is worth to a man who has no face of his own.

**The answer the story gives.** At first, seeing helps others. The yakuza boss confesses a childhood accident and leaves violence behind; a schoolgirl's homunculus vanishes (Japanese Wikipedia; renote). But each cure lands on Nakoshi's own body, and the story turns: the homunculi may be his own projections. Ito reveals a placebo study, yet Nakoshi names a forgotten event, so the question stays open (honcierge). In the end Nakoshi's search for himself leads to more drilling, a woman's death, and a world where every face is his. Summaries read this as acceptance and total loss at once (ciatr). The story offers no clear healing for the protagonist.

**Characters as arguments.** Nakoshi argues that a self built of money, surgery and lies cannot be found by looking deeper, only lost. Ito argues for science and for the fear of being known; his arc toward self-acceptance (Looper) is the nearest thing to a hopeful counter-argument. The kumicho argues that guilt can be confessed and laid down. Yukari and Nanako argue nothing; they are used, and their suffering is what the reader must weigh.

**Symbols and motifs.** The hole in the skull as a door to the inner world. The left eye and the right eye as two ways of seeing. Homunculi as images of trauma: a boy in a robot, a girl made of symbols, liquid, water with guppies. The faceless or changing face; plastic surgery; Nakoshi's hidden pre-surgery face, kept from the reader until the final volume (TV Tropes). The title comes from alchemy's "little man", and from the neurological homunculus that maps the body in the brain (from general knowledge; to verify).

**Traditions it echoes.** Freudian and Jungian psychoanalysis (Ito explains psychoanalytic theory, per Wikipedia); the long history of trepanation; existentialist fiction on bad faith and the absent self; Buddhist teaching on no-self as some readers may see it (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Science (Ito) is respected but cannot account for everything. The occult is raised and never confirmed. Self-invention through surgery and lies is critiqued. Gender self-acceptance (Ito) is treated with sympathy. Atonement is honored in one side character.

**Shifts across the series.** The early volumes (JP v1-6, Seven Seas omnibus 1-2 and part of 3-4, likely) read as a supernatural case-of-the-week: the trepanation, the yakuza, Yukari. The middle (JP v7-11, omnibus 5-6 and 7-8, likely) turns to Ito's trauma and Nakoshi's face. The last volumes (JP v12-15, omnibus 9-10) turn wholly inward: self-trepanation, Nanami/Nanako and the ending. Honcierge reports that the collected edition adds a "true ending" not in the magazine finale (to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nakoshi sees other people's hidden pain but hides his own. Why might that be easier?
2. The kumicho finds peace by confessing and atoning. What makes his healing different from Nakoshi's?
3. Nakoshi believes he is "saving" Yukari. What does the story show about using someone in the name of helping them?
4. Ito runs an experiment on a person for money. What makes research on people ethical? What does your family's tradition say about the body?
5. Is the ending, where every face is Nakoshi's, a kind of peace or a kind of prison?
