**Premise.** Susumu Nakoshi, 34, is a former foreign-bank employee and a habitual liar who now lives in his car between a luxury hotel and a park full of homeless people in Shinjuku. Manabu Ito, a flamboyant 22-year-old medical student from a wealthy family of doctors, offers him 700,000 yen to undergo trepanation (drilling a hole in the skull), which is said to open a "sixth sense." Nakoshi refuses until his car is towed. Afterward, when he covers his right eye, he sees some people as distorted shapes that Ito names homunculi: pictures of each person's hidden trauma. Nakoshi sets out to "cure" them: a feared yakuza boss who appears as a boy inside a robot, a high-school girl sold at a burusera shop under the number 1775 (Yukari) who appears as a figure of sand and symbols, and others. Each cure passes the trait onto Nakoshi's own body. Over the series his own past comes out: a face changed by plastic surgery, a girlfriend, Nanako, whom he abandoned with their child, and a life built on masks. Ito's own secret, a self he hid from his father, becomes the series' second story. The ending sees Nakoshi, having drilled more holes in his own skull, turn the drill on Ito as police arrive (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia; renote.net; TV Tropes; Looper).

## Life message

**The series does not hold up a model of a good life. It shows people hiding guilt and trauma behind masks (money, rank, surgery, lies) and asks whether a man with no true self under his masks can heal anyone; its answer is bleak, since Nakoshi's "cures" wound others and end in his own ruin, while the one person who finds a real self is Ito.**

Hope source: N — the story finds little hope: Nakoshi's search for a self ends in dissolution; the nearest thing to hope is Ito living openly as herself (S), which Looper calls the arc's resolution.

**Where traditions disagree.** Every frame reads the story's portrait of lies, vanity and trauma as serious, and almost every frame marks the depiction as a significant concern. The split is over how much a bleak, unresolved study of the self is worth to an adult reader. Buddhist readers find the closest echo, since Nakoshi's grasping after a fixed self ends in dissolution, and grade it highest (B). Reformed, Lutheran, Anglican, Catholic-adjacent literary readers and secular frames grade it C or D for its art and honesty but against its framing of the Yukari scene; most other religious frames, Secular progressive and Historically Black Protestant grade it D because that scene is presented as a cure and the series offers no repentance by its protagonist and no hope.

## Creator and publisher context

- Hideo Yamamoto, born 1968 in Tokorozawa, Saitama; Tetsuya Chiba Award for new artists (1988); assistant to Kenshi Hirokane before his 1989 debut. Wikipedia names crime, sexual deviation and the human mind as his recurring themes; Homunculus followed Ichi the Killer and preceded Hikari-Man (Wikipedia, Hideo Yamamoto).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a self, and can anyone see it, including their own? Nakoshi has a hole drilled in his skull and gains a way to see other people's hidden wounds. He has no idea what his own face looked like before surgery. The series asks whether insight into others is possible for a man who will not look at himself, and whether the "sixth sense" is real or only his projection.

**The answer the story gives.** Insight without honesty is just another mask. Each time Nakoshi "cures" someone, their wound lands on his body (PRH Book 3), until in the last volume the only homunculus left is a woman whose face keeps changing and, finally, everyone looks like him (renote.net). The story does not say the visions are false, but it shows a man so curved in on himself that the world becomes a mirror. Its one counter-example is Ito, who stops hiding and lives as herself (Looper).

**Characters as arguments.** Nakoshi argues that a self can be bought, rebuilt or borrowed, and loses. Ito argues for science and then, more deeply, for honesty about who one is; the experiment fails but Ito's own life does not. The yakuza boss argues that guilt can be paid off with violence, then learns to pay it with confession, cutting off his own finger and leaving the gang. Yukari is a girl emptied by her parents' control; what Nakoshi does to her is the series' most troubling scene, and the story's framing of it as a cure is what most readers object to. Ita-san shows guilt with no way out.

**Symbols and motifs.** The hole in the skull and the covered right eye; the car as a home on the border between the hotel and the park; masks and mirrors; sand, robots and water as shapes of trauma; the guppy Ito's father killed (Japanese Wikipedia); faces, surgically changed, drawn over, and finally all the same.

**Traditions it echoes.** The homunculus of alchemy (a little man made by art); Freudian and Jungian depth psychology, which Ito explains on the page; the doppelganger tale. Buddhist readers will hear the teaching that clinging to a fixed self is the root of suffering, though the series offers no path out. Christian readers may think of the heart as deceitful and of the need for a new heart rather than a new face.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and postmodern doubt about identity drive the story; naturalism supplies its explanations (surgery, brain injury, trauma); nihilism is where Nakoshi ends. Expressive individualism is affirmed only in Ito. Transhumanist hopes of upgrading the self through surgery are shown as ruin. The yakuza honor code is exposed as guilt in disguise.

**Shifts across the series.** Omnibus 1-2: the surgery and the first visions, relatively restrained (SKJAM!). Omnibus 3-6: the yakuza boss and Yukari, each "cured" at a cost to Nakoshi. Omnibus 7-8: lies between Nakoshi and Ito come out, and Nakoshi's old face is revealed (PRH). Omnibus 9-10: the shifting-faced woman, self-trepanation and the ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nakoshi can see other people's wounds but not his own. Why might that be?
2. Is Ito's experiment ethical? What would you have needed to know before agreeing to it?
3. The yakuza boss frees himself by confessing. What makes confession work for him when nothing works for Nakoshi?
4. Many readers say the scene with Yukari is the worst part of the book. What does it mean when a story calls harm a cure?
5. Ito is the only character who seems to end better off. What did Ito do differently?
