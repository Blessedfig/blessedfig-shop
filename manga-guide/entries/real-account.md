**Premise.** Orphaned high-school student Ataru Kashiwagi lives in poverty with his younger sister Yuri and prefers his online friends on the social network Real Account to classmates. He and about 10,000 other users are pulled into a virtual version of the app, where the mascot Marble runs death games while the world watches: a player who loses all followers dies, and when a player dies, every follower dies too (Wikipedia; Kodansha blurb). Early games turn on looks, retweets and exposed secrets (ch. 1-10, vols. 1-2). From vol. 3 the lead passes to Ataru's look-alike twin Yuuma Mukai and his partner Ayame Kamijou through a long run of games satirizing online life: ignoring messages, flame wars, collectibles, hide-and-seek with hunters, anonymous classrooms, fake news (vols. 3-23). The family behind the games is revealed: the twins' parents' company built Real Account and a cloning technology; Yuuma and a girl named Koyori turn out to be clones; Ataru kills Koyori to protect Yuri and joins the company as Yuuma's enemy, wearing Marble's mask (fan wiki). The series ends with the site destroyed and its creators dead, a news report of two bullied teenagers' suicides showing the internet's dark side persists, Ataru keeping a distance from his friends, and Yuuma taking a break from the internet while granting it can do good (Wikipedia; vols. 23-24).

## Life message

**Follower counts are a hollow measure of friendship; the people who stand by you in real life, especially family, are the ones worth protecting.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Status games made literal: popularity, looks and approval decide who survives. Family loyalty (Ataru protecting Yuri) is the deepest drive, carried to the point of killing. |
| Pragmatic | Each game is a puzzle won by reading the rules and other players (MangaUpdates Strategic Minds, Mind Games); Ataru first wins by cheating (ch. 2-3, fan wiki). |
| Religious | No religion shapes the plot; a dead character's soul is hinted to live on in her sister in the epilogue bonus pages (fan wiki). Faith readers weigh the critique of vanity and the value of family against the cruelty, the sibling storyline and the killing of a clone. |
| Socio-cultural | A satire of social media: ANN calls its message about follower counts, the shrinking meaning of "friend" and narcissism heavy-handed but valid, and notes it can read as a generational critique. Later arcs take on flaming, doxxing and fake news. |

Outlook: Bittersweet — the deadly site is destroyed and Ataru and Yuri survive, but thousands die, a sister is killed by her brother, and the ending's news of two bullied teens' suicides says the problem continues (Wikipedia).
Hope source: F — family and friends: Ataru's will to protect Yuri and the bonds among Yuuma's group carry the story, and Yuuma ends allowing that the internet can do good (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a friend, if friendship can be counted? Real Account makes follower counts life and death to ask what we owe the people who "follow" us and what we owe ourselves.

**The answer the story gives.** Numbers lie; family and the friends who stay are what matter. ANN sums up the theme as the cheapening of "friend" and the narcissism of chasing approval. The ending keeps some balance: the site is gone, cruelty online continues, and Yuuma allows that the internet can also do good (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Ataru argues for family first, and the story follows that argument to its worst end when he kills one sister to save another. Yuuma argues for wit and real bonds, and that a copy can still be a person. Marble argues that people are content. Aiji argues for cruelty as entertainment, and the series lets him be both villain and ally.

**Symbols and motifs.** The follower count; the mask (Marble's face worn by Ataru); twins and clones as doubles; the smartphone as both lifeline and leash.

**Traditions it echoes.** Death-game manga (ANN compares it to BTOOOM! rather than Sword Art Online), Black Mirror-style media satire (from general knowledge; to verify), and old warnings against vanity and gossip that many traditions share.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Transhumanist technology is shown as dangerous; postmodern instability of truth is the setting of the fake-news arcs; communitarian bonds are affirmed. The sexual content involving minors and the sibling storyline sit outside the series' social message and are what most frames weigh most.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-2: Ataru's games and his secret. Vols. 3-10: Yuuma and Ayame's games and hide-and-seek. Vols. 11-21: tower games, anonymous classrooms, the "symptom." Vols. 22-24: Fake News Island, the family truth (including the flashback flagged above), the final battle and ending.

**Discussion questions.**
1. In the first game, Ataru blocks his sister to save her. Was that love or control?
2. How do you tell a real friend from a follower?
3. The story calls several games "flaming" and "fake news." Have you seen something like that online? What did people do?
4. Yuuma is a clone. Is he still a person with his own life? Why?
5. The ending shows a news report about bullied teens. What could have helped them?
