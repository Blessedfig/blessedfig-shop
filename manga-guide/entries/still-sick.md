**Premise.** Makoto Shimizu is a hard-working engineering team leader at a Tokyo company who secretly draws yuri (girls' love) fan comics and sells them at events. Her coworker Akane Maekawa spots her at a yuri manga exhibition. Makoto fears blackmail, but Akane turns out to have a secret of her own: she was once a popular professional manga artist and stopped drawing after a block she could not get past. Makoto promises to help her draw again (vol. 1; Wikipedia; Okazu). On a company hot-spring trip Makoto confides in a sympathetic coworker over ping pong and Akane overhears; Akane presses Makoto to admit that she has always liked women and hidden it (vol. 2; Okazu). Makoto was bullied as a child over rumors about her sexuality and fears rejection by real women, so she retreats into fiction and fandom (WWAC). In volume 3 Akane's editor pushes her, she works through a long-rooted depression and gets past her block, and both women become honest about their feelings; the series ends with them together, and the after-story shows them living together (Okazu; AniList).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it cost to hide what you love? Makoto is a capable team leader with a secret life drawing girls' love comics; she hides it, and she hides that she likes women. Akane hides a different wound: a career that stalled and a block she cannot get past. The series asks whether two people who each guard a secret can help each other open up.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly, through honesty and practical help. Makoto's promise to help Akane draw again (vol. 1) becomes the frame for their relationship. The turn comes when Akane makes Makoto admit what she has hidden since childhood (vol. 2), and when Akane, pushed by her editor, faces her own depression (vol. 3). Okazu's Erica Friedman summed up the start as "broken woman meets broken woman" (vol. 1 review); by the end, both are mended enough to choose each other.

**Characters as arguments.** Makoto argues that loving something in secret is still loving it, and then learns that being known is better. Akane argues that a failed career can be a closed door; the story answers that a gift can come back with help. The editor stands for honest standards that push a person to grow. The bullies of Makoto's past stand for the fear that kept her silent.

**Symbols and motifs.** The title, "Still Sick," plays on otaku slang for being hopelessly devoted to a hobby, and on wounds not yet healed (my own reading). The drawing desk, the fan-comic table at events, the hot-spring trip where a private conversation is overheard (vol. 2), and the after-story's shared home.

**Traditions it echoes.** The office romance, the "mangaka in a manga" story about the craft, and the yuri tradition of adult women's romance (Okazu's field). Its theme of a talent buried and dug up again echoes the parable of the talents for Christian readers and svadharma for Hindu readers.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins: living openly is the happy ending. Progressivism's acceptance of queer love is taken for granted rather than argued. Secular humanism is the background: people heal each other. Faith traditions are absent; religious readers will find much to admire in the virtues and will weigh the romance by their own teaching.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 is office comedy and fear of exposure. Volume 2 deepens into confession and the hot-spring trip. Volume 3 turns to Akane's depression and her return to professional work, ending with the two together; Okazu notes it became a very different story from where it began.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why do you think Makoto kept her hobby secret at work? Was she right to be careful?
2. Akane could have used Makoto's secret against her. Why didn't she?
3. What helped Akane start drawing again: Makoto, her editor, or her own choice?
4. Makoto was bullied as a child because of rumors. How do old hurts shape the way people act as adults?
