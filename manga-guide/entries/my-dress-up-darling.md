**Premise.** Wakana Gojo, a 15-year-old first-year high school student raised by his grandfather, a maker of traditional hina dolls, wants to become a kashira-shi (a doll-head craftsman). A childhood friend's mockery taught him to hide his hobby, and he has no friends. Marin Kitagawa, a popular, outgoing classmate who loves anime and games, finds him sewing in the home economics room and asks him to make her a cosplay costume (ch. 1-14). Their projects bring in other cosplayers: the sisters Juju (a second-year) and Shinju (a middle-schooler), the cross-dressing university cosplayer Amane, the photographer Suzuka and the prop maker Akira (ch. 15-95). Wakana gains confidence and friends, works with his class at the culture festival (ch. 56-63), and his costume for Marin's "Haniel" cosplay goes viral at Winter Comiket (ch. 96-102). A romance between the two leads is a main thread; they become a couple in ch. 107. In the final chapters Wakana reconciles with the childhood friend who mocked him (ch. 103-109), and an epilogue shows him, as an adult, a successful doll maker married to Marin (ch. 115) (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Square Enix synopses).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when someone stops hiding what they love? Wakana has spent years ashamed of making dolls because a friend called it wrong for a boy.

**The answer the story gives.** Being seen and accepted lets a person grow. Marin's open enthusiasm and her respect for his craft give Wakana courage; the cosplay community gives him friends; and in the end he faces the friend who mocked him and becomes the craftsman he wanted to be (ch. 103-115).

**Characters as arguments.** Wakana argues for patient craft and care. Marin argues that no one should be ashamed of their tastes. Juju argues for perfection and learns to loosen up. Amane argues that costume can express who you are. Nobara stands for the mockery the story wants undone, and is given a chance to make amends. The grandfather argues that mastery is handed down with love.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bisque doll of the title; the doll's face (kashira) that Wakana must learn to make; costumes as transformation; the camera, which critics note sometimes looks at Marin differently than Wakana does (Anime Feminist).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese craftsman-apprentice story and the otaku comedy; for religious readers, the dignity of work; for progressive readers, the challenge to gendered hobbies.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and virtue ethics are affirmed together: self-expression is earned through skill. Progressivism on gender and hobbies is affirmed. Hedonism and consumerism are present but bounded by friendship and work. Feminist critics praise the heroine's confidence and fault the framing.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 (ch. 1-39) form the partnership and the first cosplays; vols. 6-8 (ch. 40-63) bring crossplay and the culture festival; vols. 9-12 (ch. 64-95) widen to photography, sewing culture and group cosplay; vols. 13-15 (ch. 96-115) cover the viral Haniel cosplay, the reconciliation with Nobara and the adult epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did Wakana hide his love of dolls for so long?
2. What did Marin do that helped him stop hiding it?
3. Nobara mocked Wakana as a child. Should he forgive her, and why does it matter that she apologizes?
4. Are some hobbies "for boys" or "for girls"? Who decides?
5. Reviewers say the drawings sometimes focus on Marin's body in a way Wakana does not. Why might that matter?
