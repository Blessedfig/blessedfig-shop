**Premise.** When Hikari Hanazono was six, her father, a carpenter and pro-wrestling fan, introduced her to Kei Takishima, and Kei beat her in a wrestling match: her first defeat. She has sworn to beat him at everything ever since, and follows him to Hakusenkan, an elite private academy that costs her father a great deal. Its top seven students form "Special A," with their own greenhouse clubhouse; Kei is always first and Hikari always second (Wikipedia). Kei has loved Hikari since that first match, because she kept coming back after every loss, and he teases her about always being second; Hikari, dense about romance, does not notice (fan wiki). The S.A friends get their own arcs: Akira and Tadashi, childhood friends who hide their feelings until Akira's parents arrange a marriage meeting for her; the twins Jun and Megumi, whose friend Ryu is like family to them; Sakura, a rich girl who prizes honesty; and Yahiro, a rich heir who plays the villain to protect Akira and is drawn to Megumi (fan wiki; Gaffney, vol. 15). Later arcs bring Finn, a princess raised as a boy because her country's law requires a male heir (from ch. 47), and Iori, a new rival for Hikari (vol. 14-16). Kei's grandfather opposes Hikari; Hikari proposes to Kei (ch. 91), then must choose between family and the school; her friends' farewell turns into one last contest, and in the final chapter Kei proposes and Hikari answers that she will marry him the day she beats him (fan wiki; Gaffney, vols. 16-17).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it effort or gift that makes a person worthy? Hikari has worked for nine years to beat a boy who seems to win without trying (Publishers Weekly). The series asks what winning is for.

**The answer the story gives.** Winning matters less than playing honestly and fully. Hikari never wants a thrown match: when Kei let her win as a child, she wept and begged him to fight at full strength, and that is when he fell in love with her (Gaffney, vol. 17). The final proposal keeps the rule: she will marry him the day she beats him (fan wiki).

**Characters as arguments.** Hikari argues for effort and fair play. Kei argues for devotion, and also shows how devotion can become jealousy (Gaffney, vol. 14; MangaUpdates review). Yahiro shows contempt as a mask for care (fan wiki). Akira and Tadashi show love hidden by fear; Sui shows how comparison with a gifted sibling crushes a child (fan wiki). Kei's grandfather shows grief turned into control, and its healing (vol. 16-17).

**Symbols and motifs.** The rank number (always second); the greenhouse clubhouse; the wrestling match as origin; Hikari's barely edible cooking, which Kei always eats (fan wiki); the last contest at the farewell party.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shojo rivals-to-lovers comedy; Dacey compared the pair to a gender-reversed Annie Oakley and Frank Butler (Wikipedia). Many readers will hear the ethic of doing one's best without clinging to results, found in several traditions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics wins: effort and honesty are what is loved. Traditionalism is gently kept: families are reconciled, marriage is the goal. Feminism is contested: a heroine as driven as the hero who still never wins.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-7 (to ch. 39; the anime's span) are contests, festivals and introductions; volumes 8-13 deepen the side couples and bring Finn (from ch. 47); volumes 14-17 bring Iori, the grandfather, the proposals and the ending (Gaffney; fan wiki).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Hikari hate it when Kei lets her win?
2. Is Kei's jealousy a sign of love, or a problem? What would a respectful boyfriend do?
3. Hikari's father pays a lot for her school. How does she honor that?
4. Akira's and Sakura's parents arrange marriage meetings for them. What do you think of that?
5. Who is more admirable: the person with a natural gift or the person who works hard? Why?
