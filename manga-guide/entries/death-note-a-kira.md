**Premise.** In 2013 the death god Ryuk gets back a spare notebook and goes looking for a new human as entertaining as Light Yagami. In 2017 he drops it on Minoru Tanaka, a middle-school boy who tops Japan's intelligence tests three years running but fails ordinary exams. Minoru learns the rules, admires how Kira used them, and judges that in an age of cameras and online tracking no one could repeat Kira's killings. He gives up the notebook, losing his memories of it, and asks Ryuk to come back in two years. In 2019, now in high school and old enough for a bank account, he gets it back and announces his plan: he will not use it but sell it. Ryuk carries signs onto a live Sakura TV broadcast announcing an auction of "the power of Kira" by hashtag. Matsuda storms the studio and is recognized, which convinces the world the offer is real. Near, now acting as L, names the seller "a-Kira" and waits to catch him through the payment, but Minoru routes the money so it lands in the accounts of huge numbers of ordinary Japanese people, and Near admits defeat. The US and China bid highest; the US wins. The King of Death, angered that a notebook is for sale, adds a rule that a human who sells or buys one dies. Ryuk tells the president, who refuses the notebook to save his life but announces that he has it and will never use it. Minoru, who had told Ryuk never to come back, is not warned: he dies of a heart attack after withdrawing his money at the bank. Ryuk wrote his name on the same page as Light's (Wikipedia; fan wiki; Kotaku).

How the money is paid is reported differently: 1 quadrillion yen split among Tokyo residents under 60 (fan wiki), every Japanese citizen under 60 (Wikipedia, Death Note), or US$10 trillion split among Yotsuba Bank account holders, about a million people getting some $10 million each (Kotaku); the Wikipedia Ryuk article gives $100 trillion. To verify on the page.

## Life message

**The chapter shows a world in which the power to kill becomes merchandise: a clever boy who will not kill sells the means to whoever pays most, governments bid while promising not to use it, and the seller dies by a rule he could not foresee. It holds out cleverness and money as hollow answers, and leaves death, and Ryuk's boredom, as the only constants.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the one-shot |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Status and resources replace the main series' contest for dominance: an underachieving boy uses the notebook to gain wealth, not rank, and states compete for an ultimate weapon. There is no romance. Family appears only as Minoru's mother's "apple diet," a joke that keeps Ryuk fed (fan wiki). |
| Pragmatic | Minoru's plan is all means and no ends: he studies the rules, tests how far Ryuk can travel (14 km), avoids the internet, waits until he can open a bank account, and hides his share by paying everyone (fan wiki). When the US and China say they would use the power for good, he says he does not care why they want it, only what they will pay (fan wiki). The plan works and then fails to a rule change he could not predict. |
| Religious | Minoru says ethics class taught that Kira was evil and history class that he was a mass murderer, yet most people still see Kira as good (fan wiki). After the sale Japan's economy booms and people praise Kira "as a god once more" (fan wiki). The King of Death acts as an arbitrary lawgiver. The main series' rule that notebook users go to neither heaven nor hell stands in the background (main entry). |
| Socio-cultural | A satire of world politics, surveillance and social media: an auction by hashtag, world leaders who look like real ones (a US president drawn like Donald Trump and a Chinese leader like Xi Jinping, per Kotaku), a president who values his life over his country's use of the weapon and lies for reputation (fan wiki), and a public that cheers whoever brings money. The Mary Sue reviewer reads the collection alongside COVID-era attitudes to the deaths of the elderly (in "C-Kira," not this chapter). |

Outlook: Bleak — the clever protagonist dies the instant his plan succeeds, the governments are shown eager to buy the power to kill, the notebook returns to a bored death god, and the public again hails Kira as a god.
Hope source: N — the chapter offers no way through: Minoru's cleverness, the money he spreads and the president's caution all serve self-interest, and the only lasting gainer is Ryuk, who still longs for a human to entertain him (Wikipedia; fan wiki).

## Creator and publisher context

- **Release.** Ohba and Obata reunited for the chapter while both were working on Platinum End (Jump Square, 2015-2021). It was announced in the June 2019 Jump Square and part of the manuscript was shown at Obata's 30th-anniversary exhibition "Never Complete" (July 13 to August 12, 2019) before publication on February 4, 2020, the same day VIZ put it online in English (Wikipedia; fan wiki).
- **Creator statements about this chapter.** None found on pages opened. No interview about the chapter's intent or its depiction of a US president was found in sources opened; Kotaku's report quotes none, and ANN's 2019 announcement item has no creator quotes, only Jump SQ's tease that the story concerns what death gods bring about when they come to Earth (ANN). **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- **Ohba's privacy.** "Tsugumi Ohba" is a pen name and Ohba's identity is "a closely guarded secret" (Wikipedia). Speculation about who Ohba is is not repeated here. Ohba has said he likes comedy films best and admires Akira Kurosawa and Charlie Chaplin (Wikipedia).
- **Obata.** His main later series are Platinum End (with Ohba, 2015-2021) and Show-ha Shoten! (with Akinari Asakura, 2021-2025) (Wikipedia). For his 2006 arrest over a knife in his car, see the main entry.
- **On the depicted leaders.** The chapter does not name the US president or the Chinese leader; news outlets identified them as Donald Trump and Xi Jinping from the art (Kotaku). The chapter was published in Japan in February 2020, during Trump's first term.
- **Publisher.** Shueisha (Jump Square); VIZ Media in English, which rates the collection Teen Plus. VIZ is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you held a weapon that could kill anyone, and you would not use it yourself, would it be right to sell it? The main series asked whether one person may judge others. This chapter, set about ten years later, asks what happens when that power is treated as property.

**The answer the story gives.** Minoru is not Light. He has learned in school that Kira was a murderer, he admires Kira's cleverness, and he judges that modern surveillance would catch anyone who tried the same thing (fan wiki). So he does what a market would do: he sells. When the American and Chinese leaders promise to use the power for good, he says he does not care why they want it, only how much they will pay (fan wiki). The plan is perfect, the money arrives, Near gives up. Then the King of Death declares that anyone who sells or buys a notebook dies, and Minoru, who told Ryuk never to come back, dies at the bank (Wikipedia; fan wiki). The answer is a dark joke: you cannot sell death without paying with it.

**Characters as arguments.** Minoru argues for cleverness without a cause: he sees through everyone and believes in nothing. Beneath the Tangles' reviewer sums him up: he "clearly does not want to kill," "seems to just want to survive," and uses the notebook "as a tool for survival without even truly using said tool." The US president argues for the instinct of power to protect itself: he bids, promises restraint, then refuses the notebook and pretends to hold it (fan wiki). Near is the law's argument, and it loses for the first time. Ryuk is the reader's double, watching for fun.

**Symbols and motifs.** Apples again (Minoru's mother is on an "apple diet"). The live TV sign and the hashtag #POWEROFKIRA put a supernatural weapon into a modern media loop (fan wiki). The bank, where the money becomes Minoru's, is where he dies. The same notebook page holds Light's name and Minoru's.

**Traditions it echoes.** The fable of the deal with death and the trickster outwitted by a higher rule; satire of Cold War arms races; the Japanese tradition of political caricature in manga. Kotaku and other outlets read it mainly as a Trump story; The Mary Sue reads the volume as a commentary on how governments and societies value life.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism is shown at full strength and then shown to be blind. Consumerism and might-makes-right are mocked through the auction. Nihilism hangs over the ending through Ryuk. Religious frames will notice that the only judge in the story is the King of Death, whose rule arrives like a verdict, and that people again call Kira a god.

**Shifts across the series.** The 2003 pilot, the 2006 ending and the 2008 "C-Kira" chapter all ask who may use the notebook to kill. "a-Kira" moves the question from personal morality to markets and nations, and, unlike Light, its protagonist kills no one except, indirectly, himself.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Minoru says he will not kill anyone. Is selling the notebook better, worse or the same as using it?
2. Why did Minoru not care what the buyers planned to do with the power? Should he have?
3. The leaders promise never to use the notebook. Do you believe them? Why does the president pretend to have it?
4. Minoru's school taught that Kira was evil, yet many people still saw him as good. Why might that be?
5. Ryuk could have warned Minoru but did not. What does the ending say about trying to profit from something deadly?
