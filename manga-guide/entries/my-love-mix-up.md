**Premise.** Sota Aoki, a kind second-year high school boy, has a crush on Mio Hashimoto, who sits next to him. When she lends him her eraser he sees another boy's name, "Ida," written on it with a heart (a good-luck charm for a secret crush) and assumes she likes his classmate Kosuke Ida. Ida, a volleyball player who knows little about romance, sees Aoki holding the eraser and concludes that Aoki has a crush on him. To keep Hashimoto's secret, Aoki does not correct him (Wikipedia; VIZ vol. 1). By volume 2 Aoki finds that he really does have feelings for Ida; in volume 3 Ida learns the truth about the eraser from Aoki's best friend Hayato "Akkun" Aida, so Aoki has to confess again, honestly (VIZ vols. 2-3). The eraser had in fact been meant for Akkun: Hashimoto likes Aida, not Ida (Japanese Wikipedia, drama cast notes; that the name was partly rubbed off, giving the title, is from general knowledge; to verify). In volume 4 Ida suggests they try dating, and tells all their friends. The rest follows the couple through cram school, a part-time job, college plans (Ida hopes to go to Kyoto), the cultural festival, meeting parents and entrance exams, alongside a second couple, Akkun and Hashimoto, up to graduation (VIZ vols. 4-9).

## Creator and publisher context

- Wataru Hinekure (story): VIZ calls *My Love Mix-Up!* Hinekure's first work. Japanese Wikipedia reports that Hinekure wrote the story as boys' love as an amateur; the serialized version does not name a genre, and the series was first planned as five chapters and a single volume.
- Aruko (art): born in Ishikawa Prefecture; debuted in Bessatsu Margaret in 1999; drew *Yasuko to Kenji* (2005-2006) and *My Love Story!!* (2011-2016, written by Kazune Kawahara), which won the Kodansha (2013) and Shogakukan (2016) manga awards. *My Love Mix-Up!* gave her a second Shogakukan award (2022) (Wikipedia, Aruko).
- Editorial context (Japanese Wikipedia, citing a livedoor interview): the magazine's editor at first thought a story centered on two high school boys might suit another venue better; the editor-in-chief supported running it in Bessatsu Margaret because it is the story of someone in love with all his heart.
- No other statements by the creators on the story's themes, religion or politics were found in sources opened. No controversies involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when a kind lie meets a real feeling? Aoki lets a misunderstanding stand to protect a girl's secret, and in living inside that misunderstanding he discovers that he has fallen for the boy he was fooling. The series asks how people come to know their own hearts, and what honesty requires once they do.

**The answer the story gives.** Feelings can surprise you, and the right response is to tell the truth kindly. Aoki's second confession (vol. 3-4) is the turning point: the first was a cover story, the second is honest. After that the story becomes a patient, everyday romance: dates, jobs, exams, parents, the future (vol. 5-9).

**Characters as arguments.** Aoki argues for kindness that must learn to include honesty. Ida argues for openness: he is the one who suggests dating and who tells their friends (vol. 4). Hashimoto shows how shyness and secrecy start the confusion. Akkun, the loyal friend, is the one who brings the truth into the open (vol. 3). The cram-school instructor who coaches Aoki on love (vol. 5) plays the older mentor.

**Symbols and motifs.** The eraser, a charm for a secret love, which erases and is partly erased (the Japanese title means "the vanished first love"). Exchanged seats and misread signs. The fireworks and the shrine visit as markers of seasons passing toward graduation.

**Traditions it echoes.** The comedy of mistaken identity (Shakespeare's comedies, classic shojo romance) and the Japanese boys' love tradition, here placed in a mainstream girls' magazine and told without sexual content. Its moral core (truthfulness, loyalty, kindness) is shared by every tradition in the frames; its view of same-sex love is shared by some and not others.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Progressivism and expressive individualism are affirmed: a boy's same-sex love is treated as normal and good, and the friends accept it. Communitarianism is affirmed in the circle of friends. No lens is set up as a villain; the only antagonists are embarrassment, secrecy and (in tagged scenes not located) prejudice.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 (ch. 1-13) are the comedy of misunderstanding and Aoki's realization. Volume 4 (ch. 14-17) is the honest confession and the start of dating. Volumes 5-8 (ch. 18-32) are the everyday romance and the second couple. Volume 9 (ch. 33-35) is exams and graduation.
