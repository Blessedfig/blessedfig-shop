**Premise.** A loose, surreal retelling of Stoker's Dracula with the main characters made into teenagers. It opens aboard the Demeter, where the crew is consumed by a plant-like contagion from Dracula's crates of earth (vol. 1). At Whitby School, Mina Murray, a working-class girl from Lancashire and a catch-wrestler, is the first and only female student, admitted on merit; she is mocked and made to clean by the boys: the noble Arthur Holmwood, Quincey Morris, an African American from Texas who leads the bullying, and Joe Suwa, a Japanese photographer who keeps a chained "nun," Renfield, in his room. Her friend is Luke Westenra, a frail boy who becomes Lucy at night and is friendly with Mina only then (Wikipedia; Taliesin; Epicstream). Dracula comes for Lucy; Professor Van Helsing arrives from Holland (vol. 2); the students hold Dracula and his wolves off with a hymn, but Lucy dies and returns as a vampire with an insect-like body (vol. 3). Vol. 4 goes back to Jonathan Harker, Mina's childhood friend, a boy in a wheelchair sent by the headmaster to Castle Dracula, where he is tormented by the Count and his brides. In vol. 5 Mina learns Jonathan's fate through a psychic link, the group (the Camellia Club) gives Lucy a true death, Renfield is revealed as a mechanical karakuri doll, and the hunt moves to Whitechapel; in vol. 6 madness spreads and Mina's typed records become the weapon that turns the hunters loose (VIZ blurbs; Taliesin).

## Life message

**Fear, prejudice and contagion pass through every rank of society, and they can only be resisted by people who cross the lines of sex, race and class and keep a true record together; a girl the world refuses to see becomes the one who holds the truth.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Shin-ichi Sakamoto, born 1972 in Osaka; debuted at 18 in 1990; self-taught; his career turned with The Climber (2007-2012), when he began putting his own life and thinking into his work; Innocent and Innocent Rouge followed (Wikipedia). See this site's The Climber and Innocent Rouge entries.
- On-record intent (ANN interview with Christopher Farris): he had long wanted to draw horror; rereading Stoker, he found the novel's Dracula different from the popular image and its concerns still relevant; he said, "it is my mission to revive this message from Bram Stoker in our time." He sees Mina as a new woman who keeps records by her own will, and the men who protect her as vulnerable. Of Luke/Lucy he says the character crosses lines of gender and attraction and that people live in many different ways; he rejects treating any person as a separate queer category. He draws cruelty with an eye to beauty and leaves out written sound effects.
- Because of the many lives lost in his manga, he visits a shrine to pay his respects each time (Wikipedia). A 2025 exhibition for his 35th anniversary was themed on #DRCL (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shueisha (Grand Jump, a seinen magazine). VIZ Media publishes it under VIZ Signature, rated Mature.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What spreads, and what stops it? In #DRCL the vampire is a contagion that moves regardless of rank, sex or race, while the school that should stand against it is divided by exactly those things. The series asks whether people who despise one another can stand together before the plague reaches them all.

**The answer the story gives.** Only if they cross the lines. Mina, the girl the boys make clean their floors, is the one who sees through Dracula's tricks (vol. 3), who connects with Jonathan's memories and Lucy's mind (vol. 5), and whose typed records become the weapon (vol. 6). Sakamoto calls her a new woman and says his mission is to revive Stoker's message (ANN interview).

**Characters as arguments.** Mina argues for merit and truth. Arthur argues for nobility and is exposed as a coward. Quincey bullies and is bullied, an argument that prejudice runs in every direction. Joe argues for science and machines, and builds a companion out of loneliness. Van Helsing argues for traditional lore and finds some of it fails. Lucy/Luke is the hardest case: a person whose day and night selves differ, loved by Mina at night, whom the Count's infection forces into a monstrous form. Readers disagree on whether that is a sympathetic picture of a trans girl or a troubling one.

**Symbols and motifs.** The hash mark as a record or tag; moss and roots growing through flesh; crates of earth that become the mortar of Whitechapel (vol. 5); a wolf-drawn sleigh over London (vol. 3); Möbius strips and Escher stairs (vol. 4); the cross and the hymn; pomegranates as the Count's substitute for blood.

**Traditions it echoes.** Stoker's Dracula and its epistolary form; Victorian anxieties about plague, empire and the new woman; Christian spiritual warfare; Japanese horror's body transformations; the ensemble boarding-school story.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Feminism and the critique of race and class are affirmed through Mina's eyes. Christianity is shown to have real power against evil, which conservative readers welcome and secular readers note. Progressive gender themes are affirmed in Lucy and the androgynous brides, though one ANN reviewer questions making a gender-variant character a predator. Postmodern play with records and reality runs through the art.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1: the Demeter and Whitby School. Vol. 2: Van Helsing and the Count in human form. Vol. 3: the fall of Lucy. Vol. 4: Jonathan's journey to the castle, the darkest volume. Vol. 5: the true death for Lucy and the move to London. Vol. 6: madness spreads and the hunters turn on the Count. The ending is not yet known.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mina is the only girl at Whitby School. How does the story show what that costs her?
2. Quincey bullies Mina and is insulted for his race. What does that say about how prejudice works?
3. Why do you think the cross and the hymn work against Dracula when garlic does not?
4. Lucy is Luke by day and Lucy by night. How did you read that, and how might your family read it?
5. Why might keeping a true written record be a weapon against evil?
