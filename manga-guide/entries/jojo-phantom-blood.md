**Premise.** In 1880s England, the poor, ambitious Dio Brando is adopted by the wealthy George Joestar, who believes he owes Dio's father a debt. Dio sets out to break George's son Jonathan: he isolates him from friends, forces a kiss on Jonathan's sweetheart Erina, and has Jonathan's dog Danny burned in an incinerator. Seven years later Jonathan learns that Dio is poisoning George. Cornered, Dio uses an ancient Aztec stone mask, activated by George's blood, to become a vampire, declaring "I reject my humanity, JoJo!" (ch. 11). Jonathan, helped by the street tough Speedwagon and the eccentric mentor Will A. Zeppeli, learns Hamon (the Ripple), a sunlight-like energy made by controlled breathing. They fight Dio's zombies, including Jack the Ripper and two undead knights. Zeppeli dies passing his power to Jonathan. Jonathan destroys Dio's body, marries Erina, and on their honeymoon ship meets Dio's surviving head. Jonathan dies holding Dio as the ship explodes, after sending Erina away with an orphaned baby. Erina survives, pregnant with their son (Wikipedia; jojowiki).

## Life message

**A good life is lived as a "gentleman": with courage that knows fear and masters it, loyalty to family and friends, and self-sacrifice for the weak. Being human, with its limits and its death, is better than power gained by giving up one's humanity.**

**Where traditions disagree.** On message, almost every frame sees a clear, traditional moral: pride and the wish to become a god destroy, while humility, courage and sacrifice are honored, so high grades are common. The dividing lines are two. First, the supernatural: vampires, zombies, an Aztec sacrificial mask, a prophecy and breath-based Hamon weigh most for traditions careful about spiritism and the dead (Pentecostal, Adventist, Jehovah's Witness). Second, violence as the hero's tool weighs most for peace churches (Anabaptist). Progressive readings note thin female roles and an ethnic stereotype.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The gentleman who keeps his humanity dies but wins the future; the man who rejects his humanity for power loses his body and is sealed under the sea for a century.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** The story condemns Dio, but fans love him. In a 2016 Buzz Plus poll of JoJo fans, Part 1 Dio ranked 1st among the series' final villains (Otaku USA). ANN's Jacob Chapman called him "one of the greatest supervillains ever invented in any medium," and an ANN reader poll (2015) picked him as the anime villain who would make the best real-life friend (both via Wikipedia, Dio Brando). His lines, including "Kono Dio da!" ("It was me, Dio!"), are major memes (Wikipedia; TV Tropes YMMV). TV Tropes records a "Draco in Leather Pants" reading that excuses him by his childhood, which the story itself rejects through Speedwagon. Araki said he wanted Dio to embody the "attractiveness of evil" (Wikipedia). Jonathan, by contrast, is often called bland: Araki himself said he "may be a little on the boring side" (JoJonium vol. 1), and ComicsAlliance called him "a terrible main character." Zeppeli and Speedwagon are among the most loved characters (TV Tropes YMMV).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987; Phantom Blood was its first Part.
- On the theme (Wikipedia, Phantom Blood): Araki described the themes as "being alive" and "an affirmation that humanity is wonderful," meaning people overcome hardship through their own actions. jojowiki quotes him: "the theme of this work is 'living,'" with Jonathan and Dio showing "two ways of living," a battle between "human" and "non-human."
- On good and evil (JoJonium commentary, 2013, jojowiki translation): "Good and evil, white and black—Jonathan and Dio function as symbols and are foils of one another." Jonathan is "a 'symbol of justice,' so he may be a little on the boring side." For Dio he asked, "How far can a man's ambition drive him when he takes it to the ultimate extreme?" He chose the name because it means "god" in Italian.
- On Dio (Wikipedia, Dio Brando): inspired partly by FBI profiles of serial killers and how they manipulate victims; Araki wanted Dio "unapologetically baleful" and to embody the "attractiveness of evil"; the vampire element was added to show a dark world rare in Weekly Shonen Jump at the time. In 2007 he said Dio is "a composed character that could go either way."
- On human strength (ANN interview, 2017): "I believe that people are able to grow by overcoming obstacles through the power of the human spirit and strength," and in his work "people pull through without relying on machines and divine beings to determine fate themselves."
- On craft (Wikipedia): muscular bodies inspired by Schwarzenegger and Stallone; poses by Bernini's Apollo and Daphne; Jonathan's death was "a gamble" so the lineage could pass to a new hero. Readers at first found Jonathan unlikable for losing so often, and Araki was told the series did not fit beside Dragon Ball and Fist of the North Star.
- Later edits: Araki changed Zeppeli's line about having no children in later editions to fit Part 2 (TV Tropes; jojowiki).
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to be human, and is it worth staying human? Araki says the theme is "living," and that Jonathan and Dio show "two ways of living" (jojowiki). One young man will keep his humanity at any cost. The other will give it up for power: "I reject my humanity, JoJo! I will transcend humanity!" (ch. 11).

**The answer the story gives.** The story sides with the human. Jonathan has no great talent at the start; readers in 1987 complained that he kept losing (Wikipedia). He wins by training, by friends and by inheriting what his mentor gives him. Dio gains immortality and loses everything: his body, his freedom, and in the end his rival, whom he cannot help admiring (ch. 42). Jonathan dies, but his son is born and the baby he saved lives (ch. 44). Araki calls this an "affirmation that humanity is wonderful" (Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Jonathan is the argument for the gentleman: courage, honor and kindness even to enemies. Araki admits he is "a little on the boring side" because he is a "symbol of justice" (JoJonium vol. 1). Dio is the argument for ambition without limit. Araki built him from FBI profiles of manipulators and wanted him to carry the "attractiveness of evil" (Wikipedia). His motive is real: a slum childhood and a father who drank away his mother's life (ch. 7). The story lets readers understand him without excusing him; TV Tropes notes that Speedwagon's verdict closes that door. Zeppeli is the teacher who argues for courage: "'Courage' is knowing fear and making that fear your own!" (ch. 22). Speedwagon is the argument that honor can exist anywhere, even on Ogre Street. George shows that keeping faith with a bad man can cost a good man his life.

**Symbols and motifs.** The stone mask: power and deathlessness bought with blood, first in an Aztec sacrifice (ch. 1), then with a father's blood (ch. 11). Sunlight and breath: Hamon is a "sunlight" made by breathing, so ordinary human life itself is what destroys the vampire. The dog Danny: innocence destroyed by spite. The sword Luck and Pluck, inherited from a fallen enemy knight (ch. 30). The ship and the coffin: Jonathan dies in the fire, but life floats away inside the villain's own coffin (ch. 44). Dio's name, "god" in Italian (JoJonium vol. 3).

**Traditions it echoes.** The Victorian gothic of vampires, ruins and London fog (Bram Stoker's Dracula is the obvious ancestor; my own framing). The gentleman ideal of English fiction. The rivalry of two brothers in many scriptures and myths, such as Cain and Abel or Jacob and Esau (my own framing). The martial-arts mentor of kung fu films, which Araki names for Zeppeli (jojowiki). The Faustian bargain: power bought by giving up one's humanity (my own framing).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and tradition are affirmed: the hero lives by a code, marries and leaves an heir. The will to power and the wish to transcend human limits are critiqued through Dio. Humanism, in Araki's sense, frames the whole: people win without divine help (ANN). Religion is mostly absent; its traces (a crucifix, a "god" name, an Aztec rite, a prophecy) serve the horror rather than a message. Progressive critics note what the story leaves thin: its women and its one Asian character.

**Shifts across the series.** Chapters 1-11 (Viz vol. 1) are a family drama of bullying, poison and a stolen inheritance, ending with Dio becoming a vampire. Chapters 12-27 (vol. 2) turn to gothic action: the burning mansion, Zeppeli, Jack the Ripper and Hamon training. Chapters 28-44 (vol. 3) bring the undead knights, Zeppeli's death, the final battle and the ship. The tone moves from realism to supernatural horror, but the moral stays fixed.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Dio says "I reject my humanity" (ch. 11). What would you have to give up to do that? What would you gain?
2. Zeppeli says courage is "knowing fear and making that fear your own" (ch. 22). Is that what courage means to you?
3. Dio grew up poor with an abusive father (ch. 7). Does that explain what he does? Does it excuse it?
4. Jonathan cries for Dio after defeating him (ch. 42). Why might a good person mourn an enemy?
5. Many fans like Dio more than Jonathan. Why do villains often seem more interesting than heroes?
