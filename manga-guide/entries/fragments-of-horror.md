**Premise.** Eight unconnected horror stories, in VIZ order: (1) "Futon": a husband hides under his futon from spirits he began seeing after a one-night stand with a demon woman; the visions come from hallucinogenic mold. (2) "Wooden Spirit" ("Haunted Wood Mansion"): a woman obsessed with a historic wooden house marries the owner to get inside it and merges with it. (3) "Tomio: Red Turtleneck": a cheating boyfriend's fortune-teller lover wants his head and garrotes him with her hair; his girlfriend kills her. (4) "Gentle Goodbye" ("Lingering Farewell"): a wife learns she died years ago and has lived on as an "afterimage" created by her husband's family prayers. (5) "Dissection Girl": a woman who vivisected animals as a child begs a medical student to dissect her. (6) "Blackbird": a harpy-like woman keeps an injured man alive by feeding him his own flesh. (7) "Magami Nanakuse": a cross-dressing male novelist imprisons people to study their quirks. (8) "Whispering Woman": a caretaker whispers instructions to an anxious young woman, keeps doing so after her abusive partner beats her to death, and the young woman kills him (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**No single model of a good life: betrayal, obsession and morbid curiosity draw people into horror, faithless men pay for it, and only one story finds tenderness, in letting the dead say goodbye.**

Outlook: Bleak — most stories end in death, madness, imprisonment or a horror that continues; "Gentle Goodbye" is the one bittersweet exception, and "Whispering Woman" ends with a young woman stronger but a killer.

## Creator and publisher context

- Junji Ito (born July 31, 1963, Nakatsugawa, Gifu) read Kazuo Umezu as a child, worked as a dental technician, and won an honorable mention in the Umezu Prize in 1987 with the first Tomie story (Wikipedia, Junji Ito).
- On-record (afterword, via Wikipedia): before this book he had not drawn horror for eight years and felt the time had been strangely empty; his editor worried his "instincts for horror hadn't returned," and Ito rewrote the first story but still felt it was below his usual quality.
- Ito names Umezu, Shigeru Mizuki, Hideshi Hino, Edogawa Ranpo and Lovecraft among his influences (Wikipedia).
- No statements on religion or politics found; The Guardian notes he generally avoids political themes (via Wikipedia).
- Publisher: The Asahi Shimbun Company (Nemuki+). VIZ Media (VIZ Signature) in English, rated T+.
- No controversies involving the creator were found. McCulloch's criticism of story 7 is a critical reading, not a public controversy.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when desire, curiosity or love goes past its limit? Each story takes an ordinary wish (to be loved, to own a beautiful house, to know what is inside a body, to keep a dead wife a little longer) and follows it into horror.

**The answer the story gives.** There is no single answer. Several stories punish faithless men (1, 3) and obsession (2, 7); one finds grace in a slow goodbye (4); others are pure dread with no lesson (5, 6). The book's worldview, if it has one, is that the familiar can turn monstrous at any moment.

**Characters as arguments.** Tomio argues for desire without fidelity and is tormented. Manami argues for desire without limits and wins, monstrously. Riko argues for love that lets go. Nanakuse argues that art justifies cruelty, and the story lets him win, which McCulloch reads as Ito joking about himself. Mitsu argues for devoted care, and the ending asks whether that devotion became something darker when Mayumi kills at her prompting.

**Symbols and motifs.** The futon as a shelter that rots; eyes in the floorboards; hair as a garrote; the afterimage that fades over twenty years; the cadaver full of animals; the black bird; the glass case where a victim is displayed; whispering in the ear.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese ghost stories (kaidan), ero guro (The Guardian, via Wikipedia), Lovecraftian cosmic horror (Fangoria, via Wikipedia), and Ito's own heroes Umezu and Hino.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult is horror furniture, not belief. Feminist themes are visible but possibly cynical (McCulloch). Nihilism and absurdity dominate; traditional family bonds are the setting for both horror (2) and tenderness (4).

**Shifts across the series.** A single volume: stories 1-3 are about lust and betrayal; story 4 is a gentle ghost story; stories 5-7 are about curiosity and obsession; story 8, older than the rest (2009), is about care, abuse and revenge.

**Discussion questions.**
1. In "Gentle Goodbye," the family keeps a dead wife among them for years. Is that kind or cruel?
2. Several stories punish men who cheat. Do you think the book is making a point, or just using it for scares?
3. Mayumi kills her caretaker's abuser. Does the story want us to approve? Do you?
4. Why do you think people enjoy being frightened by stories like these?
5. Which story stayed with you, and what was it really about underneath the horror?
