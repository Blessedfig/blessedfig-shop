**Premise.** Kinjiro "Jiro" Sakamachi, a 16-17-year-old second-year high school student, has gynophobia: a girl's touch gives him nosebleeds, because his pro-wrestler mother and his little sister Kureha used him as a practice dummy and stopped only when he bled (Wikipedia; Manga Bookshelf). In the boys' restroom he discovers that Subaru Konoe, the school's most admired "prince" and butler to the principal's daughter Kanade Suzutsuki, is a girl: her family has served the Suzutsukis as butlers for generations, and she must pass as a boy or lose her post. Kanade, sweet in public and gleefully sadistic in private, blackmails Jiro into keeping the secret and offers to "cure" him. Comic schemes follow: fake kidnappings, a beach trip, a school festival, a sports festival, Kanade's self-hypnosis into an eight-year-old mind (v5-6), and fan-club members (Masamune Usami, Nakuru Narumi). Subaru, Kanade and Masamune all fall for Jiro. In the final volume Jiro, cured, confesses to Subaru; she reveals before the whole school that she is a girl; he proposes, becomes a trainee butler, and they are engaged after a fight with her father (v7, ch. 35-39) (Wikipedia chapter list; Seven Seas).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can you be loved for who you really are? Subaru hides that she is a girl; Jiro hides that he is afraid of girls; Kanade hides her cruelty, and later her love, behind a polite mask; Masamune hides her loneliness behind toughness (Wikipedia). The comedy is built on keeping secrets; the story asks what happens when they come out.

**The answer the story gives.** Secrets kept for a friend are a sign of loyalty, but secrets kept from yourself make you miserable. In the last volume each character tells the truth: Jiro confesses, Subaru reveals herself to the whole school, Kanade admits her love and lets go, Masamune accepts being a friend (v7). Honesty is rewarded with a home together.

**Characters as arguments.** Jiro argues for loyalty without self-interest: he keeps Subaru's secret before he loves her. Kanade argues that love can look like torment, and the story half agrees with her. Nagare stands for tradition and a father's control, softened in the end by Jiro's grit.

**Symbols and motifs.** Nosebleeds as the body's defense against fear; the butler's suit as both duty and disguise; the stuffed sheep prize (v4); stargazing as the night of honesty (v7, ch. 35); the final book title, "The Stray Butler and the Chicken," which names the series.

**Traditions it echoes.** The cross-dressing comedy (from Twelfth Night to Ouran High School Host Club), the harem romantic comedy, and the butler fantasy. Its humor about bodies and phobias follows ecchi comedy conventions.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism wins when Subaru drops the disguise; traditionalism is kept in a new form, since she remains in service and Jiro joins it. Communitarianism closes the story. The fan-service comedy follows a hedonist logic the ending does not adopt.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3 set up the secret, the blackmail and the first rivals; volumes 4-5 are summer-vacation and festival episodes with the heaviest fan service; volumes 6-7 turn to confessions, rejection and the engagement.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does Subaru hide who she is? Would you have kept her secret?
2. Kanade says she is helping Jiro, but she enjoys his suffering. Is that friendship?
3. Masamune was lonely enough to think about dying. What did Jiro do right?
4. Many jokes depend on embarrassing the girls' bodies. How do you feel about that kind of humor?
5. What does Jiro choose at the end, and what does it cost the others?
