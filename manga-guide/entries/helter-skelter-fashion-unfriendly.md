**Premise.** Liliko is Japan's top model, actress and singer, and almost nothing about her is natural: an underground clinic has remade her whole body through surgery and experimental drug therapy, arranged by her agency's president, Hiroko Tada, whom she calls "Mama" (Wikipedia; ANN). The treatments are failing; bruises and patches of decay appear, and Liliko's mind frays with her body. She lashes out at everyone, and above all at her overworked assistant, Michiko Hada, whom she draws, with Hada's boyfriend Okumura, into a cruel and sexual web of control (Manga Bookshelf; Otaku USA; Library Journal), then directs them to attack a younger rival (Otaku USA). A new teen model, Kozue Yoshikawa, rises with natural beauty and a calm wish for an ordinary life later (Wikipedia; Manga Bookshelf), while an investigator looking into suicides and organ theft linked to the clinic closes in (Wikipedia). Liliko's overweight younger sister Chiharu realizes the sister she knew is gone. When Hada, abandoned, sends evidence to the tabloids, Liliko injures her own eye with a knife at a press conference and vanishes. Five years later Kozue is the top model, the agency has closed, and Liliko, wearing an eyepatch, is about to perform at a club known for "strange shows" in Mexico (Wikipedia).

## Life message

**A life built on manufactured beauty and the public's gaze consumes itself and the people around it; the story offers this as a warning, and it spreads the blame to the industry and the audience, not only to Liliko.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Beauty as status and survival in a market of youth: Liliko fights aging and younger rivals as if her life depends on it, and in her world it does. Kozue's natural beauty and Chiharu's natural weight loss are the contrast (Wikipedia). |
| Pragmatic | Everyone uses Liliko for what she can provide (The Slings and Arrows): the agency for money, the clinic for experiments, the public for spectacle. Kozue's plan to step away while she can is the only practical wisdom shown. |
| Religious | No religion appears in sources opened. Faith readers will see vanity, idolatry of the image and the body treated as a product, and a fall with no repentance; Silverman (ANN) reads it as a Frankenstein story. |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of the beauty and celebrity industries and of the audience that feeds them: Silverman (ANN) says Okazaki implicates readers in creating Liliko; Michelle Smith (Manga Bookshelf) reads it as a criticism of disposable, commodified celebrity. Okazaki is credited with capturing 1990s Tokyo (Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bleak — Liliko maims herself and disappears, Hada is ruined, the agency collapses, and the epilogue finds Liliko performing in a club of "strange shows"; only Kozue and Chiharu walk away whole.
Hope source: N — the story finds little hope for its heroine; what hope there is lies in stepping away from the gaze (Kozue) and choosing a natural path (Chiharu), close to S, being true to yourself.

## Creator and publisher context

- Kyoko Okazaki, born 1963 in Tokyo, debuted in 1983 in an adult manga magazine while at Atomi University; from the 1990s she worked mainly for magazines for adult women (Feel Young, Young Rose) and for the teen fashion magazine CUTIE (Wikipedia).
- Her work broke with 1970s shōjo norms by depicting female sexuality; she is seen as a forerunner of the gyaru style and credited with capturing Tokyo's shift from the bubble years to the 1990s downturn (Wikipedia).
- In 1996 she was seriously injured in a traffic accident and took a long break for medical treatment; she has not published new work since, and Helter Skelter was collected only in 2003 (Wikipedia).
- Publisher: Shodensha (Feel Young, a josei magazine). Vertical published it in English in 2013 as part of a new women's comics line (Penguin Australia blurb).
- No statements of intent by Okazaki were found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's stated intent.** No controversies were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens to a person whose whole self is an image built for others? Liliko is beauty manufactured to order, and the book asks who made her: the clinic, the agency, Liliko herself, or the audience who wanted her.

**The answer the story gives.** All of them. Silverman (ANN) argues that Okazaki implicates the reader, whose appetite for flawless faces creates people like Liliko. Liliko's body fails, her cruelty spreads to everyone near her, and she ends by destroying her own face in public (Wikipedia). The book avoids simple retribution (The Slings and Arrows): no one is redeemed, and the epilogue finds her still performing.

**Characters as arguments.** Liliko argues that beauty is power and that being sellable is being alive (Manga Bookshelf). Tada argues that a person is an investment. Hada shows how dependence becomes complicity. Kozue argues for a career held lightly and an ordinary life after it. Chiharu argues for honest, natural change. The investigator argues that the hidden costs of beauty must come to light.

**Symbols and motifs.** Bruises and flaking skin under makeup; the photograph of the young Liliko shown to the investigator (Wikipedia); the press-conference eye; the eyepatch; the club of "strange shows." The Frankenstein story (ANN).

**Traditions it echoes.** Frankenstein and The Picture of Dorian Gray (my own comparison for the second); Japanese josei realism; the vanitas theme of Ecclesiastes and Buddhist impermanence for religious readers.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Consumerism and hedonism are critiqued; feminism and critical theory supply the angle of critique; the remade body (transhumanism) fails; no faith or community offers a way out.

**Shifts across the series.** Early chapters show Liliko at the top and the first signs of decay; the middle chapters bring her cruelty and sexual control over Hada and Okumura (ANN); the late chapters bring exposure, the press conference and the five-year epilogue (Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Who made Liliko into what she is? Make a list, and decide whether the reader belongs on it.
2. Why does Kozue want an ordinary life after modeling? What does she understand that Liliko does not?
3. Chiharu is told to exercise and eat well instead of having surgery. How does her story answer Liliko's?
4. Hada helps Liliko hurt people. Is she a victim, an accomplice or both?
5. What do magazines, ads and social media today ask of people's bodies?
