**Premise.** Teppei Sasaki, 24, wants a series in Weekly Shonen Jump, but his editor keeps rejecting his work and asks whether he has anything only he could draw. As he is about to give up, lightning strikes his apartment and fuses his microwave into a time machine that delivers an issue of Jump from ten years in the future. He is overwhelmed by a new series in it, White Knight by Itsuki Aino, then loses the issue and decides he dreamed it. Believing the idea is his own, he draws it as a one-shot, and the editor-in-chief offers him a series. Back home he finds a stack of future issues and realizes he has copied another artist's work (ch. 1). He tries to back out, but decides to keep publishing White Knight for the readers waiting for it and to atone for taking its birth away from its real author (Japanese Wikipedia). That author, high-school student Itsuki Aino, comes to Tokyo, confronts him, and then becomes his assistant while developing her own series (ch. 2-7). A future issue then carries Aino's death notice, and the machine asks Teppei to save her (end of vol. 1). In vol. 2 her own series overtakes White Knight, and in the future she dies from overwork because she refuses to use assistants. In a pocket of stopped time Teppei spends 12,472 days redrawing White Knight 18 times to make it strong enough to beat her (Japanese Wikipedia). In the epilogue he has persuaded Aino not to overwork, ends White Knight, and starts series of his own, which are cancelled quickly but are his (TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Work you make yourself is worth more than success borrowed from someone else, and no dream is worth working yourself to death; owning up to what is not yours, and caring for the person behind the work, come first.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a work yours, and what do you owe the person whose idea you used? Teppei did not mean to steal: he thought he had dreamed White Knight. Once he knows the truth, the real question becomes whether a good result (fans get the story, the timeline is protected) can justify keeping it.

**The answer the story gives.** No, though it takes the whole series to get there. Teppei keeps publishing out of a mix of duty and excuse, carries heavy guilt, and in the end gives the series up. The epilogue's lesson is that honest failure with your own work beats success with someone else's (TV Tropes).

**Characters as arguments.** Teppei argues for effort without talent, and against shortcuts. Aino argues for pure talent and the demand that work be one's own, but also shows the danger of a gift pursued without rest or help: she dies from overwork in one future. The editors stand for an industry that rewards hits and asks for originality. The machine stands for a fate that wants Aino saved and never explains why.

**Symbols and motifs.** The microwave as a time machine (a nod to Steins;Gate, per Real Sound and the AniList reviewer); the stack of future magazines; the blank page; stopped time, where Teppei redraws one story 18 times over 12,472 days.

**Traditions it echoes.** Bakuman (manga artists in Jump) and time-loop stories; the commandment against stealing and the Jewish teaching that one should cite the source of what one says; the Bhagavad Gita's preference for one's own duty imperfectly done.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and duty ethics win; utilitarian excuse loses. Existentialist authenticity (find what only you can make) is the goal. Critical theory appears only as an implied critique of overwork.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-7) is the theft, the meeting with Aino and the death notice. Vol. 2 (ch. 8-14 plus a bonus) turns to saving her: the rivalry, stopped time and the epilogue. The fast pace came from the series being cut short (AniList user review).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Teppei thought he had dreamed White Knight. When did using it become wrong?
2. He kept publishing it for the fans and to keep the timeline on track. Is that a good reason?
3. Aino refused help and worked herself to death in one future. Why can talent or a dream be dangerous?
4. What would making amends to Aino have looked like?
5. Is it better to fail with your own work than to succeed with someone else's?
