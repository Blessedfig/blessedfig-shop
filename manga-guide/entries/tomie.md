**Premise.** Tomie Kawakami is a strikingly beautiful girl with long black hair and a beauty mark under her eye. Men who meet her fall in love with her, grow jealous and obsessive, and in the end kill her, often by cutting her apart. She does not stay dead. Every piece of her can grow into a new Tomie, and fire that burns her flesh to carbon is the only known way to end her. The first story begins with her death on a school trip; she then walks back into class as if nothing happened. Later stories follow new Tomies through new victims: classmates, a painter, a model, brothers, a husband, children. She is cruel on purpose and calls her admirers "accessories." Her origin is never explained (Wikipedia; Wikipedia character page; fan wiki, all opened).

## Life message

**The story promotes no good life. It shows desire for beauty and possession as a force that turns people into murderers, and it offers no cure; the few who survive are those who refuse to be drawn in.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Tomie is mate competition in its most extreme form: men fight and kill rivals for her, and women are driven to envy and madness (Wikipedia). She reproduces by budding from her own flesh (Wikipedia), so she needs no partner at all; the men's desire is pure waste. Read this way, the series is about the costs of sexual rivalry. |
| Pragmatic | Nothing works except staying away. Killing her multiplies her; hiding her remains (in sake vats in "Moromi," TV Tropes; in cement, Wikipedia) spreads her. Only full burning ends one copy (Wikipedia). The practical lesson is avoidance. |
| Religious | No gods, priests or prayers matter to the plot. The VIZ release calls her "an immortal succubus from the netherworld" (opened), language borrowed from demonology. Christian readers may see lust, envy and wrath (three of the seven deadly sins) at work; Buddhist readers may see craving as the root of suffering (both are readings, not the text's claims). |
| Socio-cultural | Set in ordinary Japanese schools, homes and small businesses. Scholars quoted on Wikipedia (Taylor, Creed, Pinedo, Dumas) read it through gender: male possessiveness and violence against women. Anime Feminist (opened) argues the stories blame the woman for the violence men do to her. The magazine it ran in was aimed at girls (Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Bleak — Tomie can never be fully destroyed, almost every relationship she touches ends in murder, madness or suicide, and each story closes with a new Tomie starting the cycle again.
Hope source: N — the story holds out no real way through; the most it offers is that a few people, such as Tsukiko in "Photo" and "Kiss," survive by refusing Tomie's pull (Anime Feminist).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when desire meets something it can never own? Tomie is beautiful, and everyone who sees her wants her. She is also cruel, and she refuses to belong to anyone. The series asks what that collision does to ordinary people.

**The answer the story gives.** It turns them into killers. In the first story a 15-year-old girl who has been having a sexual relationship with her married teacher is hit by her boyfriend, falls from a cliff, and is cut apart by her class with the teacher leading (fan wiki; Anime Feminist). She returns to school the next day. Every later story repeats the pattern with new people: a painter who wants to capture her face, a model who wants to keep her, brothers who fight over her. Killing her does not end anything. Each piece of her grows into another Tomie (Wikipedia). The story's answer is that possessive desire multiplies the very thing it tries to destroy.

**Characters as arguments.** Tomie is beauty without goodness: she calls her admirers "accessories" (Wikipedia). Ito made her unlikable on purpose (Wikipedia character page). The men are arguments about possession: each believes that if he cannot have her, no one should. Mr. Takagi, the teacher, shows authority turned predatory and then murderous. Tsukiko, the girl who photographs Tomie's true face in "Photo," is the rare person who sees through her and survives; Anime Feminist notes that she also forgives a boy who tried to kill her ("Kiss").

**Symbols and motifs.** Regeneration is the main symbol: a lizard's tail, Ito said (Wikipedia). Every attempt to cut Tomie away creates more of her. The beauty mark under her eye marks every copy. Fire is the only cleanser. Photographs, portraits and mirrors show the gap between how Tomie looks and what she is.

**Traditions it echoes.** The succubus of European demonology (VIZ calls her one), the vengeful female ghost of Japanese folklore, the femme fatale of film noir, and Kazuo Umezu's horror manga (the prize Ito won bears Umezu's name). Readers also hear the seven deadly sins (lust, envy, wrath) and the Buddhist teaching that craving causes suffering. These are readers' comparisons, not the text's claims.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Hedonism and possessive desire are critiqued by their results. Nihilism is close to affirmed: there is no justice, and the cycle never ends. Feminism is the contested lens. Anime Feminist (Barraza, 2019) argues that the stories "justify and disguise their displays of male misogynistic violence" by making Tomie the villain. Taylor, cited on Wikipedia, reads one scene's likeness to a gang rape as a critique of misogynistic violence rather than an endorsement. Both readings point to the same scenes. Religion is almost absent.

**Shifts across the series.** The first stories (1987) are school horror built on a single murder and its aftermath. Later stories widen to adult settings (a painter, a model, a brewery, a marriage) and to stranger body horror: dozens of Tomies in a town, tissue experiments, a child turned into a Tomie by blood injections (TV Tropes; Wikipedia). TV Tropes says Tomie rarely looks older than her teens in the manga, so the sexualized teen image persists across eras. ICv2 says it "may not be the best starting point for new Ito readers."

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does killing Tomie always make more Tomies? What might that say about trying to destroy what we desire?
2. Mr. Takagi is an adult with power over a student. How does the story show what happens when an adult abuses that trust? What does your tradition teach about protecting the young?
3. Anime Feminist says the stories blame Tomie for men's violence; another scholar says they critique that violence. Which reading do you find stronger, and why?
4. Tsukiko survives by seeing Tomie clearly and refusing her pull. What helps a person resist something others cannot?
5. Ito made Tomie unlikable on purpose. Can a story with no good characters still teach something good?
