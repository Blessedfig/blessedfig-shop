**Premise.** Naples, 2001. Fifteen-year-old Giorno Giovanna, the secret son of the vampire DIO (villain of Parts 1 and 3), conceived while DIO wore Jonathan Joestar's body (from general knowledge; to verify), grew up neglected by his mother and beaten by his stepfather (jojowiki). He dreams of becoming a "Gang-Star" so he can take over the mafia gang Passione from within and end its trade of drugs to children. His Stand, Gold Experience, gives life to objects and later heals wounds. He joins the crew of Bruno Bucciarati, a young capo (age 20, from general knowledge; to verify) who hates the drug trade too, alongside Leone Abbacchio, Guido Mista, Narancia Ghirga and Pannacotta Fugo. The faceless Boss orders the crew to guard his 15-year-old daughter Trish from his own assassin squad, then reveals he wants her delivered so he can kill her and erase every trace of his past. Bucciarati turns on the Boss; Fugo stays behind. The crew flees across Italy, from Venice to Sardinia to Rome, fighting the assassins and the Boss's bodyguards. Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die. At the Colosseum Giorno's Stand evolves into Gold Experience Requiem, which traps the Boss, Diavolo, in an endless loop of deaths. Giorno becomes the new boss of Passione (Wikipedia; jojowiki).

## Life message

**You cannot change your fate, but you can refuse to give up your sense of right: loyalty to friends who chose the same path, protection of the weak against those who prey on them, and the courage to walk that road to its end are what make a life golden, even inside a criminal world.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A gang is a coalition with rank, territory and threat, and the heroes survive by trust within a small band. Diavolo kills his own daughter's protectors and tries to kill her to protect his secrecy, which reverses the parental instinct (Wikipedia; jojowiki, Trish Una). The DIO bloodline passes through Giorno, but he chooses the opposite of his father's goals. Romance is almost absent. |
| Pragmatic | Every member was failed by family or society and found a place in a gang (Araki, Bunko vol. 10). Giorno takes power in the organization because power is the only lever that can stop the drug trade; ANN's This Week in Anime: "Diavolo seeks power for its own sake, while Giorno wants power so he can make things right." Fights are won by wit with odd powers. |
| Religious | Little explicit religion. Names and titles echo Catholic Italy: "Diavolo" (devil), "Requiem," the climax at the Colosseum in Rome. Bucciarati keeps fighting as a "walking corpse" after his death and his soul is shown rising at the end (Wikipedia; ANN This Week in Anime). Diavolo's final fate, dying again and again forever, works like a punishment beyond death (ANN: "damning him to an eternity"). Diavolo's own backstory involves the priest who raised him (from general knowledge; to verify). |
| Socio-cultural | A Japanese artist's Italy: the mafia as "the dark underbelly of society," drawn through characters with little choice in life (Araki via Wikipedia). ANN's Andy: "Everyone in this show has been spurned and exploited and abused by those in power." Drugs sold to young people are the central social evil (Wikipedia; jojowiki). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Giorno wins and the drug trade's protector falls, but Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die on the way, and the epilogue says their fates were already written.
Hope source: S — Araki's stated answer is that the heroes "didn't try to change their 'fate'... but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness," so the way through is staying true to one's own sense of right whatever fate brings.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame agrees the drug trade and a father who would kill his child are framed as evil. The dividing line is means: the heroes are criminals, and the happy ending is a 15-year-old taking over a mafia family. Traditions with strong teaching on lawful authority and nonviolence (Anabaptist, Jehovah's Witness, Bahá'í) weigh that most; traditions that read stories by inner intention and duty within fate (Hindu, Sikh, Shinto, Satanist, Secular humanist) weigh it least. Fate itself is a useful talking point for traditions with teaching on providence or decree (Reformed, Muslim, Sikh, Hindu).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987.
- On the Part's theme (Vento Aureo Bunko vol. 10 afterword, August 2005, jojowiki translation): he contrasts Part 3's Jotaro, who stands up to inherited karma, with Part 5's question of what to do when one regrets being born. He asks whether fate can be changed by effort and concludes it cannot, or it would not be called destiny. The protagonists showed him the answer: "They didn't try to change their 'fate' or their 'destiny,' but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness." He writes that all of the crew "grew up shunned by family and society, or were forced to grow up that way," that happiness dwells in "hearts of justice," and he thanks them as "the golden wind that blows in my heart."
- On Fugo (same afterword): Araki planned for one of the four allies to be revealed as a spy and felt it would have been Fugo, but he could not draw it; he was in a dark mood, disliked the direction, and says he "truly hated" his profession at that time. He instead drew the farewell at San Giorgio Maggiore. A later novel (not by Araki) gave Fugo a side story.
- On the mafia (Wikipedia, citing Araki): he described the themes as human relationships, friends versus enemies and "the beauty of betrayal," and portrayed the mafia as the dark underbelly of society, focusing on characters with little choice in life.
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened.
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media publishes the English edition; Viz is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). The anime was made by David Production (Wikipedia).
- Controversies: (1) The English anime's change of "Gang-Star" to "primo mafioso" drew "a small bit of outcry" (TV Tropes). (2) Viz's digital chapters used altered Stand names (jojowiki English chapter list). (3) ANN's Leach raised the casting of gay characters as villains. (4) Some fans object to Trish's design given her age (TV Tropes). No widely reported controversy over the drug or mafia content was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you cannot change your fate, what is left to choose? Araki put the question plainly: what should people do when they regret being born at all? He decided that effort cannot change destiny, "or it wouldn't be called destiny" (Bunko vol. 10). Golden Wind puts that question to a crew of outcasts, most of them teens, who were failed by their parents and their society before the story begins.

**The answer the story gives.** The heroes do not escape fate. Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die, and the epilogue, "Sleeping Slaves" (ch. 151-155), shows that their paths were already set. What the story honors is how they walk those paths. Araki: "They didn't try to change their 'fate' or their 'destiny,' but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness." ANN's Sam Leach puts it another way: "Nah, fate is real and it sucks and you just have to deal with it." The reward is not survival but meaning: dying on the right side.

**Characters as arguments.** Giorno is the argument that power can be taken to do good; he wants to become a "Gang-Star" to end a drug trade that hurts children. Bucciarati is conscience over obedience: once the Boss's orders turn to killing his own daughter, he turns on him. Narancia, a dropout taken in by Bucciarati, is loyalty earned by kindness. Fugo is the prudent choice not to betray the organization, and he leaves the story. Diavolo is the argument for safety through secrecy and total control; ANN's writers say "his entire existence is predicated around living in fear and isolation." Trish begins as a thing to be delivered and becomes a fighter.

**Symbols and motifs.** Gold and life: Gold Experience makes living things. The Arrow and the Requiem: power beyond its owner's control. The faceless Boss and the erased photograph: a man who hides his past. Venice, Sardinia and Rome: a road from the place of betrayal to the place of judgment, the Colosseum. Diavolo's endless deaths (ch. 150): a punishment that never arrives at an end. Bucciarati as the walking dead: a man living on borrowed time for his friends.

**Traditions it echoes.** The gangster film (Leach notes "the most Godfather-y Godfather reference"). Stoic acceptance of fate with virtuous action. The Gita's teaching on duty without attachment to results. Greek tragedy, where fate is fixed and character shows itself in how one meets it. (The Stoic, Gita and tragedy links are my own framing.)

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and loyalty are affirmed. Authoritarian control is critiqued through the Boss. Might makes right is explored but not rejected: power decides the end, and the question is only who holds it. Religion is atmosphere, not argument. Critical readings of institutions appear in reviews (ANN). Gender and sexuality are thin; ANN's Leach flags gay-coded villains.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-33): Giorno joins the gang and proves himself. Volumes 3-5 (ch. 34-87): guarding Trish against the assassin squad. Volumes 5-6 (ch. 77-105): betrayal of the Boss in Venice, Fugo's exit, Trish's awakening. Volumes 7-8 (ch. 106-140): Sardinia and Rome, Abbacchio's death, the Green Day mold, Chariot Requiem, Narancia's death at the Colosseum (ch. 138). Volume 9 (ch. 141-155): Bucciarati's death, Requiem, and the "Sleeping Slaves" epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Giorno joins a gang to stop the drugs the gang sells. Can you change a corrupt group from inside, or does it change you?
2. Bucciarati breaks his loyalty to the Boss to protect Trish. When is it right to disobey someone you have promised to obey?
3. Araki says fate cannot be changed. Do you agree? How does your family talk about destiny, providence or choice?
4. Fugo chooses not to follow. Was he a coward, or prudent?
5. Diavolo's punishment never ends. Is that justice? Who should decide?
