**Premise.** Naples, 2001. Fifteen-year-old Giorno Giovanna, the secret son of the vampire DIO (villain of Parts 1 and 3), grew up neglected by his mother and beaten by his stepfather (jojowiki). In ch. 4 Jotaro reveals that he killed Giorno's father, DIO (jojowiki, ch. 4). Giorno dreams of becoming a "Gang-Star" so he can take over the mafia gang Passione from within and end its trade of drugs to children. His Stand, Gold Experience, gives life to objects and later heals wounds. He joins the crew of Bruno Bucciarati (20), a young capo who hates the drug trade too, alongside Leone Abbacchio (21), Guido Mista (18), Narancia Ghirga (17) and Pannacotta Fugo (16) (ages from jojowiki). The faceless Boss orders the crew to guard his 15-year-old daughter Trish from his own assassin squad, then reveals he wants her delivered so he can kill her and erase every trace of his past. Bucciarati turns on the Boss (ch. 78); Fugo stays behind (ch. 84). The crew flees across Italy, from Venice to Sardinia to Rome, fighting the assassins and the Boss's bodyguards. Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die. At the Colosseum Giorno's Stand evolves into Gold Experience Requiem, which traps the Boss, Diavolo, in an endless loop of deaths (ch. 149). Giorno becomes the new boss of Passione (Wikipedia; jojowiki).

## Life message

**You cannot change your fate, but you can refuse to give up your sense of right: loyalty to friends who chose the same path, protection of the weak against those who prey on them, and the courage to walk that road to its end are what make a life golden, even inside a criminal world.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | A gang is a coalition with rank, territory and threat, and the heroes survive by trust within a small band. Diavolo kills his own daughter's protectors and tries to kill her to protect his secrecy, which reverses the parental instinct; he had earlier buried his own mother under his floor and burned his home village (jojowiki, Diavolo; ch. 130). The DIO bloodline passes through Giorno, but he chooses the opposite of his father's goals. Romance is almost absent. |
| Pragmatic | Every member was failed by family or society and found a place in a gang (Araki, Bunko vol. 10). Giorno takes power in the organization because power is the only lever that can stop the drug trade; ANN's This Week in Anime: "Diavolo seeks power for its own sake, while Giorno wants power so he can make things right." Fights are won by wit with odd powers. |
| Religious | Little explicit religion. Names and titles echo Catholic Italy: "Diavolo" (devil), "Requiem," the climax at the Colosseum in Rome. Diavolo was raised by a village priest, who died when he found Diavolo's buried mother (jojowiki, Diavolo; ch. 130). Bucciarati keeps fighting as a "walking corpse" after his effective death in ch. 83-84, and his soul is shown rising "to Heaven" in ch. 145 (jojowiki). Abbacchio meets his dead partner in an afterlife restaurant (ch. 111). Diavolo's final fate, dying again and again forever, works like a punishment beyond death (ANN: "damning him to an eternity"). Araki says he first imagined Fugo as a traitor like "Judas" in the Bible (strategy guide, 2002). |
| Socio-cultural | A Japanese artist's Italy: the mafia as "the dark underbelly of society," drawn through characters with little choice in life (Araki via Wikipedia). ANN's Andy: "Everyone in this show has been spurned and exploited and abused by those in power." Drugs sold to young people are the central social evil (Wikipedia; jojowiki). Araki says of Narancia that he wanted "to describe the sorrow of living, the fate that is often decided by the favorable or unfavorable context of one's birth" (Lucca Comics, 2019). |

Outlook: Bittersweet — Giorno wins and the drug trade's protector falls, but Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die on the way, and the epilogue says their fates were already written.
Hope source: S — Araki's stated answer is that the heroes "didn't try to change their 'fate' or their 'destiny,' but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness," so the way through is staying true to one's own sense of right whatever fate brings.

**Where traditions disagree.** Almost every frame agrees the drug trade and a father who would kill his child are framed as evil. The dividing line is means: the heroes are criminals, and the happy ending is a 15-year-old taking over a mafia family. Traditions with strong teaching on lawful authority and nonviolence (Anabaptist, Jehovah's Witness, Bahá'í) weigh that most; traditions that read stories by inner intention and duty within fate (Hindu, Sikh, Shinto, Satanist, Secular humanist) weigh it least. Fate itself is a useful talking point for traditions with teaching on providence or decree (Reformed, Muslim, Sikh, Hindu). On depiction, all traditions share a concern about the gags around 15-year-old Trish, though they name it differently (modesty, consent, respect for persons).

## Creator and publisher context

- Hirohiko Araki has created JoJo's Bizarre Adventure since 1987.
- On the Part's theme (Vento Aureo Bunko vol. 10 afterword, August 2005, jojowiki translation): he contrasts Part 3's Jotaro, who stands up to inherited karma, with Part 5's question of what to do when one regrets being born, if fate is "like some kind of law written by God or the movements of the stars." He concludes effort cannot change destiny. The protagonists showed him the answer: "They didn't try to change their 'fate' or their 'destiny,' but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness." He writes that all of the crew "grew up being shunned by their families and by society at large," that happiness dwells in "hearts of justice," and he thanks them as "the golden wind that blows in my heart."
- On censorship and betrayal (Bunko vol. 1 afterword, March 2005): from around 1996 he received many editorial orders to "fix this page," "change that line," under shonen "self-censorship" (which he lists as covering violence, abuse of the weak, nudity, smoking, drinking). He says real evil "sexually harasses others... rapes both women and men," and that he wanted to show evil without holding back, but he stresses he is not criticizing his editors. He felt "a great crisis" over freedom of expression, and says those feelings shaped the heroes: Giorno and Bucciarati betray the organization, "the 'home' that raised them," to "live under justice."
- On Fugo (Bunko vol. 10; GioGio strategy guide interview, 2002; Lucca Comics 2019): Araki planned for one ally to be revealed as a spy; he thought of Fugo as a traitor like "Judas" in the Bible, but "kind of hated it" and felt it would make the story "too dark." At Lucca he said that for Jump's young readers "betrayal is a very heavy and terrible act." He says he "truly hated" his profession at that time, and drew the farewell at San Giorgio Maggiore instead; he asked the authors of the novel Golden Heart, Golden Ring to write Fugo's later story.
- On the mafia and good (2002 interview): asked why a mafia crew sticks to justice, he said "Evil has its charms, as well. But I believe people who are truly good would never enter the world of violence or battle." On Trish, he said earlier manga girls were "the object of boys' admiration," but because girls "became more active in society during the '90s," he was able to draw her.
- On Narancia (Lucca Comics, 2019): "I wanted to describe the sorrow of living, the fate that is often decided by the favorable or unfavorable context of one's birth." On his wider theme: "affirming the human race, without forgetting one's own humanity."
- Wikipedia, citing Araki: he described the themes as human relationships, friends versus enemies and "the beauty of betrayal," and portrayed the mafia as the dark underbelly of society.
- No public statement by Araki about his own religion or politics was found in the pages opened. He names a love of Italy's museums, films and food as the reason for the setting (Lucca 2019).
- Publisher: Shueisha. Viz Media publishes the English edition; Viz is owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify). The anime was made by David Production (Wikipedia).
- Controversies: (1) The English anime's change of "Gang-Star" to "primo mafioso" drew "a small bit of outcry" (TV Tropes). (2) Viz's digital chapters used altered Stand names (jojowiki English chapter list). (3) ANN's Leach and Anime Feminist raised the casting of gay-coded characters as villains. (4) Some fans object to Trish's design and the gags about her given her age (TV Tropes). (5) Araki's own account of editorial self-censorship during serialization (Bunko vol. 1). No widely reported controversy over the drug or mafia content was found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you cannot change your fate, what is left to choose? Araki put the question plainly: what should people do when they regret being born at all? He decided that effort cannot change destiny, "or it wouldn't be called destiny" (Bunko vol. 10). Golden Wind puts that question to a crew of outcasts, most of them teens, who were failed by their parents and their society before the story begins. Araki later said he wanted to show "the sorrow of living, the fate that is often decided by the favorable or unfavorable context of one's birth" (Lucca 2019).

**The answer the story gives.** The heroes do not escape fate. Abbacchio, Narancia and Bucciarati die, and the epilogue, "Sleeping Slaves" (ch. 150-155), shows that their paths were already set; at its start their ghosts tell Giorno they "have won over fate" (jojowiki, ch. 150). What the story honors is how they walk those paths. Araki: "They didn't try to change their 'fate' or their 'destiny,' but refused to abandon their sense of righteousness." ANN's Sam Leach puts it another way: "Nah, fate is real and it sucks and you just have to deal with it." The reward is not survival but meaning: dying on the right side. Abbacchio's dead partner gives the plainest version: he looks not for results but for "the truth" (ch. 111).

**Characters as arguments.** Giorno is the argument that power can be taken to do good; he wants to become a "Gang-Star" to end a drug trade that hurts children. Bucciarati is conscience over obedience: he joined Passione believing it fought drugs, and once the Boss's orders turn to killing his own daughter, he turns on him (ch. 78). Narancia, a dropout taken in by Bucciarati, sees in Trish's wound the pain of being betrayed by those who should love you and swims after the boat (ch. 84). Fugo is the prudent choice not to betray the organization, and he leaves the story. Diavolo is the argument for safety through secrecy and total control; he buried his own mother and burned his village to hide his past (ch. 130), and ANN's writers say "his entire existence is predicated around living in fear and isolation." Trish begins as a thing to be delivered and becomes a fighter who tells her father she will "overcome the fate I inherited."

**Symbols and motifs.** Gold and life: Gold Experience makes living things. The Arrow and the Requiem: power beyond its owner's control. The faceless Boss and the erased photograph: a man who hides his past. Venice, Sardinia and Rome: a road from the place of betrayal to the place of judgment, the Colosseum. Diavolo's endless deaths (ch. 149): a punishment that never arrives at an end. Bucciarati as the walking dead: a man living on borrowed time for his friends. The bus and the restaurant where Abbacchio meets his partner: death as a last stop.

**Traditions it echoes.** The gangster film (Leach notes "the most Godfather-y Godfather reference"). The Judas story, which Araki says he first had in mind for Fugo. Stoic acceptance of fate with virtuous action. The Gita's teaching on duty without attachment to results. Greek tragedy, where fate is fixed and character shows itself in how one meets it. (The Stoic, Gita and tragedy links are my own framing.)

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics and loyalty are affirmed. Authoritarian control is critiqued through the Boss. Might makes right is explored but not rejected: power decides the end, and the question is only who holds it. Religion is atmosphere, not argument. Critical readings of institutions appear in reviews (ANN). Gender and sexuality are thin; ANN's Leach and Anime Feminist flag gay-coded villains, and the one girl is the butt of several gags even as she becomes a fighter.

**Shifts across the series.** Viz vol. 1 (ch. 1-15): Giorno meets Bucciarati and passes Polpo's test. Vols. 2-3 (ch. 16-51): Polpo's death, the crew, Capri, Trish's arrival (ch. 29), Pompeii and the train. Vols. 4-5 (ch. 52-87): the assassin squad, Baby Face, Ghiaccio, the Boss's betrayal in Venice (ch. 78-84) and Fugo's exit. Vol. 6 (ch. 88-105): Squalo and Tiziano, the plane, Trish's awakening, Sardinia and Doppio. Vol. 7 (ch. 106-123): Risotto, Abbacchio's death (ch. 111), the Green Day mold. Vol. 8 (ch. 124-140): the Colosseum, Diavolo's past (ch. 130), Chariot Requiem, Narancia's death (ch. 137-138). Vol. 9 (ch. 141-155): Bucciarati's ascent (ch. 145), Requiem (ch. 148-149), and the "Sleeping Slaves" epilogue (ch. 150-155).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Giorno joins a gang to stop the drugs the gang sells. Can you change a corrupt group from inside, or does it change you?
2. Bucciarati breaks his loyalty to the Boss to protect Trish. When is it right to disobey someone you have promised to obey?
3. Araki says fate cannot be changed. Do you agree? How does your family talk about destiny, providence or choice?
4. Fugo chooses not to follow. Was he a coward, or prudent? Araki thought of him as a Judas and then could not draw it. Why?
5. Diavolo's punishment never ends. Is that justice? Who should decide?
