**Premise.** Tadakuni, Hidenori Tabata and Yoshitake Tanaka are three friends at Sanada North, an all-boys high school, whose days run on boredom and big ideas. Hidenori launches the schemes, Yoshitake goes along, and Tadakuni, the straight man, is dragged in and sometimes left out entirely. In short sketches they try on a schoolgirl's skirt to see what it is like (ch. 1), tell ghost stories that are more strange than scary, invent games, rehearse dates they will never go on, and lose to their sisters, who are faceless, fierce and unimpressed. Around them orbit the student council (a charismatic president and a gentle vice-president who looks like a delinquent), a rival girls' school council led by the proud Ringo, a riverbank novelist girl ("Yassan") who turns Hidenori into dialogue for her books, and Habara, a former neighborhood bully known as the Archdemon. A side series, "High School Girls Are Funky," gives three girls the same treatment. The final chapter has the trio chatting about the masked hero who saved Hidenori from bullies as a child, and ends by revealing who he was (ANN, 2012; Wikipedia; Comics Beat; Anime UK News).

## Life message

**Being young and foolish with good friends is worth treasuring; the boys' overblown dramas are part of growing up, and the story laughs with them more than at them.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | Teenage males testing status and courage inside a peer group, posturing about girls they barely talk to, and losing every contest with older sisters. The comedy is in how little the posturing achieves. |
| Pragmatic | Schemes fail, plans backfire and the boys shrug and try again. Rebecca Silverman (ANN) says the show treats high schoolers as still partly kids, moments of maturity giving way to foolishness. |
| Religious | No religion appears in sources opened. Faith readers will find little to object to beyond a peeking gag and a cross-dressing joke, and a warm picture of friendship. |
| Socio-cultural | A comic portrait of all-boys school culture and of adolescent masculinity: bravado, embarrassment, and shared nonsense. The film director said that watching it felt like a documentary of his own boys'-school years (Wikipedia). |

Outlook: Hopeful — nothing bad lasts; friendships hold, the boys stay foolish, and the last chapter looks back fondly on the childhood hero who saved Hidenori (ANN, 2012).
Hope source: J — the hope is in small everyday joys: ghost stories by the river, made-up games, a day with friends; friendship (F) is the close second.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a teenage boy's ordinary day actually like? The title promises "daily lives," and the joke is that real daily life is mostly talk, boredom and imagined drama. The series asks, without saying so, whether that is a waste or a treasure.

**The answer the story gives.** A treasure. The boys' schemes always fail, their speeches are overblown, their sisters crush them, and none of it matters, because they are together. The last chapter looks back to the childhood hero who saved Hidenori from bullies (ANN), a quiet reminder that under the nonsense these friendships are built on loyalty.

**Characters as arguments.** Hidenori argues for life as a grand story and is turned into one by Yassan's novel. Tadakuni argues for common sense and is ignored. Yoshitake argues for going along, and turns out to have been a hero. The sisters argue that boys should not take themselves so seriously. Habara shows that even a bully can grow up and try to make amends.

**Symbols and motifs.** The riverbank, where Hidenori and Yassan trade lines; faceless sisters, who are forces of nature rather than people to the boys; the masked hero Rubber Shooter; the skirt in ch. 1, trying on someone else's life for a moment.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese gag manga and manzai double acts; Nichijou, which critics compare it to (This Week in Anime); the boys'-school story in many cultures. Proverbs' fondness and impatience with fools fits it for Christian and Jewish readers; Buddhist readers may enjoy its deflation of the ego.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Absurdism is the engine, affirmed with warmth. Communitarianism is affirmed through the friend group. Hedonism and expressive individualism appear only at the scale of a teenage afternoon.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (adapted by the anime) are pure sketches with recurring cast; volumes 6-7 add callbacks and end with the reveal of the childhood hero (Penguin Random House; ANN).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Which of the three boys are you most like, and why?
2. The boys' plans almost always fail. Why do they keep trying?
3. The sisters always win. What do they see that the boys do not?
4. The boys feel guilty after peeking at Ringo. Were they right to? What would respect have looked like?
5. What does the story say about what makes an ordinary day good?
