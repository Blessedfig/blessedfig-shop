**Premise.** Myu, a magical mascot fleeing a monster, looks for a brave girl to become a magical girl and picks Kayo Majiba, a chain-smoking, class-skipping high-school delinquent who settles everything with her fists (Seven Seas, vol. 1). Kayo lives alone in a sterile underground bunker beneath her absent parents' mansion (ANN guide). Her devoted lackey Rei Masanido wears a pompadour wig and is, reviewers say, 25 (ANN guide). Powered up, Kayo pounds monsters (the Atasunmo) and bystanders alike. Rivals arrive: Nako Shusai, a student-council magical girl with equal power (vol. 2); an evil magical girl (vol. 3); Mon-chan (Montres), an Atasunmo who hypnotizes her friends (vol. 4) and later, gaining feelings and guilt, tries to turn over a new leaf (vol. 7); Masanido's supposedly dead gang (vol. 5); and Annabelle Anderson, a veteran American magical girl with a traumatic past (vols. 6-9). Later volumes turn to Kayo's origins: celestial factions (the Soshi and the Mika), the truth about her birth (vol. 10), the discovery that her mother is not living overseas and that her father has been building a Kayo clone (vol. 11), her reluctant magical-girl team (vol. 12), and an enemy who looks like Rui, her childhood best friend (vol. 13) (Seven Seas blurbs).

## Life message

**On the surface the joke is that raw force and attitude win; underneath, the story slowly reveals a neglected girl who will fight anyone except the few people she cares about, and asks what made her that way.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What if the girl chosen to save the world is the wrong person? The series asks whether the magical-girl story's promise (pure heart, true power) holds up when the power goes to a chain-smoking delinquent, and, more quietly, what made her that way.

**The answer the story gives.** At first, only laughs: force works, bystanders are scenery, the mascot is a con artist. Over many volumes the answer deepens: Kayo's loneliness (vol. 1), the one line she will not cross (attacking her friends, vol. 4), a monster who learns guilt (vol. 7), and the slow uncovering of her parents' secrets (vols. 10-11) suggest a story about a neglected child who protects the few people she has.

**Characters as arguments.** Kayo argues for strength and honesty about one's own anger (she gets angry at unfairness, ddnavi). Myu argues for duty, and is exposed as using people. Nako argues for order and rivalry. Mon-chan argues that even a predator can grow a conscience. Annabelle shows that power often hides trauma (vol. 9).

**Symbols and motifs.** The cigarette (the seed of the series). The transformation that strips the heroine instead of armoring her. The underground bunker beneath a mansion. The pompadour wig on an adult playing a teenage tough.

**Traditions it echoes.** The magical-girl genre and its darker parodies (Navarro compares it to Panty & Stocking; Gaffney places it among "magical girl splatter" manga), and yankii manga. For religious readers, its blunt question (is power the same as goodness?) echoes old warnings about strength without virtue, though the series rarely answers it.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Absurdism and strength-rules are enjoyed rather than argued. Nihilism is light. Loyalty and late redemption arcs carry the series' small store of values.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-3: pure parody and rival magical girls. Volumes 4-8: friends hypnotized, a reformed monster, an American rival. Volumes 9-13: family secrets, origins, a team and a lost childhood friend (Seven Seas).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Myu picked Kayo by mistake. What would a good magical girl have done differently, and would it be a better story?
2. Kayo will not hurt her friends even when hypnotized friends attack her. What does that say about her?
3. Mon-chan feels guilt for the first time. Can a monster change?
4. The story jokes about smoking and swearing. Why might a creator make those the joke?
5. What do you think Kayo's parents owed her?
