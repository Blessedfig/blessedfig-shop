**Premise.** Kyouko Hori is a popular, capable student at Katagiri High. At home she cooks, cleans and raises her little brother Souta while her parents work. Izumi Miyamura is the quiet boy in glasses whom classmates call gloomy. When Miyamura brings an injured Souta home, Hori finds he has nine self-made piercings and tattoos he hides at school (ch. 1). Each keeps the other's secret. Their friendship becomes a romance by volume 4-5, and the series then follows the couple and a widening friend group (Tooru Ishikawa, Yuki Yoshikawa, the student council trio of Kakeru Sengoku, Remi Ayasaki and Sakura Kouno, Shu Iura, Akane Yanagi) through to graduation (vol. 16). It is a slice-of-life romantic comedy with no single villain.

## Life message

**A good life comes from being known and accepted as you really are by a few people who choose you, and from caring for them in daily, ordinary ways; loneliness is the thing to be rescued from.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | Pair-bonding is the spine of the story: courtship, jealousy of rivals (vol. 9, 15), a mate-choice drama in the side couples, and a turn toward marriage (Christmas proposal, likely vol. 9) and children (webcomic ending). Hori raises her brother, a picture of kin care. Miyamura's early isolation is shown as real pain that the group heals: belonging as a survival need. |
| Pragmatic | Small acts solve real problems: Miyamura cooks, babysits and tutors; friends cover for each other; the couple talks through fights. The anime-era critics note his habit of acting without thinking through consequences (tattoos in a country where they mark yakuza; ANN vol. 5). The future is practical: he plans to take over his family's bakery (ch. 8). |
| Religious | Almost no religion on the page. A New Year shrine visit (vol. 10) is treated as an ordinary custom. Romance, including an implied first sexual experience between high school students (ch. 37), sits outside any religious frame and is not judged. Readers from faith traditions mainly weigh the sexual ethics, Hori's hitting, and the self-harm history. |
| Socio-cultural | A critique of judging by appearance: the "gloomy" boy and the "perfect" girl both have hidden selves. Japanese school norms (tattoos as yakuza markers, hiding them in summer uniforms) are the pressure Miyamura lives under. Gender roles are partly flipped: Hori is assertive and sometimes violent; Miyamura is gentle and domestic. Hori's young, absent father is a comic figure (TV Tropes). |

**Where traditions disagree.** The main dividing line is sexual ethics: frames that hold sex for marriage (most Christian frames, Latter-day Saint, Orthodox and Conservative Jewish, Muslim, Bahá'í, Sikh, Hindu, Jehovah's Witness) read ch. 37 as a real message conflict, though the scene shows nothing. A second line is body modification (tattoos and piercings), raised by Jewish, Muslim, Latter-day Saint, Adventist and Sikh families and welcomed by Satanist and some secular readers. Hori's violence is a shared concern across religious and secular frames. Progressive, humanist, Shinto, Pagan and UU frames find the closest fit.

**Fan favorites versus the official moral.** TV Tropes reports that Shu Iura, the series' comic punching bag, placed third in an official popularity poll; Akane Yanagi fifth despite little screen time; and the middle-school bully Tanihara tenth in Japan, where fans see him as tsundere, while Western fans largely dislike him and Miyamura's forgiving him (to verify against the official poll). Hori herself is a base-breaker: fans love her warmth but some call her violence and jealousy abusive (TV Tropes YMMV). Yuki divides fans over the love quadrangle. MyAnimeList ranks the manga #17 by popularity. The anime's fast pacing made Hori's jealousy and Miyamura's headbutting look abrupt, and the second season, *The Missing Pieces* (2023), was made to adapt skipped chapters (TV Tropes YMMV; Wikipedia).

## Creator and publisher context

- HERO wrote the original webcomic *Hori-san to Miyamura-kun* as a Japanese high school student. Square Enix asked for a "remake" as a print manga; Daisuke Hagiwara won a drawing contest to become the artist and began in October 2011 (Manga Passion interview, March 9, 2022; Wikipedia).
- On-record statements (Manga Passion, translated from German): HERO told Hagiwara, "As long as the plot and setting don't deviate too much from the original, draw it however is easiest for you," and checked every chapter. Hagiwara describes the work as full of "the sweet and pleasantly bitter taste" of youth. Hagiwara's favorite scene is the Christmas proposal; HERO's is the two families' feet tangled under the kotatsu. HERO says no character is based on HERO. Hagiwara says they relate most to Sakura and see a link to Miyamura before he met Hori, as Hagiwara "wasn't a particularly cheerful student."
- No statement by either creator about Miyamura's self-harm, the sexual content, Hori's violence, religion or politics was found. Both creators use pen names and keep private lives private. Hagiwara appeared at Japan Expo 2023 (Manga Passion headline, not opened). **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**
- Publisher: Square Enix (Monthly GFantasy). English: Yen Press, owned by Kadokawa (majority) and Hachette (from general knowledge; to verify). Anime: CloverWorks (an Aniplex studio).
- No reputable reports of controversy involving the creators were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to be truly known by another person, and what happens to someone who is not? Horimiya opens on two students who each live a double life. Hori is the model student at school and a tired housekeeper at home. Miyamura is the gloomy boy in glasses at school and a pierced, tattooed, gentle young man outside it. The series asks whether people can love the whole of each other, not just the half they show.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, and that love heals. The story's darkest material is in Miyamura's past: a child called "weird," "gloomy" and "creepy," always picked last (ANN, episode 3), who pushed a safety pin through his own ear (ch. 10) and, in a later scene quoted by Bloom Reviews, asked, "Nobody needs me. Can I just die?" The present-day story answers that younger boy. Friends notice him, Hori chooses him, and he becomes someone others rely on. The story never shows a counselor, parent or faith community helping; healing comes through being seen by peers. In the final volume, Miyamura walks through the empty school and imagines who he would be without them (vol. 16).

**Characters as arguments.** Miyamura argues that appearance lies, in both directions. Hori argues that a capable person also needs care, and also that love can carry anger: her jealousy and hitting are the series' most debated trait. Ishikawa argues for steady, unselfish friendship. Yuki and Sakura show unrequited love handled with more or less honesty. Sengoku and Remi show a couple who fight loudly and stay together. Shindou is the first person who was kind to Miyamura; Tanihara, the old bully, is forgiven, which some readers find generous and others find too easy (TV Tropes).

**Symbols and motifs.** Piercings and tattoos stand for the hidden self, and in Miyamura's past they also carry pain. His haircut (ch. 26) marks his choice to be seen. The bakery stands for continuity and ordinary work. The kotatsu where both families' feet tangle (HERO's favorite scene) stands for two households becoming one. Graduation (ch. 119-122) closes the frame.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese school romance and slice-of-life tradition; the "gap" character who differs at home and school; webcomic intimacy and small daily scenes. Its picture of healing through belonging echoes communitarian and humanist ideas more than any religious one. Its move from dating to proposal to family echoes traditional romance arcs.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is welcomed but bounded by group belonging. Virtue ethics shows in kindness and apology. Religious frameworks are absent; sex before marriage is treated as a natural step in a committed teen relationship, which faith traditions read very differently. Progressive readers welcome the accepted transgender character and reversed gender roles, while feminist critics (Anime Feminist; Girls in Capes) flag Hori's violence and Miyamura's appropriated tattoo designs. Partner violence is named as wrong in ch. 42 but returns as comedy, and Hori asking Miyamura to be "meaner" (ch. 43) is the most contested gag.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-34) are the core romance: secrets, confession by a fevered bedside (vol. 4), first kiss (ch. 27). Volumes 6-10 (ch. 35-71) deepen the couple: first sex (ch. 37), the hitting arc (ch. 42-43), the sports festival, Christmas and a proposal, New Year. Volumes 11-15 (ch. 72-118) widen to the side cast. Volume 16 (ch. 119-122) is graduation; volume 17 adds a prologue, "Pieces" and an epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hori and Miyamura are different people at school and at home. Do you have a "school self" and a "home self"? Who knows both?
2. Young Miyamura felt that nobody needed him. What helped him, and what could an adult in his life have done?
3. Hori hits Miyamura when she is jealous. Would readers react differently if a boy did this to a girl? What does your family believe about anger in a relationship?
4. Hori and Miyamura sleep together as high school students. What does your family's tradition teach about sex and commitment, and does the story agree?
5. Miyamura forgives the boy who bullied him. Is forgiveness always right, and does it mean forgetting?
