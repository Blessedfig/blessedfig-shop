**Premise.** Kyouko Hori is a popular, capable student at Katagiri High. At home she cooks, cleans and raises her little brother Souta while her parents work. Izumi Miyamura is the quiet boy in glasses whom classmates call gloomy. When Miyamura brings an injured Souta home, Hori finds he has nine self-made piercings and tattoos he hides at school (ch. 1). Each keeps the other's secret. Their friendship becomes a romance (confession vol. 4; first kiss ch. 27), and the series follows the couple and a widening friend group through graduation (ch. 119-122). It is a slice-of-life romantic comedy with no villain. The webcomic ending, and the fan wiki, have the two marry and have a son (Yen Press blurb; fan wiki).

## Life message

**A good life comes from being known and accepted as you really are by a few people who choose you, and from caring for them in daily, ordinary ways; loneliness is the thing to be rescued from.**

## Creator and publisher context

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it take to be truly known by another person, and what happens to someone who is not? Hori is the model student at school and a tired housekeeper at home; Miyamura is the gloomy boy in glasses at school and a pierced, tattooed, gentle young man outside it. Can people love the whole of each other, not just the half they show?

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, and that love heals. The darkest material is in Miyamura's past: a boy left out by classmates who pushed a safety pin through his own ear when school was hard and, the fan wiki says, thought about dying (ch. 10). The present-day story answers that younger boy: friends notice him, Hori chooses him, and he becomes someone others rely on. No counselor, parent or faith community appears; healing comes through being seen by peers.

**Characters as arguments.** Miyamura argues that appearance lies, in both directions. Hori argues that a capable person also needs care, and that love can carry anger: her jealousy and hitting are the series' most debated trait. Ishikawa argues for steady, unselfish friendship. Sengoku and Remi fight loudly and stay together. Shindou was the first to be kind to Miyamura; Tanihara, the old bully, is forgiven.

**Symbols and motifs.** Piercings and tattoos stand for the hidden self and, in Miyamura's past, for pain. His haircut (ch. 26) marks his choice to be seen. The bakery stands for ordinary work and continuity. The kotatsu where both families' feet tangle (HERO's favorite scene) stands for two households becoming one. Graduation (ch. 119-122) closes the frame.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese school romance and slice-of-life tradition; the "gap" character who differs at home and school; webcomic intimacy. Its picture of healing through belonging echoes communitarian and humanist ideas; its move from dating to proposal to family echoes traditional romance arcs.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism is welcomed but bounded by belonging. Virtue ethics shows in kindness and apology. Religion is absent; sex before marriage is a natural step in a committed teen relationship, which faith traditions read very differently. Feminist critics flag Hori's violence and the tattoo designs.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 (ch. 1-34): secrets, confession, first kiss (ch. 27). Vols. 6-10 (ch. 35-71): first sex (ch. 37), the hitting arc (end of vol. 6, ch. 42-43), Christmas, New Year. Vols. 11-15 (ch. 72-118): the side cast. Vol. 16 (ch. 119-122): graduation; vol. 17 adds a prologue, "Pieces" and an epilogue.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Hori and Miyamura are different people at school and at home. Do you have a "school self" and a "home self"? Who knows both?
2. Young Miyamura felt that nobody needed him. What helped him, and what could an adult in his life have done?
3. Hori hits Miyamura when she is jealous. Would readers react differently if a boy did this to a girl?
4. Hori and Miyamura sleep together as high school students. What does your family teach about sex and commitment, and does the story agree?
5. Miyamura forgives the boy who bullied him. Is forgiveness always right, and does it mean forgetting?
