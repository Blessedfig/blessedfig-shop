**Premise.** Life in the town of Tokisadame, Gunma, told in short, mostly unconnected skits. Three high school friends are at the center: energetic, lazy Yūko Aioi, short-tempered Mio Naganohara, who secretly draws boys' love (BL) manga, and deadpan Mai Minakami, who pranks Yūko with elaborate gags. Nearby live Nano Shinonome, an android girl with a wind-up key in her back who longs to pass as human; the Professor (Hakase), the eight-year-old genius who built her; and Sakamoto, a talking cat (Wikipedia). Around them: a principal who wrestles a deer, a vice principal bitter about his demotion, a science teacher scheming to capture Nano, a rich girl who fires guns at the boy she likes, and a go-soccer club. Nano starts attending school and befriends the trio (fan wiki). The original run ends with the friends writing letters to their future selves and burying a time capsule (ch. 192), and time-skip chapters show some of them as adults, Mio as a working manga artist (ch. 185; fan wiki). The 2021 revival continues the everyday skits (vols. 11-12).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is an ordinary day worth? The title means "everyday life," and the series asks whether the daily round of school, friends and snacks is boring or quietly wonderful.

**The answer the story gives.** Wonderful, because the ordinary is never quite ordinary. A principal wrestles a deer, a robot girl hides a roll cake in her arm, and a friend's fall becomes the funniest thing that happened all week. Creamer sums it up: the mundane and the magical are the same thing. The original run closes with letters to future selves (ch. 192), a quiet hope that friendships will last.

**Characters as arguments.** Yūko argues for making each day fun, at others' expense and her own, and her clumsy actions often help others (Beneath the Tangles). Mio argues for a secret dream guarded by fear, and her friends' acceptance shows the fear was not needed. Nano argues for belonging: she wants to be ordinary, while the Professor insists her wind-up key is cute. Nakanojō, the would-be scientist who sets out to disprove the supernatural, keeps getting the opposite result.

**Symbols and motifs.** Nano's wind-up key, the mark of difference she wants removed; Mio's wooden hair cubes; the time capsule; Sakamoto's scarf that lets him speak.

**Traditions it echoes.** Azumanga Daioh and School Rumble (SLJ), four-panel gag manga, and slice-of-life comedy. Christian readers may hear Psalm 118:24; Beneath the Tangles connects it to the "little way" of seeing God in small things. Buddhist readers may see attention to the present moment.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Absurdism is joyful rather than bleak: a world that makes no sense is still a good place to have friends. Communitarianism is affirmed through the web of the town. There is no politics or critique.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-5 (ch. 1-87) introduce the cast and Nano's arrival at school; vols. 6-10 (ch. 88-192) add time skips and end with the time capsule; vols. 11-12 (ch. 193-231, 2021-2024) return to the same everyday skits.

**Discussion questions.**
1. What ordinary thing happened today that could be a Nichijou skit?
2. Nano wants to hide that she is a robot. Why do people hide what makes them different?
3. Mio is scared her friends will find her drawings. Were her friends as bad as she feared?
4. Some jokes in the series are rough (guns, hitting, pranks). Where is the line between teasing and being mean?
5. If you wrote a letter to yourself in ten years, what would you ask?
