**Premise.** When her parents leave to care for her ailing grandmother, Miko "Meeko" Sonoda, 17, a second-year student at a girls' high school, moves into a share house run by her uncle. Her housemates are adults: Jun Matsunaga, a gruff, fussy graphic designer who works from home (he turns 28 in vol. 3) and mothers everyone; Ryo, a quiet university student of 20; Asako, a divorced nail artist of 25; Kentaro (Ken), a bartender of 26 who likes drink, women and games; and Akane, who later leaves to marry (Japanese Wikipedia; Kodansha blurb). Meeko learns housework and independence and falls for Matsunaga. She learns that her homeroom teacher was his former girlfriend (vol. 6); she confesses and he admits he returns her feelings, but he says they will not date until she graduates (vols. 8-9); he then moves out for a big job (vols. 9-10). In vol. 11 a missed birthday and Ryo's confession test her, and she and Matsunaga confirm their feelings; the ending sees them living together after she finishes university (PRH/Kodansha blurbs; ciatr; Japanese Wikipedia).

## Creator and publisher context

- Keiko Iwashita (story and art) is a shojo artist for Kodansha's Dessert; she says she had mostly drawn grown-up heroines before and tuned Meeko by recalling her own high-school years (Japanese Wikipedia, citing Dessert). Born and career dates not found. **Status: Needs more info on the creator.**
- On-record intent (Japanese Wikipedia, citing Dessert interviews): she wanted to draw an "adult man"; with a high-school heroine, the usual options (a teacher, a brother's friend) felt limited, so her editor suggested a share house; she made it one run by the heroine's uncle so the setup would feel safe and natural. She gave Matsunaga a "mother-like" side and said she can only draw a hero she could fall for herself. She visited a real share house, which felt like a family, and wanted readers to think share houses are nice. She said Ryo was never going to end up with Meeko.
- Publisher: Kodansha (Dessert, a shojo magazine for older teens and young women; ANN calls the audience slightly older). English: Kodansha USA, rated 13+.
- No statements on religion or politics, and no controversies involving the creator, were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What makes a home? Meeko leaves her parents' house for a building full of strangers and learns that a family can be made by people who cook for each other, nag each other about chores and notice when someone is sad. The second question is harder: what should an adult do when a teenager who lives under his roof falls in love with him?

**The answer the story gives.** A home is made by care, and love is shown by patience. Matsunaga does not push Meeko away, but when their feelings are mutual he sets a limit: they will not date until she graduates (vols. 8-9). He moves out for work (vols. 9-10), and the story tests whether their bond survives distance and his busy job (vol. 11). The ending, in which they live together after she finishes university (ciatr), presents the wait as having been worth it.

**Characters as arguments.** Matsunaga argues for care that looks like fussiness: the creator says she designed him as part older brother, part mother (Japanese Wikipedia). Meeko argues for earnest, whole-hearted first love. Ryo, the quiet student, is the road not taken: a peer who loves her silently and is turned down (vol. 11). The ex-girlfriend who turns out to be Meeko's teacher (vols. 5-7) shows that adult love has a past. Ken and Asako, a womanizer and a divorcee, find each other late (vols. 10-11): adults also grow up.

**Symbols and motifs.** The living room of the title, where everyone meets. Housework as love. The spare key to Matsunaga's new apartment (vol. 10). Sabako the house cat, whose own crush on Ryo is told in short comic extras (Japanese Wikipedia). Birthdays: his (vol. 3) and hers (vol. 11).

**Traditions it echoes.** The shojo romance of a girl and an older man, the share-house drama popular in Japanese TV, and the coming-of-age story of a first home away from parents. For faith readers it raises the old question of whether love that waits is love at its best, and how far waiting should go.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communitarianism is the series' warm center. Duty ethics and virtue appear in Matsunaga's restraint. Secular humanism is the background: comfort comes from people, not from any faith. Hedonism appears only in Ken, who settles down. The romance's age gap is presented without critique; parents may want to supply one.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-4: moving in, housework, the first crush and a kiss stolen while he sleeps. Vols. 5-7: his past, the teacher who was his girlfriend, a love-hotel night with nothing reported, and a planned Christmas confession. Vols. 8-9: mutual feelings and the rule to wait; he moves out. Vols. 10-11: distance, a new household, Ryo's confession and the couple's reunion.
