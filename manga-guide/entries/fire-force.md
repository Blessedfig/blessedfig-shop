**Premise.** Two hundred and fifty years after the Great Cataclysm set the world on fire, survivors live in the Tokyo Empire, powered by the Amaterasu energy plant and guided by the state religion, the Holy Sol Temple. People burst into flame without warning and become Infernals. Special Fire Force companies, set up partly by the Temple, put them down while a nun prays "Latom" for their souls. Shinra Kusakabe, a teenage pyrokinetic nicknamed "the devil" for his nervous grin, joins Company 8 to become a hero and to learn why his mother died in a fire and his baby brother Sho vanished. He finds a cult, the White-Clad, serving a being called the Evangelist, which plans a second Cataclysm, and he learns the Temple itself was founded to serve that plan. The story ends with Shinra remaking the world and becoming a direct prequel to Soul Eater.

## Life message

**A good life is the hero's life: you keep hope against despair, protect people even when they call you a devil, and treat your team as a family. Faith is honored when it is sincere and serves people; institutions that use faith to control people are exposed.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Atsushi Ohkubo, born 1979, assisted Rando Ayamine (GetBackers) before debuting with B. Ichi (2001-2003). Soul Eater (2004-2013, Square Enix) came next; Fire Force (2015-2022, Kodansha) followed, and in May 2020 he announced it would be his final manga (Wikipedia, opened, via the Soul Eater entry).
- On-record statements about Fire Force: at Anime Expo 2019 he said he wanted broad age appeal, drew on occult reports of spontaneous combustion, and settled on the "burning zombie" as the scary element: "Burning zombie's scary." He chose firefighters over a military force to put out burning zombies (ComicBook.com, July 31, 2019, opened). In a Kodansha House 2025 interview he said firefighting led him to focus on "the team and the teamwork," that he felt "maybe it is necessary to include elements like religion," that he had "a broad map" from the start but "didn't plan it all the way to the end," and that he wanted the Soul Eater link "sort of ambiguous" (Anime Corner, November 12, 2025, opened).
- No on-record statement about Tamaki's Lure, fan service, the Holy Sol Temple's real-world models, or his own religion or politics was found. The Soul Eater entry records that he made Maka female to depart from the typical male hero.
- Publisher: Kodansha (Japan); Kodansha USA is its American arm (from general knowledge). David Production animated the anime.
- No reputable reports of controversy involving the creator were found. Criticism of the fan service concerns the work (ANN; CBR; Anime Feminist; ComicBook.com).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do people need to believe in order to keep going when the world is burning? The Tokyo Empire answers with a state religion that tells everyone their soul will "return to the great flame." The villains answer with despair. Shinra answers with the stubborn hope of a hero.

**The answer the story gives.** The story sides with hope, and it locates hope in people more than in institutions. The Holy Sol Temple turns out to have been founded by Yona posing as the first emperor, to make people accept fire as their fate and so feed a second Cataclysm (fan wiki). The Evangelist draws power from Adolla, described as a collective unconscious (fan wiki). In the finale, the despair of humanity meets the brothers Shinra and Sho and their mother joined together, and Shinra remakes the world by imagining it: flowers, mountains, lost animals and revived people (fan wiki). He creates a figure of Death that removes fire powers and makes death "more personal" and less feared (fan wiki; TV Tropes). The epilogue leads into Soul Eater (ch. 304).

**Characters as arguments.** Shinra is the argument for hope that persists when the crowd calls you a devil. Iris is the argument that sincere faith keeps its worth when its institution fails her; Shinra tells her faith matters whatever its object (Drunken Anime Blog). Rekka is faith turned into fanatic cruelty, a priest who turns children into Infernals (ch. 22-33). Hibana lost faith after watching her sisters burn (ch. 19). Benimaru argues that people should do right for its own sake, not for gods (TV Tropes). Haumea, who hears humanity's darkest thoughts (ch. 241), argues that hope is only resignation. Tamaki carries the series' fan-service gag and, late on, the argument that she can save the world "her own way" despite shaming (ch. 280).

**Symbols and motifs.** Fire is both energy and destruction (ANN's review opens with Prometheus). The sun is worshiped by the church and imitated by the Amaterasu plant, which hides a trapped person. "Latom," the prayer said over the dying, became a buzzword in Japan. Shinra's grin, a trauma response read as a devil's smirk (ch. 0), marks the gap between appearance and goodness. Flaming footprints, "devil's footprints," become a hero's mark.

**Traditions it echoes.** Catholic imagery in vestments, nuns and rites; Shinto purification in the prayer for the dead (TV Tropes); apocalyptic religion; the Prometheus myth; Jungian ideas of a collective unconscious (my own framing). Shonen conventions of rival companies, tournaments and power-ups. The ending turns the world of Soul Eater, with its personified Death, into the fruit of this story.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Communal hope and team loyalty are shown as true. Nihilism lives in the villains and fails. Authoritarian collusion of state, church and corporation is exposed. Christianity appears mostly through the look of a corrupt institution and the sincere faith of one nun; reviewers disagree on whether this is respectful. Feminist critics see capable women undercut by fan service; ch. 280 is read by some as the author's answer.

**Shifts across the series.** Early chapters (0-52) mix firefighting horror, rival-company mysteries and comedy. The Nether arc (ch. 68-91) turns personal with Sho and Shinra's mother. The VS Holy Sol Temple and Haijima arcs (ch. 123-147) aim the story at institutions. The Stone Pillars through Great Cataclysm arcs (ch. 206-302) become an apocalypse and end in cosmic re-creation, which split readers (MAL review).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Iris keeps praying after learning the church was founded on a lie. What does the story say about faith and the institutions that carry it? What does your tradition say?
2. Shinra is called a devil by people he protects. Why might the story make its hero look like a villain?
3. The church taught people to accept fire as their fate. How can a belief be used to control people?
4. In the finale a human remakes the world and creates Death. How does that compare with your family's view of who creates and gives life?
5. Critics call Tamaki's "Lucky Lecher Lure" humiliating; ch. 280 has a crowd shame her. Is the gag funny, harmful, or both? Who is the joke on?
