**Premise.** Isao Komori, 21, moved to Tokyo from Gunma for university, failed to make friends and stopped attending. He lies to his parents, lives on their allowance and spends his days on games, the internet and masturbation (Japanese Wikipedia; MangaUpdates). His one outing is the convenience store where he sees a high-school girl he privately calls "the convenience store angel," and he begins following her home at night. One night she turns and smiles at him; he blacks out and wakes in her body. She is Mari Yoshizaki, a popular second-year student. His own body is still in his apartment, inhabited by someone who seems to be the ordinary Komori and who has never heard of Mari. Mari's quiet classmate Yori Kakiguchi, who idolizes Mari, sees that "Mari" is someone else, and the two search for the real Mari. The twist (spoiler): the "Komori" inside Mari is not Komori at all but a second personality Mari built from watching Komori, whom she had been following; her mother had renamed her from "Fumiko," the name her late grandmother gave her, and she long felt unseen. After reading the real Komori's diary, Mari parts from the imagined Komori and the child Fumiko, regains herself and becomes friends with Yori (Japanese Wikipedia; ciatr).

**A self cannot be borrowed or escaped into: the way out of loneliness is to be seen as you are by someone who looks, and to face your own history rather than hide inside an imagined other.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Shuzo Oshimi (born 1981, Kiryu, Gunma) is a manga artist whose works deal with adolescence, sexuality and family (see The Flowers of Evil and Blood on the Tracks entries). Inside Mari ran in Manga Action from 2012 to 2016, overlapping with The Flowers of Evil (Wikipedia).
- **Anime Feminist interview, December 6, 2024 (Tony Sun Prickett and Vrai Kaiser, by email; opened).** Oshimi: "At the time, I had a deep admiration for, but also a fear of, women. Those feelings weren't so much directed at real-life women as they were toward the femininity within myself." "Looking back on it now, Inside Mari feels like an attempt to understand and confront the femininity within me." "I don't have a full understanding of my own sexuality." On body-swap and self-fragmentation: "This feeling of self-fragmentation and dismantling is something I want to share with readers." The interviewers paraphrase his Inside Mari afterwords as musing on whether his desire to be a girl is authentic; that is their description, not his words. In the same article he is quoted, from his Welcome Back, Alice afterword, as rejecting "trying to hook male readers by inflaming their libido": "I myself felt something unsavory in those desires."
- **Volume 1 afterword (as summarized by ANN's Dupree; opened):** the story grew from a wish to understand women's perspectives.
- 2024 ANN interview (cited in The Flowers of Evil entry): he writes for "myself when I was 14," calls his protagonists "projections of myself," and spoke of "some ambiguity about my own gender."
- No statement on religion or politics found. This entry reports only what he has said on the record and does not speculate about his private life. No widely reported personal controversy found; the 2020 Kinokuniya removal concerned the store and a legislator, not Oshimi.
- Publishers: Futabasha (Japan). Denpa, a small US publisher, released the English edition (2018-2023); Crunchyroll Manga carried it digitally from 2014 (Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is a self, and can you live inside someone else? The title says "I am inside Mari." The story asks whether that "I" is real, and what it costs a person to hide in another.

**The answer the story gives.** You cannot. The man who seems to be inside Mari turns out to be her own creation, a figure she built from watching a lonely stranger. Mari's escape into "Komori" was a way to be someone, since the girl she was, renamed by her mother from Fumiko, felt unseen. Healing comes when she reads the real Komori's diary and lets the imagined man and the child Fumiko go. The self is recovered, not swapped.

**Characters as arguments.** Komori, in his room, is the case for withdrawal and consuming others as images; Dupree calls him "instantly unlikable" and an honest portrait of the man who "fetishizes teen girls' bodies." Mari is the case for a self built in hiding. Yori is the case for looking: she alone notices "Mari" is someone else because "Mari" says "cute" when the real Mari never would (Japanese Wikipedia). The mother is the case for a parent defining a child. The real Komori, who ends up cutting his hair and working, is the case for small, plain steps out.

**Symbols and motifs.** Mirrors and faces: a mirror sequence in which the two figures merge, faceless and "empty hole-faced" figures (AniList reviews). The convenience store as a place of watching. Names: Fumiko and Mari. The diary as the truth that breaks the illusion. The chapter titles track the arc: "First-Time Shojo" (ch. 1), "I Am Isao Komori" (ch. 24), "I Look at Mari" (ch. 30), "I Look at Myself" (ch. 39) (BookBrainz).

**Traditions it echoes.** The body-swap comedy (turned inside out), the yuri school story, the psychological thriller about split personality, and Oshimi's own run of stories about adolescent shame. Wikipedia calls it an existential deconstruction of both genres.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential and postmodern ideas about a constructed self drive the plot. Feminist readings find a frank picture of the male gaze and of a girl who wants to be seen; some readers will find that the same pages also put a teenager's body on display. Buddhist echoes of non-self are present but unstated. Religious traditions appear nowhere. Traditional readers may note that the real Komori recovers by going home.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2: the swap, school life in Mari's body, Yori's discovery and the search (ch. 1-17 likely). Volume 3: Hiroki's forced kiss, the friend group's rejection and "Komori" coming out to Yori and others (ch. 21-24). Volumes 4-6: the mystery deepens, the phone calls, the sexual scenes between the two Komoris, the memory of "Fumiko." Volumes 7-8: the amusement park, the mother's denial and departure, Mari's collapse. Volume 9: the diary, the reveal, the parting and recovery (ciatr; Japanese Wikipedia).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Komori watches Mari every night. How does the story make you feel about him at first, and does that change?
2. Why does Yori notice that Mari is "someone else" when no one else does?
3. Mari's mother changed her name. Why might that matter so much to who Mari thinks she is?
4. What does the twist say about whether we can know someone from the outside?
5. The story includes sexual scenes to show a crisis of identity. Does showing something in order to criticize it change whether it should be shown?
