**Premise.** High schooler Honoka Takamiya keeps getting beaten up by the fan club of the school's idol, Ayaka Kagari, until a school building is hurled at him and Ayaka saves him as a fire-wielding witch. She explains that she is his sworn guardian: a powerful entity, Evermillion (the White Princess), lives inside him, the Tower Witches want his "white stuff," and their lives are magically linked so that any wound he takes appears on her (ch. 1-4; Wikipedia). Ayaka, tall, stoic and fiercely protective, calls him her "princess," a role reversal at the heart of the comedy (Wikipedia; MangaUpdates Role Reversal). The Workshop Witches who protect him include Ayaka's centuries-old mother Kazane, chair of the school, and Honoka's jealous younger sister Kasumi, who has a running brother complex (v3; The Fandom Post). Their mothers arranged long ago for Honoka and Ayaka to marry (Wikipedia). Villains such as the five-girl "KMM Gang," the potion witch Chronoire, Medusa and the bomber Weekend try to kidnap him; several are defeated and later taken into Honoka's home (Wikipedia, anime episode summaries). Later volumes reveal that Ayaka was raised in chains as a living weapon by the witch Kayou Kagari, that as a child Honoka saved her by taking Evermillion into himself, and that their memories were erased (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). The kind Honoka gradually changes even Evermillion, who had meant to devour his soul (Wikipedia). In the last volume Fulcanelli, the witch who governs all witches, offers Honoka a peaceful future at a price (Kodansha USA, vol. 17); the series reportedly ends with Honoka and Ayaka together (mangashed.com; to verify).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to protect someone, and what does it cost? Ayaka's wounds are literally Honoka's wounds, so every attack on him is an attack on her (Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** Protection is love, and it runs both ways. Ayaka guards Honoka, but it was Honoka who first saved her, by taking a demon into himself as a child (fan wiki). His kindness is the series' real power: it changes Evermillion, defeats enemies without destroying them, and fills his mother's house with former foes.

**Characters as arguments.** Ayaka argues for total devotion, and sometimes for cruelty in its service (her torture of captives is played as a joke). Honoka argues for mercy and for learning to stand on his own. Kazane argues that order needs force. Kayou argues that a child can be a tool, and is the series' clearest villain. Kasumi argues for possessive family love, played for laughs. Komachi argues for open arms.

**Symbols and motifs.** Fire (Ayaka, Evermillion); the shared wound; the "princess" title on a boy; the sealed room where Ayaka was kept; the five seals inside Honoka; bears and rabbits as familiars.

**Traditions it echoes.** Knight-and-princess romance with the sexes swapped; the magical-girl and urban-fantasy genres; the demon tamed by a pure heart, an old folk-tale shape. Religious readers may see an echo of love casting out fear (1 John 4:18) in Evermillion's change, while also noting the tradition's warnings about witchcraft.

**How each lens is portrayed.** The occult is the setting, not a creed. Feminism shows up as role reversal, undercut by fan service. Communitarianism and nonviolence win in how enemies are treated. Might-makes-right is enjoyed in Kazane and condemned in Kayou.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-6 (ch. 1-27, the anime's span): school comedy, the KMM Gang, Chronoire, Medusa and Weekend. Middle volumes: Ayaka's past, Kayou, and the Kagari clan. Volumes 16-17: the Kagari estate crisis, Honoka's trial and Fulcanelli's offer (Kodansha USA; TV Tropes).
