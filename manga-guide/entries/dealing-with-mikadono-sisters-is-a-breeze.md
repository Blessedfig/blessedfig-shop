**Premise.** Yuu Ayase, 17, the son of the late, legendary actress Subaru Ayase, has no talent of his own: below average at study, sport and acting. He and his mother were estranged by her fame and only reconciled before she died of illness; her last wish was that he build a happy family. He is taken in by her friend Toru Mikadono, a strict believer that mediocrity is worthless, and enrolls at Saika Academy for gifted students, where he finds he is living with the school's "Three Royals": Kazuki, a male-role star in an all-female theater troupe; Niko, a karate champion who secretly loves cute things; and Miwa, a shogi prodigy with an IQ over 180 (Wikipedia; Japanese Wikipedia). The sisters cannot cook or keep house, and Yuu quietly takes care of them, cooking meals tailored to each, helping Kazuki rehearse, standing up for Niko against a rival who wants her to transfer, and supporting Miwa when she loses to her rival Sakura (anime episodes 1-12, ch. 1-45). The sisters fall for him, though he sees them as family. Later arcs bring a film director, young star actors and a rival school (Japanese Wikipedia).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is a person worth something without a talent? Yuu is the son of a genius and has none of her gifts. Mr. Mikadono says mediocrity is evil. The series asks whether he is right.

**The answer the story gives.** No. Care, patience and practice are gifts too. Yuu's cooking and housework (episodes 1-2), his persistence (episode 5) and his willingness to lose in public for Niko (episode 8) are what change the sisters. Caitlin Moore (ANN) reads it as treating caretaking as a talent.

**Characters as arguments.** Yuu argues for kindness and steady effort. Mr. Mikadono argues for talent above all; his daughters push back (episode 5). Sakura argues that hard work can rival genius, and Miwa learns from her (episodes 10-12). Hayato argues for strength without softness; Niko beats him by being herself (episode 8).

**Symbols and motifs.** Meals made for each sister; the old childhood photo from a festival (episodes 3-4); the yukata Niko chooses; the marshmallow doll (episode 11); the family photo at the theme park (episode 9).

**Traditions it echoes.** The household comedy (Fruits Basket, which Moore names), the harem romance, and the Christian and Buddhist ideals of humble service. Yuu's mother's dying wish frames the whole story as honoring a parent.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Found family and virtue through effort are affirmed; self-expression wins in small ways; the Confucian pressure to excel is questioned but the father is not made a villain.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-5 (ch. 1-45, the anime): moving in, each sister's arc, the theme park, Sakura. Later volumes (to v20, ch. 226) add a film director, star actors and a rival school (Japanese Wikipedia); their content was not covered by sources opened.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Yuu says he has no talent. Do you agree? What is he good at?
2. Mr. Mikadono thinks mediocrity is bad. Why might a father believe that, and what does it do to his daughters?
3. Niko thought she had to choose between being strong and being cute. Why?
4. Miwa played shogi to be praised. Is that a bad reason?
5. What makes the Mikadono house a family by the end?
