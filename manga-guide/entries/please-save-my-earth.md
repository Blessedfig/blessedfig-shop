**Premise.** Alice Sakaguchi, a shy girl who can make plants grow by singing to them, moves from Hokkaido to Tokyo and is stuck babysitting her bratty seven-year-old neighbor, Rin Kobayashi. Her classmates Jinpachi and Issei share dreams of being alien scientists on a moonbase watching Earth; soon Alice dreams too, as Mokuren, the team's biologist and a sacred "Kiches" priestess of the goddess Sarjalim. During a quarrel Alice slaps Rin, who falls from a 15th-floor balcony; he survives, and wakes from a coma with the memories, mind and psychic powers of Shion, the team's bitter engineer. Rin blackmails his way into an engagement with Alice, secretly turns Tokyo Tower into a transmitter to the moonbase and collects the seven scientists' passwords by deceit, threats and violence. Long flashbacks (vols. 8-11 and 15-20) reveal the past: a war orphan's loneliness, a love triangle, the homeworld's destruction, Shion's assault on Mokuren, a virus that killed the team, and Shion's nine years alone on the Moon. At Tokyo Tower Rin learns that Mokuren's recorded song kept the base alive, lets go of Shion's rage, and Alice confesses her love for him (vol. 21). (Wikipedia volume summaries; ANN.)

## Life message

**Your past can explain you but must not rule you: face old wounds honestly, let go of grudges, forgive, and live your own life now, in care for the living Earth.**

Outlook: Hopeful — the moon team's past ends in war, plague and a lonely death, but in the present Rin lets go of Shion's rage, the moonbase is not used as a weapon, Alice and Rin choose each other, and a four-years-later epilogue shows everyone moving on (vol. 21).
Hope source: R — forgiveness and redemption: Mokuren forgives Shion, her song outlasts his despair, and Rin is freed when he lets go of the grudge against Shukaido and the world (vols. 11, 20-21); love between two (L) is the close second.

**Where traditions disagree.** Traditions that teach one life and a final judgment (most Christian, Muslim, Adventist, Witness and Orthodox Jewish frames) read the reincarnation premise as a real worldview concern, sharpened by the reader fad of 1989; Buddhist, Hindu, Pagan and spiritual-but-open readers find the same premise a natural frame for a story about letting go. Nearly every frame values the forgiveness theme. The sharpest shared concern is not explicitness but message: the past-life assault is later folded into a love story, and a seven-year-old's engagement to a teenager is played as romance; progressive and conservative readers object to these for different reasons.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

Those who face the truth and let go of old grudges are freed (Haruhiko, Issei, Rin at the end); those who live by envy, control or revenge suffer (Shion, Majima), but the story extends sympathy to almost everyone and punishes almost no one in the present.

## Creator and publisher context

- Saki Hiwatari (born 1961, Kanagawa) has spent most of her career on this series and its sequels Embraced by the Moonlight (2003-2014) and I Sing with the Earth (2015-); other works include Global Garden (2001-2004) and Tower of the Future (1994-1998) (Wikipedia). She now draws digitally (Bunshun 2022).
- Intent: exhausted after her previous series, she wanted something "healing" to draw and started from a magnolia tree at her workplace; she added suspense as she wrote, and calls the series the result of her struggle over whether to draw something serious or funny; it was planned to last one year (Wikipedia, citing Comic Natalie 2014). Critic Jason Thompson notes it grows darker and more adult from vol. 9 (Wikipedia).
- On readers who took it as real: she was surprised by letters claiming to be the moon scientists; with her editor she placed a fiction disclaimer at the start of vol. 7 and her own statement in vol. 8 (1989), and says she would have done so even without the Tokushima incident, adding (translated) that telling fiction from reality is difficult "at any age" (Bunshun Online 2022, pp. 2-3). The story's rule that suicide prevents rebirth was part of her answer, though she found the issue hard to address in fiction.
- No statements on religion or politics were found. Status: no controversies involving the creator were found beyond the reader-response episode above.
- Publishers: Hakusensha (Hana to Yume, a shoujo magazine); VIZ Media in English, rated T+ (Teen Plus).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** If you remembered who you were in a past life, and what you did and suffered, would you still be free to be yourself? Alice is afraid that recovering Mokuren will erase Alice. Rin has already lost that fight: from the moment he wakes from his coma, a bitter adult engineer from a dead world is steering a seven-year-old body. The series asks whether memory is destiny.

**The answer the story gives.** No: the past explains but does not decide. Mokuren's spirit, falling to Earth, saves the falling child and tells Alice to live her life her own way (vol. 20). Rin is saved not by beating Shion's enemies but by learning that what Shion hated and feared was answered long ago by Mokuren's song, still playing on the dead moonbase (vol. 21). Forgiveness, honesty and letting go free each character in turn.

**Characters as arguments.** Shion argues that the world never gave him love, so he owes it nothing; his foster father's patience (vol. 8) and Mokuren's forgiveness (vol. 11) are the counter-argument. Shukaido argues for punishment and then lives with the guilt of what he did, which Haruhiko confesses (vol. 14). Gyokuran stands for upright rules; Enju for faithful but unreturned love; Tamura, a former yakuza, for learning to protect instead of running from failure. Alice argues for compassion toward the person who is hurting you.

**Symbols and motifs.** The Earth seen from the Moon: a green, untouched paradise. Plants that grow when Mokuren sings, wrecking Shion's computers. The passwords, each a piece of a person's trust. Tokyo Tower as the bridge between past and present. Mokuren's recorded song, playing for years after everyone died. The magnolia (mokuren) that started the series.

**Traditions it echoes.** Wikipedia links it to The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter (a moon-born woman on Earth); it echoes Buddhist rebirth and the release from clinging; for Christian readers it echoes forgiveness of an enemy and a lost son coming home; for environmental readers it echoes the Earth-from-space view and Silent Running (ANN).

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism wins: the present self chooses. Buddhist and New Age ideas of rebirth are the setting rather than the argument. Environmentalism is affirmed gently. Feminist questions are raised by Mokuren's story but the series' own handling of the assault later turns it into romance, which is where many modern readers object.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-7 are a mystery among teenagers, with comedy and 1980s in-jokes (Wikipedia). Volumes 8-11 go deep into Shion's childhood and the moonbase; ANN and Thompson mark this as the turn to darker, adult material. Volumes 12-14 bring confession and the truth about Rin. Volumes 15-20 tell Mokuren's side. Volume 21 resolves at Tokyo Tower, with a quiet epilogue that ANN finds weaker than the buildup and Soliloquy in Blue finds satisfying.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Alice is afraid that remembering Mokuren will erase her. What part of you would you want to keep no matter what?
2. Rin blackmails, lies and hurts people. Why does the story try to save him instead of punishing him?
3. Three characters remember the night in Shion's cell three different ways. How can we know what really happened, and whose account should count most?
4. Some real readers came to believe they were the moon scientists, so the author printed a statement that it was fiction. Why do you think stories can feel that real?
5. A seven-year-old with an adult's memories is engaged to a sixteen-year-old. How do you feel about how the story treats that?
