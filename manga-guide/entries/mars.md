**Premise.** Kira Aso is a silent, withdrawn high-school art student; Rei Kashino is a popular, reckless playboy who races motorcycles. When Rei asks her for directions, she hands him a wordless map with a drawing on the back; soon they are classmates, and Rei walks in on their art teacher sexually harassing her (v1). He promises to protect her in exchange for a painting, and she asks him to model. Their romance draws out two buried wounds: Kira's stepfather raped her when she was fourteen, which is why she fears men, and Rei's younger twin Sei killed himself in front of him in junior high, leaving a note meant to scar him. Around them move Harumi, a bully who becomes Kira's fiercest friend; Shiori, Sei's and then Rei's girlfriend, who blames herself for Sei's death; and Masao Kirishima, a scarred, androgynous classmate who murdered the boy who abused him and who sees Rei as his violent 'god.' Across 15 volumes the couple faces suicide attempts, Rei's rage, a breakup when Kira moves back in with her mother and stepfather, Rei's discovery that his uncle was his real father, and Masao's stabbing of Rei on the way to a party celebrating the couple's marriage. Rei survives, chooses life over Sei's pull, and returns to racing (Wikipedia; TV Tropes; Abigail Jackson; PRH blurbs).

## Life message

**Love that is patient and faithful can help two wounded people heal, if both face the past honestly and choose life over the pull of violence and despair.**

## Creator and publisher context

- Fuyumi Soryo (born January 6, 1959, Beppu, Oita) grew up in the household of a master of the Kanze school of Noh, studied at a fashion college, debuted in 1982 in Bessatsu Shojo Comic and worked as an assistant to Fuyumi Ogura. She won the 1988 Shogakukan Manga Award (shojo) for Boyfriend, then moved to seinen work for Morning in 2001: ES (Eternal Sabbath) and Cesare, a 13-volume study of Cesare Borgia (Wikipedia).
- Garrity notes her old-school shojo line, influenced by the Year 24 Group, her racing art, and her use of clothes and hair to show inner growth, which she links to Soryo's fashion training (ANN, House of 1000 Manga).
- No interview about Mars's themes was found in sources opened. **Status: Needs more info on the creator's stated intent.**
- Publisher: Kodansha (Bessatsu Friend, a shojo magazine). Kodansha USA's 2026 reissue is rated 16+ and markets it as a shojo classic on par with Nana (PRH blurb).
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Can love heal what violence and abuse have done, or does it only bind two wounded people more tightly to their pasts? Kira has been taught by her stepfather that men are dangerous; Rei has been taught by his twin's suicide that he destroys what he loves. The series asks whether two such people can make each other whole.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, slowly, if both face the truth. Kira must speak about the abuse; Rei must stop answering pain with fists and speed. The climax gives the answer in an image: in his coma Rei is drawn toward his dead brother, then turns toward Kira and chooses life (v15; Abigail Jackson). Garrity sums it up: the world may belong to the god of war, but one should serve the god of love.

**Characters as arguments.** Rei argues for passion and protection, and learns their limits. Kira argues for gentleness and truth-telling through art. Sei is the case against despair turned into revenge: his suicide note wanted to wound. Masao is Rei's dark mirror, a boy who answered abuse with murder and wants Rei to be his violent god; Abigail Jackson calls him a warning of what Rei could become. Harumi shows that a bully can change. Shiori shows grief that clings. The stepfather and the art teacher show adults abusing trust.

**Symbols and motifs.** Mars, the god of war, and Kira's painting of Rei as Mars, which later unseals buried memories (v5-6, PRH). The wordless map with a drawing of mother and child (v1): the maternal love both lack. The motorcycle and the racetrack as the edge of death. Rooftops, where Sei died and Kurasawa nearly did. The bedsheet and the clock: the night Kira strikes back at her abuser and the night she chooses Rei.

**Traditions it echoes.** Romeo and Juliet ('star-crossed lovers,' UK Anime Network), the Western bad-boy romance and the 1990s shojo melodrama (Brenner calls it a soap opera). Its 'choose life' climax echoes Deuteronomy 30:19 for some Jewish and Christian readers and the Buddhist call to let go of clinging, though the series gives no religious frame.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Secular humanism and a personal form of nonviolence are affirmed: love, art and therapy, not force, solve things. Nihilism is critiqued through Sei and Masao. Hedonism (Rei's playboy past) is outgrown. Traditional readers find fidelity and marriage honored but the teen sexual relationship approved; progressive readers find survivors centered but abusers unpunished.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2: meeting, the art teacher, Harumi's bullying. Volumes 3-4: Sei revealed, Kurasawa's suicide attempt, therapy and a dangerous race (PRH). Volumes 5-6: Sei's past and the 'Mars' painting. Volumes 7-8: Masao's obsession, Kira's mother hospitalized (PRH). Likely volumes 8-10: Kira strikes her stepfather, the couple's first night, the breakup when she moves back home (PRH; TV Tropes). Volumes 11-15: Rei's parentage and his mother's suicide, reconciliation, engagement, Masao's attack and the wedding (Abigail Jackson; TV Tropes; to verify volume placement).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rei protects Kira with his fists. When does that help, and when does it make things worse?
2. Sei's note was meant to hurt Rei. How does Rei finally stop letting it rule him?
3. Kira's mother takes back the stepfather. Why might Kira go home anyway, and what do you think of Rei's reaction?
4. Masao says Rei and he are the same. What makes them different by the end?
5. The story shows two teenagers sleeping together as a loving step. What would your family say about that choice?
