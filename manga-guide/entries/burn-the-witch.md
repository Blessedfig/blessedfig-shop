**Premise.** Dragons are real but invisible to most people, and long ago they caused 72% of all deaths in London. Only the people of "Reverse London," a hidden city under the ordinary one, can see them, and their agency Wing Bind protects, manages and, when needed, exterminates dragons; touching a dragon without a licence can bring 100 years in prison or execution. Noel Niihashi, a solemn 17-year-old with a love of Japanese culture, and Ninny Spangcole, a loud ex-member of the pop band Cecile Die Twice, are low-ranking witch partners who work for pay and promotion points. In the one-shot (ch. 0.8) their schoolmate Balgo Parks, who keeps trying to see Noel's underwear, learns that his childhood friend Shelby was a dragon in disguise; bitten, Balgo becomes a "Dragonclad" who draws dragons to him, and the witches must look after him. In ch. 1-4 Wing Bind's directors (the Top of Horns) order Balgo's elimination, and Ninny's lonely former bandmate Macy Baljure turns out to have secretly raised a baby dragon that becomes a legendary "Märchen" dragon, Cinderella. Noel and Ninny protect both Balgo and Macy until their boss Billy Banx Jr. kills Cinderella with a long-range shot; Balgo's death order is lifted and Macy, now Dragonclad too, moves in with Ninny. The last panel of the one-shot reveals the agency is the "Soul Society: West Branch" of Bleach (Bleach wiki; Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**Real magic is doing your job well and protecting the people in your care, even when your own bosses would sacrifice them; waiting to be chosen like a fairy-tale princess leads people astray.**

| Pragmatic | Noel and Ninny want pay and achievement points and argue over credit (ch. 1, 4); the ending docks their pay for property damage (ch. 4). Duty is done because it is the job, and the job is done well. |

## Creator and publisher context

- Tite Kubo (story and art) created Bleach (Weekly Shōnen Jump, 2001-2016) (from general knowledge; to verify). Burn the Witch shares Bleach's universe: Reverse London is the British counterpart of the Soul Society (Wikipedia; TV Tropes, citing Kubo).
- On-record context (Comic Natalie, reported by Comicbook.com, November 2020): the one-shot was requested by Jump's editor-in-chief for the magazine's 50th anniversary; the season 2 announcement was his editor's idea, Kubo drew sketches for it, and he asked readers to wait patiently because it would not be soon.

- Publisher: Shueisha (Weekly Shōnen Jump). VIZ Media (owned by Shueisha and Shogakukan (from general knowledge; to verify)) publishes it under Shonen Jump, rated T.
- No controversies involving the creator were found.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who gets protected, and who decides? Reverse London lives by a cold rule: dragons kill people, so anyone who becomes a danger can be written off. Burn the Witch puts a goofy teenage boy and a lonely pop singer on the wrong side of that rule and asks whether two low-ranking witches will do their job or do what is right.

**The answer the story gives.** Do both. Noel and Ninny never quit Wing Bind, but they will not hand over Balgo or Macy (ch. 3-4). The happy ending comes partly by luck and partly from the system's own pragmatism: Balgo is spared because he may be useful (ch. 4).

**Characters as arguments.** Ninny argues against fairy tales: princesses think they choose but are chosen, so she would rather cast the spell (ch. 1). Macy is the case for why that matters: she wanted to be chosen and special, and her secret dragon nearly destroyed the city (ch. 3-4). Noel argues for quiet competence. Bruno is the institution: harsh, then fair. Shelby, a monster in a friend's shape, warns that appearances deceive, as the one-shot's title says.

**Symbols and motifs.** The phone booth that drops into another London. Fairy tales as dragons (Cinderella the Märchen). Collars and chains on the Dragonclad. The title, taken from a Radiohead song about mob fear (Wikipedia; song meaning from general knowledge; to verify), set against a story where witches are the police.

**Traditions it echoes.** Bleach's soul-reaper bureaucracy, the British urban fantasy of hidden magical Londons, and the fairy-tale debunking of modern fantasy. For Christian readers it raises the old question of whether "good witches" in fiction are harmless; for Wiccan readers it is a rare story where witches protect people.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pragmatism is the world's operating system and is gently mocked (the pay and points). Duty to the innocent wins over institutional calculation. Occult imagery is pure world-building, with no worship or spirit contact beyond the Bleach tie-in.

**Shifts across the series.** The one-shot (ch. 0.8) is a comic introduction with Balgo's gag and Shelby's betrayal. Ch. 1-4 turn more serious: a death order, Macy's loneliness and a city-wrecking dragon. Season 2 has not appeared.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Wing Bind ordered Balgo eliminated to keep the city safe. Were they wrong? What else could they have done?
2. Ninny says fairy tales are lies. What does Macy's story say about wanting to be "chosen" or special?
3. Noel and Ninny work for points and pay. Does it matter why someone does a good job?
4. Balgo keeps trying to see Noel's underwear and gets hit every time. Is it funny? What would respect look like?
5. Some families are uneasy with witches as heroes. How is magic in this story different from, or like, real-world witchcraft?
