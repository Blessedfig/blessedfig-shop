**Premise.** Nanoka Koyanagi, a nine-year-old fourth grader (ANN), thinks she is smarter than nearly everyone in her class, likes to announce what "life is like," and has no friends at school. After school she visits three friends: "Skank-san," a woman in her twenties or thirties who helped her care for an injured black cat (Nanoka took a slur scrawled on her nameplate for her name); "Granny," an old woman who bakes for her; and "Minami," a high school girl Nanoka finds cutting her wrist on the roof of an abandoned building, who writes stories. Her teacher assigns the class to discuss with their seatmates what happiness is. Nanoka fights with her parents when work keeps them from visiting day; Minami, who regrets never making up with her own parents before they died, makes her promise to reconcile, and her parents rearrange their work to come. Her seatmate Kiryu, who loves to draw, is bullied after his father is accused of shoplifting without proof; when Nanoka defends him, he rejects her and the class shuns her. Skank-san advises her to help Kiryu in the way he needs, and the two go back to school together. Then the three friends vanish. They were other versions of Nanoka: futures in which she did not reconcile with her parents (who would have died in a plane crash on the trip they canceled for visiting day), in which she grew up proud and friendless and fell into sex work, and one in which she lived a long life. In the last pages the grown Nanoka wakes from the same dream, and Kiryu, now a painter, proposes to her (Japanese Wikipedia, plot of the novel).

## Life message

**Happiness is being accepted by others and caring seriously for someone else; pride that looks down on people leads to loneliness, while apologizing, reconciling and standing with the outcast open a happier life.**

| Meta lens | How it reads the series |

| Evolutionary biology | A child's need for her parents and for a place among peers drives the story; the three futures show what isolation costs, and the ending is a marriage proposal to the boy she stood beside. |
| Pragmatic | Answers are tested in practice: Nanoka's clever arguments fail with Kiryu, while Skank-san's advice to help him the way he needs works (Japanese Wikipedia). Each friend's definition of happiness is tried in Nanoka's own life. |
| Religious | No religion appears. The supernatural device (other selves, a guiding black cat, the dream) is left as a gentle mystery. Faith readers can find repentance and apology, honoring parents, defending the falsely accused and compassion for the despairing; secular readers find meaning built in this life. |
| Socio-cultural | Classroom bullying and group shunning in Japan; a family accused of theft without evidence; parents too busy at work for school events. Silverman (ANN) reads it through The Little Prince, with Nanoka as the prince who brings hope to the adults' deserts. |

Outlook: Hopeful — Nanoka reconciles with her parents (who thereby escape a plane crash), stands by Kiryu, and grows up happy; the futures of self-harm, loneliness and sex work are paths she escapes.
Hope source: R — the hope is second chances: Silverman calls the book "a tribute to second chances" (ANN), as Nanoka apologizes, reconciles and changes course; family and friends (F) are the close second.

## Creator and publisher context

- Yoru Sumino, a novelist living in Osaka Prefecture, began writing in high school and first posted I Want to Eat Your Pancreas on the site Shōsetsuka ni Narō in 2014, debuting with it at Futabasha in 2015 (second place, 2016 Japan Booksellers' Award). I Had That Same Dream Again (2016) was their second novel (Wikipedia, Yoru Sumino).
- Sumino named the book after a line in 10-FEET's song "Shinkirō," heard at the band's Kyoto Daisakusen festival (Japanese Wikipedia).
- Idumi Kirihara drew the manga; Silverman says the art works so smoothly with the text that it hardly feels like an adaptation (ANN). No interview with either creator about the book's themes was found. **Status: Needs more info on the creators.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is happiness? A teacher's assignment turns into a child's search, and each of her three friends answers differently: Minami says it is being accepted, Skank-san says it is caring seriously for someone, and Granny leaves the last hint (Japanese Wikipedia).

**The answer the story gives.** Happiness is relational, and pride blocks it. Nanoka's cleverness and sense of being special isolate her. When she makes up with her parents, apologizes to Kiryu and helps him the way he needs, she becomes the version of herself who grows up happy.

**Characters as arguments.** Minami argues from regret: she never made up with her parents before they died. Skank-san argues from despair: the friendless, proud girl she was became a woman who had given up. Granny argues from a long life. Ogiwara, the book-loving classmate who spread the rumor, shows how a crowd turns. Kiryu shows quiet endurance.

**Symbols and motifs.** The recurring dream. The black cat who guides Nanoka and never enters her real home. Minami's stories. Kiryu's painting signature, "live me," a reversal of a pun on his name that sounds like "kill you" (Japanese Wikipedia). Nanoka's habit of saying what "life is like."

**Traditions it echoes.** The Little Prince (ANN): seeing rightly with the heart; A Christmas Carol's visits from other times (from general knowledge; to verify); the moral lesson of how small choices shape a life. Many faiths' teaching that pride isolates and humility heals finds a secular echo here.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existentialism and classical virtue ethics agree: you write your life, and humility writes it better. Expressive individualism, as the sense of being special, is critiqued. Secular humanism frames the answer, and the supernatural device is left as gentle mystery.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 introduces the three friends and the assignment; vol. 2 brings the fight with her parents and the bullying of Kiryu; vol. 3 resolves both and reveals who the friends were (likely; chapter split to verify).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Nanoka thinks she is smarter than her classmates. How does that make her lonely?
2. Minami says happiness is being accepted; Skank-san says it is caring seriously for someone. Which do you think is right?
3. Why did the class turn on Kiryu, and why did they turn on Nanoka when she defended him?
4. Nanoka's first way of helping Kiryu failed. What did she change?
5. Minami hurts herself. If a friend did that, who could you tell?
