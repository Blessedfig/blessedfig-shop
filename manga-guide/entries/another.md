**Premise.** In 1972 a popular student named Misaki in class 3-3 of Yomiyama North Middle School died suddenly; grieving classmates and their teacher kept acting as if he were alive, and he appeared in the graduation photo (Wikipedia). Since then, in some years one extra person, someone already dead, joins class 3-3 unnoticed, records and memories quietly change to fit, and students and their relatives start dying in accidents and illnesses: the "calamity." In spring 1998, 15-year-old Kōichi Sakakibara transfers in after a collapsed lung, living with his grandparents and his aunt Reiko while his father works in India (ch. 1). He is drawn to Mei Misaki, a quiet girl with an eyepatch whom everyone ignores. She is the class's countermeasure: one student treated as "non-existent" to balance the extra one (ch. 1-8). Kōichi's contact with her seems to break the charm. Deaths begin: a classmate impaled by her umbrella on the stairs (ch. 4), a nurse in a falling elevator (ch. 6-7), a boy's heart attack (ch. 7), the homeroom teacher killing his mother and then himself in front of the class (ch. 11-12), and the suicide of the class leader's brother (manga only; fan wiki). Kōichi joins Mei as "non-existent" (ch. 8). The old librarian Chibiki shares his records (ch. 10), and a cassette left by a former student reveals that the calamity stopped once when the extra one was killed (ch. 14-15). On the class summer trip, fear turns the class on Mei; a lightning fire at the inn kills four students and the class leader Akazawa stabs Mei's eye (manga version; fan wiki). Mei's doll eye sees "the color of death" (ch. 17), and she identifies the extra one: Kōichi's aunt Reiko, the assistant homeroom teacher, who had drowned the year before. Kōichi kills her with a pickaxe, and the memories of her fade (ch. 19-20; fan wiki).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What happens when a community will not accept a death? In 1972 a class pretended a dead friend was still with them. The story imagines that refusal hardening into a curse that returns the dead, unknowing, to the class, and drags the living toward death.

**The answer the story gives.** Denial and exclusion do not protect anyone. The class's countermeasure, pretending one girl does not exist, fails; its silence keeps newcomers like Kōichi in the dark (Akazawa admits this, fan wiki). What ends the calamity is knowledge, gathered by those who break the silence, and the painful act of returning the dead to death.

**Characters as arguments.** Kōichi argues for compassion and curiosity. Mei argues for seeing clearly and enduring. Akazawa argues for rules and blame, and is humanized by her own losses. Chibiki, the teacher who fled but kept the record, argues that bearing witness matters. Reiko, loving and unknowingly dead, is the story's hardest argument: the thing to be let go is someone you love.

**Symbols and motifs.** Dolls (Mei's mother's empty-eyed dolls, Mei's doll eye) as the living dead. The graduation photo with a dead boy in it. The eyepatch. Rain. The class roster that silently rewrites itself.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Japanese school ghost story, the honkaku puzzle mystery (Ayatsuji's own genre), and the Ring and Final Destination films (Clements and McCarthy, via Wikipedia). Religious readers may hear older teachings about accepting death and not clinging to the departed.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Existential and pragmatic lenses win: facing death and testing what works. Communitarian conformity and its utilitarian logic of sacrificing one for many are put on trial; the mob's version is condemned, but the final sacrifice is accepted.

**Shifts across the series.** Volume 1 (ch. 1-4): mystery and unease, the first death. Volume 2 (ch. 5-9): deaths mount; Kōichi joins Mei as "non-existent." Volume 3 (ch. 10-14): the history of the curse, the teacher's suicide, the tape. Volume 4 (ch. 15-20): the class trip, fire, the mob and the revelation of the extra one.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The class agreed to treat Mei as if she did not exist. Why did they think it would help? Was it fair to her?
2. Why did the class's silence make things worse for Kōichi?
3. The curse began when classmates would not accept a death. What helps people accept a loss?
4. Akazawa blamed Mei and Kōichi. Where did her blame come from?
5. Kōichi had to let go of someone he loved. How did the story make that bearable, or did it?
