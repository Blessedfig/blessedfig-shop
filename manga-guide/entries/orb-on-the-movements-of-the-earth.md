**Premise.** In 15th-century "Kingdom of P" (later revealed as Poland), the Church controls learning and heresy means death. Rafal, a 12-year-old prodigy headed for theology, is forced by the imprisoned scholar Hubert to help with forbidden research and comes to see that the Earth moves around the Sun. Hubert takes the blame and is burned; Rafal's foster father reports him to the inquisitor Nowak; Rafal refuses to recant and takes poison before his execution (Part 1, ch. 1-4). Ten years later the despairing sellsword Oczy and the hopeful Gras find the hidden research; with the half-blinded monk Badeni and the gifted girl Jolenta, kept from scholarship because she is a woman, they complete the theory before Badeni and Oczy are executed (Part 2). Twenty-five years on, Jolenta, now leading a Heretic Liberation Front, and the money-minded Romani woman Draka try to print the theory; nearly all die, Nowak included (Part 3). A final part (1468) follows the historical astronomer Albert Brudzewski, who goes on to teach and influence the young Copernicus (Wikipedia; Seven Seas blurb).

**Doubt, curiosity and the search for truth are worth more than safety, and what one person discovers can be carried forward by others long after they die.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What is worth dying for? Rafal, a 12-year-old who has always chosen the rational, safe path, discovers that the Earth moves and finds he would rather die than unsee it. Each later protagonist faces the same question with less to lose and less to gain.

**The answer the story gives.** The truth about the world, and the freedom to keep looking for it. Most seekers die; the theory survives, passed hand to hand from Hubert to Rafal, through a sellsword, a monk, a girl scholar and a Romani trader, until a baker's assistant becomes the teacher of Copernicus (Wikipedia). Uoto adds a twist: what drives discovery is doubt, even misunderstanding (MANTANWEB 2024, 2025), and in the final part a grown-up Rafal's fanatical certainty is itself shown as dangerous.

**Characters as arguments.** Rafal argues that beauty and reason are worth more than life. Nowak, the inquisitor, argues that peace and order justify any cruelty, and at the end sees himself as the villain of the story. Oczy, who wanted to die and reach heaven, learns to look up at the sky without fear. Badeni argues that knowledge belongs to an elite; Jolenta, barred because she is a woman, proves him wrong. Draka begins by wanting only money. Albert's father urges doubt, the adult Rafal urges belief; the story finds both extremes wanting (Lost in Anime).

**Symbols and motifs.** The pendant, passed from seeker to seeker. The stars as God's watching eyes or as nature (Comics Beat). Mars turning back in its course. Fire: the stake, the burned book, the burning church. The title's three meanings: earth, knowledge and blood.

**Traditions it echoes.** The Galileo affair and the popular "science versus religion" story, which Anime Corner and Uoto both call historically exaggerated. Christian martyr stories, inverted: here the martyrs die for astronomy. Nietzsche and existentialism in the call to choose one's own meaning. For Catholic readers, the Church's own teaching that faith and reason do not conflict; for secular readers, a defense of free inquiry.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Authoritarian control of knowledge is the clear target. Christianity is explored from many sides: persecutors, devout seekers, a reforming bishop and a priest who sets Albert on his way. Liberalism (freedom of conscience and of information) and secular humanism are affirmed. Nihilism is answered by wonder.

**Shifts across the series.** Part 1 (ch. 1-4, v1): Rafal. Part 2 (from ch. 4, v1-5, likely): Oczy, Gras, Badeni, Jolenta. Part 3 (likely v6-8): Jolenta, Draka and the press. Final part (v8): Albert in 1468, set in a history that may differ from the earlier parts.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Rafal chose death over recanting. Was he brave, wrong, or both?
2. Nowak believed he was protecting people. How can someone do great harm while thinking he is good?
3. Most of the seekers believed in God. Does the story set faith against science, or something else against both?
4. Historians say the persecution is exaggerated. Does that change how you read the story?
5. Uoto wanted to show the importance of doubt. What is the difference between doubt and cynicism?
