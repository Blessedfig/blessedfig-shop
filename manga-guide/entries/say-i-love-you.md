**Premise.** Mei Tachibana, 16, has refused friendship since grade school, when classmates blamed her for the death of the class rabbit and a teacher punished her for it (ch. 1; fan wiki). When she kicks Yamato Kurosawa, the most popular boy in school, by mistake, he takes an interest in her. She calls him for help when a stalker follows her, and he scares the man off by kissing her; he then treats her as his girlfriend (ch. 1-3). Through Yamato, Mei makes friends: Asami, teased for her large chest; Aiko, who once slept with Yamato for comfort and crash-dieted for him; the bullied Kai; and the model Megumi, who schemes to take Yamato before finding real friends. Side stories follow Kakeru's empty casual sex, Daichi's grief for a dead girlfriend and Nagi's false friends (Wikipedia; AniList). After high school, Mei trains in early childhood education and Yamato becomes a photographer; the last volume jumps years ahead, and the two marry and have a son (fan wiki; MyAnimeList review).

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Is it safer to be alone? Mei decided as a child that friends only betray you, after classmates let her take the blame for the class rabbit (ch. 1). The series asks whether that safety is worth what it costs.

**The answer the story gives.** No. Every character who opens up, despite the risk, grows: Mei, Aiko, Megumi, Kai, even Nagi. Trust is learned in small steps and sometimes hurts, but isolation is the bigger wound. A MyAnimeList reviewer calls the romance a veil over a larger story about people overcoming their own limits.

**Characters as arguments.** Mei argues for honesty over popularity. Yamato argues for kindness to everyone, and learns that kindness without boundaries (his comfort sex with Aiko) can hurt. Aiko argues that love must be earned by changing your body, and learns it need not be. Megumi argues that friendship can be bought, and learns otherwise. Kakeru argues that sex brings status, and is left empty. Kai shows that a bullied boy can become a protector.

**Symbols and motifs.** The kick that starts it all; the kiss that drives off a stalker; Megumi's cut hair; Daichi's original hairstyles; Yamato's camera, which shows people as they are; the theme park "Land" Kai loves.

**Traditions it echoes.** The shojo coming-of-age romance (Kimi ni Todoke is a common comparison, from general knowledge; to verify) and the story of the outcast welcomed in. Christian readers may hear 1 Corinthians 13 in Yamato's patient love and the parable of the lost sheep in Mei's welcome, though the series does not draw on faith.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Expressive individualism and communitarianism work together: be yourself, and be with others. Hedonism is critiqued through Kakeru. Feminist concerns about girls' bodies are explored sympathetically. Traditionalism returns at the end in marriage and a child.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-7 (ch. 1-28, the anime's span) are the first year: friends, rivals and Megumi's scheme. Middle volumes deepen the couple and the side romances, including the leads' sexual relationship (chapter to verify). Late volumes follow college, careers and distance, and the final volume jumps years ahead to marriage and a son.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Mei decided never to trust anyone after the rabbit incident. Was that fair to herself?
2. Yamato slept with Aiko to comfort her. Was that kind? What else could he have done?
3. Aiko and Megumi both tried to change how they looked to be loved. What did they learn?
4. What makes Mei's friendships different from Megumi's at first?
5. The story shows teen relationships becoming sexual. What does your family believe about that, and why?
