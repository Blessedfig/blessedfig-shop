**Premise.** Riku Aibetsu, a second-year high school student whose father is a police officer and mother a nurse, likes helping people and seems to sense danger. A classmate rumored to have psychic powers, Iori Katanagi, calls her to an empty classroom sealed with paper talismans and tells her a phantom (reikai, a hostile spirit) is clinging to her; on a train it tries to swallow her and Iori destroys it (ch. 1). Iori is a shaman (the Japanese word is closer to "spirit medium") in Nabari, a small team led by his older sister Yayoi; he controls Ongyoki, a giant shadow oni he summons by unlocking a door with a key, and wants only to be normal. Riku turns out to be a Beckoning Hand, a person who attracts phantoms, so Iori must guard her; she chooses to train and learns a barrier that separates phantoms from people (ch. 8) (Japanese Wikipedia; Wikipedia). Cases follow: a kidnapping phantom at school, a mirror phantom, a teammate who raises three tengu. In the disappearing-Beckoning-Hands arc (ch. 9-13) Iori meets Senjudoji again, a phantom in a little girl's form created by a forbidden rite and the killer of someone he was protecting; she escapes, losing an arm (MyAnimeList). Later arcs pit the team against a disgraced 82-year-old doctor who worships Senjudoji and tries to fuse a younger girl, Tsumuji, with a phantom to regain his youth (ch. 14-20), and against Senjudoji's five "hosts" in a house of dolls (ch. 21-27). Chapter 28 tells how Fuuka Yukizuki, a 20-year-old Beckoning Hand, died saving the 15-year-old Iori. The series ends at ch. 30 with Senjudoji still free (TV Tropes; Japanese Wikipedia).

## Life message

**Gifts that feel like curses can be turned to protecting others, and people who look out for each other can carry what no one should carry alone.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What do you owe other people when you have a gift you never wanted? Iori can see and command phantoms and wants only to be ordinary; Riku draws phantoms to herself and wants only to help. Each has a power that runs against what they want.

**The answer the story gives.** Use it for others, and do not carry it alone. Iori learned that from Fuuka, who absorbed his spiritual wounds for a year and died saving him (ch. 28). He shut down after her death and opens up again through Riku, who chooses to train rather than hide (ch. 8). The heroes' rule is protection; the villains' rule is consumption.

**Characters as arguments.** Iori argues that duty can coexist with grumbling; his anger at Senjudoji is real but he spares the puppeteer Reta. Riku argues that a weakness can become a way to help. Yayoi is authority with a smile, demanding but protective. Senjudoji, made by forcing a thousand spirits to kill one another, is the logic of power through destruction. The doctor who worships her shows where the desire for endless youth leads.

**Symbols and motifs.** Shadows: Iori's power is his shadow, and he can thin it to vanish, the boy who is only dimly seen. Keys and doors: spirits are released by naming and unlocking. Hands: the Beckoning Hand who calls danger, the thousand-handed demon (Senjudoji means roughly "thousand-hand child"; from general knowledge, to verify). Dolls: the puppeteer's house of dolls makes people into objects.

**Traditions it echoes.** Japanese yokai lore and onmyodo-style exorcist fiction; the Jump exorcist line (Bleach, Jujutsu Kaisen) that reviewers compare it to; the old idea of a sacred protector who takes on others' harm. For many religious readers it also raises the old question of whether one may fight evil spirits with spirits.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Pagan/occult practice is the world's furniture and the heroes' tool, presented as a dangerous trade rather than a creed. Virtue ethics shapes the heroes. Might-makes-right and the hunger for new bodies belong to the villains and are condemned.

**Shifts across the series.** Vol. 1 (ch. 1-7) is case-of-the-week setup. Vol. 2 (ch. 8-16) brings Riku's training, the missing Beckoning Hands and the first clash with Senjudoji. Vol. 3 (ch. 17-25) covers Tsumuji's rescue and the dollhouse. Vol. 4 (ch. 26-30) gives Fuuka's backstory and a rushed ending (TV Tropes: the main villain and most of her lieutenants never defeated).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Iori wants to be normal but keeps protecting people. Which matters more to him in the end?
2. Riku's gift puts people in danger. Why does she decide to train instead of hiding?
3. Fuuka died for Iori, and he hates letting anyone sacrifice for him. Why?
4. The heroes use spirits to fight spirits. What does your family believe about that?
5. The doctor wanted to be young again at any price. What did he become?
