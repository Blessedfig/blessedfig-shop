**Premise.** Madoka Kawahara, a second-year high school student, loves shōjo manga in which cute younger boys fall for older girls, and helps out at her family's small restaurant now that her brother has left for university. Her father hires a part-timer, first-year Shikura Momoki, who looks like a boy from her manga but is rude and distrustful (ch. 1). When a girl from his school comes to the restaurant and photographs him without asking, treating him like a pretty object, Madoka has her delete the photo and the girl's father takes her away; Madoka realizes his looks have made him a target, confronts the girls who come back, and sets out to show him that not every girl sees him that way (ch. 1-4; ANN). She confesses and runs off (ch. 5); after a summer festival he says yes and they start dating (ch. 9-12). Later volumes follow their first kiss as a couple and a rival, Koga, a third-year model who pursues Madoka and at one point drives off with her (vols. 4-6); both families' approval (vol. 5, vol. 8); Shikura's hometown and childhood friend (vol. 8); Valentine's Day (vol. 9); and the two living together after "an incident" (vol. 10) (Kodansha and German publisher blurbs).

**People deserve to be seen as persons, not admired or used as objects for their looks; love grows from respecting someone's boundaries, and honesty and effort keep it alive.**

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** What does it mean to really see someone? Shikura is admired by everyone and seen by no one: girls photograph him, fawn over him and treat him like a character from a manga. Madoka loves exactly that kind of character in her books. The story asks whether she can love the real boy rather than the type.

**The answer the story gives.** Yes, by respecting him first. Madoka's first act is not a confession but a defense: she has the photo deleted and confronts the girls who treat him as a thing (ch. 1-4). Trust comes before romance, and the romance that follows is built on honesty and effort from both sides (ANN).

**Characters as arguments.** Madoka argues for respect and straightforward honesty; she is the fan who learns to tell fiction from a person. Shikura argues, at first, that girls are all the same, and is proved wrong. The photographing classmates stand for admiration that is really use; one of them later apologizes for standing by (vol. 2), so the story leaves room for repair. Koga, the confident older rival, tests whether the couple's bond holds (vols. 4-6).

**Symbols and motifs.** The phone camera (ch. 1). The family diner as neutral ground. Shōjo manga panels in Madoka's head set against real life. The word "cute" itself, which Shikura first resents and later accepts from Madoka (vol. 7).

**Traditions it echoes.** The shōjo romantic comedy and its "younger boy" type, here turned inside out. Readers from many faiths will recognize the idea that people look on the outward appearance while the heart is what matters (1 Samuel 16:7), and Kant's principle that persons are never merely means.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Virtue ethics carries the relationship (respect, honesty, patience). Feminist critique of objectification is applied to a boy, and the story takes it seriously. Community and family are warm and supportive. Religion is absent.

**Shifts across the series.** Volumes 1-2 (ch. 1-8): harassment, trust and confession, the most message-heavy part (ANN). Volumes 3-7 (ch. 9-28): dating, first kiss, the Koga rivalry and misunderstandings. Volumes 8-10 (ch. 29-38 and on): families, Shikura's hometown, Valentine's Day and living together, with the relationship growing more physical.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why did being admired make Shikura unhappy?
2. What is the difference between liking someone's looks and liking them?
3. Madoka asks the girl to delete the photo. What else could she have done?
4. Would the story feel different if a boy were photographing a girl?
5. What boundaries do Madoka and Shikura keep, and which ones would you keep?
