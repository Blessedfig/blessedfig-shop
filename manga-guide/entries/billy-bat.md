**Premise.** In 1949 Los Angeles, Japanese American cartoonist Kevin Yamagata draws Billy Bat, a hit funny-animal detective comic. He learns he may have unknowingly copied the bat from a drawing he saw in occupied Japan, and travels there to ask the original artist's permission. He is drawn into murders, cover-ups and prophecy tied to the bat image. The bat turns out to be a real, speaking entity that only a few people can see. It has appeared at turning points across history: a cave painting 200,000 years ago, Judas and Jesus in the first century, Francis Xavier and the Iga ninja in the 16th century, Hitler and Einstein, the 1949 Shimoyama incident, the JFK assassination, the Moon landing and September 11. An ancient scroll said to let its holder change history drives a centuries-long conspiracy. After Kevin Yamagata is shot (ch. 93), a second Kevin, Kevin Goodman, whom Yamagata saved as a baby in Dallas in 1963, becomes the protagonist and the next author of Billy Bat. The story ends in 2032 and 2063, in a world in ruins (Japanese and English Wikipedia; TV Tropes).

## Life message

**A good life means taking responsibility for the stories and images you put into the world, because they shape what people do; resisting those who would use stories to rule; and choosing, in each generation, which side of the "bat" (the white or the black) to follow.** One reviewer calls the series "a message to humanity" (Sebsus, AniList); the bat's line that "humans are foolish" is the most quoted moment of the ending (notPara and ryfor, AniList).

**Where traditions disagree.** The dividing line is the Judas and Jesus arc (ch. 14-17, vol. 2) and the series' larger claim that a morally ambiguous entity stands behind sacred history. Christian frames that hold Christ as the unique revelation of God (Catholic, Orthodox, Reformed, Evangelical, Pentecostal, Jehovah's Witness) tend to grade C or D on that reframing, not on intensity; Orthodox and Reformed families also weigh rules on images, and Pentecostal and Witness families weigh the speaking spirit as spiritism. Muslim and Bahá'í families weigh the depiction of Jesus as a prophet or Manifestation itself. Jewish frames focus instead on the Hitler material and a character study that families may want to check for stereotype. Secular frames grade higher for the critique of racism and power but raise the use of conspiracy theories and real tragedies as plot. No review from any faith outlet was found, so none of these readings is attested in print.

## Winners, losers and sympathy

The people who protect others and take responsibility for their drawings come out morally ahead, though not safely; those who try to own the bat or the scroll to rule history lose, and humanity as a whole is shown failing.

## Creator and publisher context

- Naoki Urasawa and Takashi Nagasaki developed the story for four years; Billy Bat was announced in Morning in October 2008 as Urasawa's first major work at Kodansha rather than Shogakukan (English Wikipedia).
- Nagasaki, to Christoph Mark of the Daily Yomiuri: "since moving to Kodansha, I think we lost a bunch of readers with the first couple of stories" (quoted in English Wikipedia). The first two chapters, a Dick Tracy-style comic-within-the-comic, drew negative fan reaction online (same source).
- English Wikipedia's background section says the creators considered making Billy Bat like Jesus Christ, with an ancient mystery around it. No original-language interview on the religious content was opened, so the wording is not confirmed.
- On the long wait for an English edition, Urasawa told Andrew Osmond in June 2019: "major animation or film studios could take offence," and that they might "draw some non-existent similarities"; asked whether Billy looks like a Disney character, he said "I have no intentions whatsoever of that!" (All the Anime interview, opened). Claims that Disney or DC blocked the series are framing, not fact (Slashfilm, opened).
- Kana editor Kristiina Korpus says "There was never any question of censoring or needing to frame the story" for its political and historical material, and that the team kept the Japanese color pages (Anime Corner interview, opened).
- The series was left out of Kodansha's D Morning digital edition in 2013 (English Wikipedia); no reason was found.
- No on-record statement by either creator about religion, Judas, Jesus, 9/11 or the ending was found. For Urasawa's broader public background, see the 20th Century Boys entry.

## Philosophy essay

**The central question.** Who writes history? The series asks whether events are shaped by the images people draw and the stories they tell, and who should hold that power. Its starting question is small: did Kevin Yamagata steal his cartoon bat? Its last question is vast: is the bat a god, a devil, a curse, or "a reflection of humanity's own nature" (ryfor, AniList)?

**The answer the story gives.** The story does not settle what the bat is. It does make a moral claim: whoever draws the bat must answer for what the drawing does. Yamagata and Goodman try to draw it honestly and to warn others. Kurosu, Hitler and the fake Chuck Culkin try to own it and rule through it, and they fail. The bat itself is ambivalent: TV Tropes calls it the "Administrator of the Human Race," one that prevents early apocalypse while manipulating people. Its closing verdict, "Humans are foolish," is the line readers quote most. The final scene, in 2063, gives a quieter answer: two enemy soldiers save a child together, and the child vows to study and save the world (Japanese Wikipedia).

**Characters as arguments.** Yamagata is the argument that an artist owes the truth to his readers and his source; his guilt over Shizu and his alcoholism show the cost. Goodman is the heir who inherits both the gift and the burden of prophecy. Kurosu is the argument that control of history is the deepest temptation; in one strand he finally chooses not to wish. The fake Chuck Culkin and Timmy are the argument that stories become property and property becomes power. Oswald, whom one reviewer reads through Nietzsche's will to power (Sebsus), is recast as a man who dies to save Jacky. Judas is a seeker who asks what God looks like.

**Symbols and motifs.** The bat drawn black (filled) or white (unfilled), said to be one being seen two ways (TV Tropes; fan wiki). The scroll that can change history. Cave paintings in prehistory and in the Basque country. The Moon, formed in the story from a piece of the bat. The comic-within-the-comic, first in a Dick Tracy style. Two towers.

**Traditions it echoes.** American noir and hard-boiled detective comics (ANN guest review). Conspiracy fiction about JFK and the Moon landing (Sammut, CBR). Metafiction about authorship. Gnostic-style stories of a hidden power behind religion and history (my own framing). Japanese ninja history and the Tensho Iga War.

**How each lens is portrayed.** Power-seeking is condemned. Commerce in stories is criticized. Religion is drawn into the mystery rather than mocked or affirmed in sources so far: Jesus heals and draws the bat; Francis Xavier carries its message; a cult forms around Yamagata. Christian readers may see this as a rival account of revelation; secular readers may see it as a myth about myths. Racism and Nazism are clearly condemned. Conspiracy theories are used as material; critics note the blend, not an endorsement.

**Shifts across the series.** Vols. 1-11 follow Yamagata from 1949 Tokyo through Dallas 1963 to Wakayama 1964, with flashbacks to Judea (vol. 2), Iga and Berlin. Vols. 12-17 follow Goodman from the 1980s to 9/11. Vols. 18-20 bring the two Kevins together, then jump to 2032 and 2063 (Japanese Wikipedia). Reviewers find the first half strongest and the second "somewhat unfocused" (Sammut, CBR) or rushed (Kreepy_Karl, MyAnimeList).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Kevin may have copied his bat without knowing it. What do we owe the people whose ideas shape ours?
2. The story says images and stories can change what people do. Can you think of real examples, good and bad?
3. In the story, Jesus draws the bat when Judas asks what God looks like. How does your family's tradition answer Judas's question?
4. Is the bat good, evil or neither? What does it mean that the same drawing looks white to some and black to others?
5. The story ends with a child choosing to study to save the world. Is that enough hope after everything else that happens?
