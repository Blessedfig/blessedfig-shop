# Shinto

## Summary

*Draft from general knowledge; verify against the listed sources.*

**Core beliefs:** The world is filled with kami: divine presences in nature, ancestors and places. The Kojiki and Nihon Shoki tell how the kami Izanagi and Izanami formed the islands of Japan. Shinto has no single founder, creed or scripture; it centers on ritual and on living in harmony with the kami. Death is a source of impurity; ancestors are honored as spirits, with afterlife beliefs often drawn from Buddhism. Authority rests with shrine tradition and priests, and for most shrines the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honchō).

**What it values:** Purity (harae, purification), sincerity (makoto), gratitude, harmony with nature and community, respect for ancestors, festivals (matsuri) and seasonal rites.

**What it forbids or discourages:** Pollution (kegare) through death, blood or wrongdoing; disrespect at shrines or to the kami; there is no list of commandments.

**Family, sex and identity:** Family and household continuity and ancestor veneration are central; weddings are often held in Shinto style. Identity is marked by shrine visits (Shichi-Go-San, New Year hatsumōde), home altars (kamidana), amulets and festivals; many Japanese practice it alongside Buddhism.

**Stories, fiction and art:** Kami, spirits (yōkai), magic and myth appear naturally in Japanese stories and anime; respectful portrayal of shrines and kami matters more than avoiding the supernatural. See [Teaching on art, media and depiction](#teaching-on-art-media-and-depiction).

**Range within the tradition:** Shrine Shinto, folk Shinto, Imperial household rites and sect Shinto (such as Tenrikyō historically); most practitioners do not claim an exclusive religious identity.

**Own sources:** Kojiki (712); Nihon Shoki (720); Engishiki norito (ritual prayers); Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honchō); Ise Grand Shrine.

## Teaching on art, media and depiction

No doctrine on fiction. Kami and yokai are common in Japanese popular culture and generally accepted. Concerns center on disrespect toward shrines and kami; sincerity, purity and gratitude are the values applied.
